The Anchor · The Week in Markets — Four Losing Weeks, a 19-Year Rate High, and a Flat Friday That Said Thursday Was Right

The week that ended Friday, September 25 was defined by its culminating event: Thursday’s global bond rout, which pushed the 10-year Treasury yield to 5.11% — a 19-year high and a 19.7-basis-point single-session spike — and the flat Friday that followed said the rout priced the shock accurately rather than overshooting. Four consecutive weekly Dow losses told the same story in calendar form. The four-week losing streak began at the September FOMC meeting, which signaled a “higher for longer” rate path that markets had not fully priced. Each subsequent week added a new negative catalyst: the Trump-Xi summit’s trade deadline extension created uncertainty about the tariff trajectory; the Iran oil supply disruption pushed Brent above $90 for the first time in the current conflict; and Thursday’s Williams speech triggered the bond rout that pushed the 10-year to 5.11%. Different catalysts, same direction, four consecutive weeks. The flat Friday close is the week’s most informative data point because of what it didn’t do: it didn’t continue lower, which would have implied Thursday underpriced the shock; and it didn’t recover, which would have implied Thursday overpriced it. The market’s verdict was that 5.10%–5.11% on the 10-year is the rate the Fed’s signaling warrants, and that equities at S&P 7,704 are the prices that 5.10% warrants, and that the two are consistent with each other at current valuations. Brent at $102.50 for a third consecutive day is the oil market’s parallel verdict on the Iran conflict: three days above $100 with no diplomatic breakthrough establishes $100 as the floor rather than a temporary spike, with the Houthi Bab el-Mandeb territorial capture from Friday adding a new dimension to the supply disruption risk that keeps the risk premium at the $100 level. The week ahead brings the August PCE inflation data — the Fed’s preferred inflation measure — and multiple Fed speakers, which will either validate the current 75.3% October FOMC rate hike probability or move it in either direction depending on whether the data and communication confirm or soften the “higher for longer” signal from September’s FOMC.

US equity markets are closed Saturday; the most recent close is Friday, September 25 — S&P 500 at 7,704 down 0.02%, Nasdaq at 26,939 up 0.01%, Dow at 51,350 down 0.31% for its fourth straight losing week, and the Russell 2000 at 2,854 down 0.28% — a near-flat session that functioned as the market’s verdict that Thursday’s bond rout priced the Williams rate signal accurately. No major catalyst arrived Friday in either direction to extend or reverse Thursday’s repricing.

The flat Friday close contains more information than its near-zero numbers imply: Thursday took the 10-year to 5.11%, and the market’s first-session response was to neither continue selling nor recover, which is the most informative possible signal about whether the repricing was appropriate. Markets that overshoot snap back; markets that undershoot continue lower. Friday’s flat says neither happened. The implication for Monday is that the 10-year at 5.10% and equities at Friday’s levels are the new neutral, not a temporary shock position waiting for correction.

The Dow posted its fourth consecutive weekly loss on Friday, closing at 51,350 and extending a streak that began with September’s FOMC meeting and ran through the Trump-Xi summit, the Iran oil supply disruption, and Thursday’s bond rout — four weeks with four different negative catalysts but the same directional outcome, reflecting a rate environment that applies consistent downward pressure on equities regardless of the specific news driver. Meta’s 4.5% single-session gain on Friday was the week’s largest among S&P 500 major components, driven by a company-specific catalyst against the macro headwind.

Four losing weeks at the Dow level does not imply a crisis — the Dow remains above 51,000, and each individual weekly loss has been modest — but the streak’s consistency is the point: four separate events over four weeks, each one adding a new justification for the same directional outcome, suggests the underlying rate environment is the structural headwind and the news is just the mechanism through which it surfaces each week. Until the rate path changes or equities reprice dramatically lower to compensate, the Dow’s weekly performance reflects the arithmetic of owning equities at a 5.10% risk-free rate.

Brent’s three-day hold above $100 is the oil market’s clearest structural signal of the week: the $100 crossing on Thursday was not a one-day momentum event but the establishment of a new pricing floor that the market has now confirmed with three consecutive closes above it. The week’s most consequential economic release is PCE: if it comes in above the 2% target on the core measure — which it has for the past two years — it validates the Fed’s September signal, keeps the October hike probability elevated, and tests whether equities can hold Friday’s levels under a confirmed inflation persistence narrative. If PCE comes in softer than expected, it opens the door to re-pricing October FOMC expectations lower, which would give equities relief from the rate headwind that has driven four consecutive Dow losing weeks.
