The Anchor · The Weekend Before CPI — What the Hormuz Escalation Means for September 10's Data

The rate-hike probability that Waller dropped from 65-70% to approximately 50% on Friday is now being tested by the weekend's geopolitical development. Before Friday's close, the market's working scenario was: strong jobs + conditional rate-hold framing = 50/50 rate decision, with CPI as the arbiter. After Saturday's Hormuz escalation — US and Iran trading direct strikes, two tankers mined, and Bessent confirming only 10 million of the normal 15-17 million barrels transit the Strait per day — the energy component of August CPI has a clearer upside mechanism. Brent at $95.23 and US diesel at $5.85 per gallon through August are the inputs. The question is whether they have flowed through to the energy component of August CPI at a level that crosses Waller's threshold for a rate hike. The market cannot answer that question until September 10 — but the Hormuz escalation raises the probability that the answer is yes. The mechanics of the Hormuz-to-CPI transmission are straightforward. Iran's blockade of the Strait since the June MOU has reduced normal oil flows by roughly 40% according to Bessent's confirmation — 10 million barrels per day instead of 15-17 million. That supply shortfall has kept Brent crude near $95 for weeks, feeding into the refinery input cost for middle distillates including diesel. US retail diesel has hit $5.85 per gallon — a record — as the cumulative effect of 67 days of elevated crude costs moves through the refinery chain and into pump prices. The Bureau of Labor Statistics' August CPI energy component directly captures retail gasoline and diesel prices from August data. If Brent averaged near $95 and retail diesel reached $5.85 during August, the energy component of August CPI is elevated. Whether that elevation is enough to cross Waller's informal threshold — the "hot CPI" that would trigger a rate hike in his framing — depends on the magnitude. Brent at $95 for a full month is a larger energy shock than, say, a brief spike. The sustained nature of the Hormuz disruption through August gives it more CPI impact than a transient price move would. Friday's markets, setting the rate-hike probability at 50%, were pricing a scenario where the energy component is elevated but not so elevated that a rate hold is foreclosed. The Hormuz escalation — specifically the fresh US-Iran strikes and the mine attacks on two tankers — does not change August's CPI data, which is already collected. But it signals that the energy supply disruption is intensifying rather than resolving, which affects both forward oil prices and the market's assessment of whether Waller's CPI threshold will be crossed. Gold's near-flat position at $4,524.70 — caught between inflation persistence (positive) and rate hike risk (negative) — captures the Saturday ambiguity precisely. The market has the two scenarios but not the data to resolve between them. Monday brings the Hormuz recalibration. September 10 brings the data.
