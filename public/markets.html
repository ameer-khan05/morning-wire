August PCE inflation came in at 3.4% — well below economists’ 3.7% forecast, with a 0.3% monthly increase — on September’s final trading day, sending 10-year Treasury yields retreating approximately 18 basis points from their 5.24% 19-year high to 5.06% and producing a broad rally in which the S&P 500 gained 0.29% to 7,671, the Nasdaq gained 0.34% to 26,860, the Dow gained 0.13% to 51,036, and the Russell 2000 fell 0.35% to 2,808 as smaller companies lagged on month-end rebalancing; Brent crude fell further toward $100 as both the Saudi pipeline restoration and softer inflation expectations converged, while gold climbed to $4,218 as the dollar softened on the yield retreat — closing a September defined by its first two weeks of Iran-war oil surges, its third week of yield breakouts to multi-year highs, and its final day of inflationary relief.

Wednesday’s markets beat is September’s final verdict: the month that began with Brent surging through $107 and the 10-year climbing to 5.24% ends with PCE proving the inflation trajectory is improving — too late to rescue September’s monthly returns, but early enough to set Q4’s starting conditions on a more favorable footing than the prior week suggested.

The Anchor · PCE 3.4% — The Month-End Data That Breaks the September Yield-Oil Divergence

Wednesday’s PCE print at 3.4% versus 3.7% expected is the data event that resolves the week’s defining tension: yields and oil had moved in opposite directions since Tuesday, and PCE determines which one was right. The answer is that both were partly right. Oil’s retreat from $107 to $100 was right about the supply picture — Saudi Arabia’s pipeline restoration genuinely removed one of the two disruption layers that had pushed Brent above $100. But the 10-year’s rise from 5.20% to 5.24% was also right: before Wednesday’s PCE data, there was no evidence that the inflation trajectory was improving enough to justify a yield retreat. PCE at 3.4% provides that evidence. The 3.4% number sits 30 basis points below the consensus estimate, which is a significant miss in a data series that markets have been watching at basis-point precision. More importantly, the monthly increase of 0.3% — while still above the Fed’s 2% annualized target — is consistent with a deceleration trajectory rather than a re-acceleration, and it reduces the probability of the Fed’s November meeting producing a hike to near zero. When the probability of a hike falls to near zero, “higher for longer” becomes “steady with a cut in view,” and the entire duration trade shifts. That shift is what sent the 10-year from 5.24% to 5.06% in a single session. The private payrolls data that accompanied PCE was also positive: 90,000 new jobs in September versus 68,000 expected, up from a downwardly revised 36,000 in August. That combination — softer inflation, stronger employment — is the Goldilocks data setup that justifies equity multiple expansion: growth without inflation acceleration. September’s Q3 close scorecard tells the month’s story in three numbers: Nasdaq +1.6%, S&P roughly flat, Dow -3.5%. Technology’s resilience reflects its oil-price insensitivity and its yield-headwind tolerance at current earnings levels. The Dow’s decline reflects the Iran war’s specific impact on industrial, energy-consuming, and rate-sensitive sectors that are more exposed to a month of $100+ Brent and 5.24% yields than the technology-weighted indices.

August PCE inflation rose 0.3% monthly, putting the 12-month rate at 3.4% — below economists’ 3.7% consensus by the widest margin in three months — and private payrolls increased 90,000 in September versus 68,000 expected, producing a Goldilocks combination of decelerating inflation and accelerating employment that sent 10-year Treasury yields retreating from 5.24% to 5.06% and triggered broad equity gains on the year’s final trading day of September; the 3.4% PCE reading reduces the probability of a Fed rate hike at November’s meeting to near zero.

The Goldilocks framing — inflation falling faster than expected while employment grows faster than expected — is the market setup that financial conditions most favor: it removes the primary downside risk (inflation re-acceleration requiring a hike) while confirming the underlying growth story (employment) is intact. The combination also creates the conditions for the Fed to shift from a neutral hold posture to a cut-preparatory posture at November’s meeting, which is what the 18-basis-point yield retreat is pricing: not a November cut, but language that makes a December or January cut explicit.

The 10-year Treasury yield retreated approximately 18 basis points in a single session to 5.06% — its fastest single-day decline since the rate cycle began — as PCE’s softer-than-expected print gave the bond market permission to price in a rate ceiling rather than a rate peak to be exceeded; gold climbed to $4,218, up 1.59% from Monday’s post-geopolitical-shock low of $4,152, as the dollar softened on the yield retreat and the safe-haven asset regained its rate-adjusted appeal; the equity risk premium, while still inverted with equities yielding roughly 3.2% in earnings against a 5.06% risk-free rate, began compressing toward a less extreme inversion.

The 18-basis-point retreat in a single session is notable for its speed relative to the prior week’s incremental climb: it took the 10-year several sessions to move from 5.10% to 5.24%, and it returned most of that ground in one PCE morning. The speed asymmetry — slow to rise, faster to fall on good data — reflects the bond market’s asymmetric conviction: it was less certain about the direction of the next Fed move than it was about the size of the repricing once a decisive data point arrived. Gold’s recovery to $4,218 resets the metal’s Q4 starting level: it enters October with the yield tailwind that was absent throughout September.

Brent crude fell further toward $100 Wednesday — declining 3.75% to $100.10 from Tuesday’s $104 — as Saudi Arabia’s pipeline restoration continued to remove the first supply disruption premium and Wednesday’s softer PCE reduced inflation expectations further, compressing the inflation-premium component of the oil price; WTI crude fell 4.65% to $96.50 as markets priced a supply normalization trajectory — though Brent’s persistence above $100 reflects the Hormuz stalemate’s continued presence as the second-layer supply risk that the Saudi pipeline repair cannot resolve.

Brent at $100.10 is the oil market’s two-day story compressed into one number: Tuesday’s $104 was already the first retreat from Monday’s $107, and Wednesday’s $100.10 is the second. The two-session, $6.90 decline from Monday’s peak represents the removal of the Saudi pipeline disruption premium in full — Brent is now priced at the Iran-war geopolitical baseline without the pipeline disruption layer. Whether it can break below $100 depends on whether Hormuz negotiations produce progress or whether the airline shutdown that has kept a premium in the oil market since early September continues into October.

September’s final market scorecard shows the Iran war’s differentiated impact by sector: the Nasdaq gained 1.6% for the month as technology absorbed oil and yield headwinds better than its earnings multiple implied, the S&P 500 ended roughly flat, and the Dow fell approximately 3.5% as the industrial, energy-consuming, and rate-sensitive companies that populate the index bore proportionally more of the month’s cost — $100+ Brent for most of the month, the 10-year reaching 5.24%, and consumer confidence collapsing; Wednesday’s PCE print sets Q4 starting conditions with yields retreating, oil below $100, and inflation trending favorably.

The Q3 scorecard is also the Q4 starting line. The September divergence between Nasdaq and Dow is the clearest evidence of how the Iran war’s specific economic mechanisms — elevated oil, elevated yields, elevated geopolitical uncertainty — affect sectors differently: technology’s relative immunity to oil input costs and its yield-adjusted earnings premium at current multiples kept the Nasdaq positive in a month that was deeply negative for yield-sensitive and energy-consuming businesses. Q4 begins with those same divergences present but moderating: PCE has given yields a reason to retreat, and Brent is approaching $100 from above rather than threatening $110 from below.
