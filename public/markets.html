Markets · Deep Dive · Nasdaq ▼2.2% · Dow ▲0.4% · Jobless Claims 189K · Gold $4,126 · Brent $96 · Thursday, November 5, 2026

Thursday’s markets split hard along a single fault line — AI regulation risk versus everything else — with the Nasdaq falling 2.2% to 25,169 and the Nasdaq 100 dropping as much as 5% as investors sold AI chip stocks on speculation that the incoming Democratic Congress will constrain the AI industry, while the Dow gained a modest 0.4% to 53,497 as strong economic data provided a macroeconomic counterweight; U.S. weekly jobless claims fell to 189,000 near a 57-year low, one day before October’s nonfarm payrolls report, providing the labor-market confirmation that the Fed’s December rate baseline remains unchanged even as chip stocks collapsed; Nvidia fell 7%, SK Hynix lost 9%, Samsung Electronics 6.7%, and Tesla and SpaceX each lost over 8%; gold extended its rebound to $4,126 (+1.9%) as Iran’s Strait of Hormuz incidents reintroduced geopolitical risk the market had priced out the day before; and the Hang Seng rose 3.7% — its best day since February 2025 — as Asian markets benefited from the view that U.S. AI regulation concerns create competitive opportunity for non-U.S. AI supply chains.

Thursday’s market story is the election result’s second-order effect: the Democratic sweep that reduced geopolitical risk on Wednesday is now introducing regulatory risk on Thursday, specifically for the AI chip sector that makes up the largest weighting in the Nasdaq. The Dow’s relative strength is the market telling you the concern is narrow and sector-specific, not a broad macroeconomic deterioration.

The Anchor · Nasdaq ▼2.2% — The Chip Selloff That the Labor Market Did Not Know About

The Nasdaq fell 2.2%. The Dow rose 0.4%. Jobless claims fell to 189,000, near a 57-year low. All three are simultaneously true — not contradictory, but three different signals about three different things. The Nasdaq’s decline is a regulatory risk premium applied to AI chip companies on speculation about a Democratic Congress; the Dow’s gain reflects the broader market processing strong economic data; jobless claims at a 57-year low say the labor market does not know there is a chip selloff. The chip selloff is a regulatory risk event, not a macroeconomic one — and the Hang Seng rising 3.7% is the global market reading U.S. AI regulatory headwinds as a competitive opportunity for non-U.S. semiconductor supply chains.

The Nasdaq fell 2.2% to 25,169 and the Nasdaq 100 dropped as much as 5% while the Dow gained 0.4% to 53,497 — the sharpest divergence between tech-heavy and broader indices in weeks — as AI chip stocks led one of the most targeted sector selloffs in the index’s recent history; the S&P 500 fell 1.9% to 7,543 and the Russell 2000 lost 1.6% to 2,816, reflecting the AI supply chain’s weight in the broader market even beyond the pure-tech indices; the selectivity of the selloff — with consumer, healthcare, and non-AI industrials largely holding while chip, cloud, and AI-adjacent names collapsed — confirms that Thursday’s session is a regulatory risk premium in a specific sector, not a broad macroeconomic deterioration.

The Dow-Nasdaq divergence is the market distinguishing sector risk from macro risk. The Dow is not full of AI chip companies; the Nasdaq is. The divergence is exactly the size of the regulatory risk premium the market is applying to the AI chip sector on the hypothesis that a Democratic Congress will act — which may or may not prove accurate in January 2027.

U.S. weekly initial jobless claims fell to 189,000 for the week ended November 1 — near a 57-year low and well below consensus estimates — released Thursday morning one day ahead of October’s nonfarm payrolls report and confirming that the labor market remains robust despite the broader equity session’s losses in chip stocks; the number provides the macroeconomic counterweight that prevented a chip-driven selloff from turning into a broad market panic, with the Dow’s 0.4% gain on a day the Nasdaq fell 2.2% reflecting that investors with broad market exposure processed the jobless claims as a positive while investors with Nasdaq-heavy exposure processed the AI regulation concerns as a negative; the Fed’s December rate-decision baseline remains unchanged, with a labor market producing 189,000 weekly claims providing no macroeconomic justification for a dovish pivot ahead of schedule.

The Fed watches both the chip selloff and the claims data — but only one of them drives the December rate decision. Jobless claims near a 57-year low is an economic event; the Nasdaq’s chip-stock selloff is a financial-market event. The Fed’s December baseline is driven by the economic event.

Gold extended its rebound to $4,126 (+1.9%) as Iran’s IRGC reported two oil tankers on fire in the Strait of Hormuz in separate incidents involving apparent mine strikes — reintroducing geopolitical risk that markets had partially priced out on Wednesday when peace deal odds were rising; Brent crude steadied near $96 after Wednesday’s oil rout, with the Hormuz incidents preventing a further decline even as the broader direction of Iran policy remained constructive relative to the peak-tension period; WTI fell 2.7% to $92.50 as the market balanced Iran risk reintroduction against the sustained directional move lower that Wednesday’s peace repricing had established; and the 10-year Treasury yield eased to 4.82%, below 4.9% for the first time in weeks, as chip-stock selling drove safe-haven flows into Treasuries alongside the gold move.

Gold at $4,126 is the asset saying Wednesday’s peace optimism was premature. Wednesday’s $4,047 was the high-water mark of post-election relief; Thursday’s $4,126 is the correction by two simultaneous events — chip regulation risk and Hormuz tanker incidents — that the peace trade had not priced.

The Hang Seng rose 3.7% — its best single-day performance since February 2025 — and is now up approximately 8% from its October 22 low, as Asian markets read Thursday’s U.S. AI chip selloff as a competitive opportunity for non-U.S. AI supply chains rather than a global technology risk event; Qualcomm and Arm’s underwhelming outlooks added to the chip sector’s Thursday pressure, reinforcing the narrative that U.S. AI hardware companies are facing both regulatory headwinds from a Democratic Congress and demand softness in some segments of the semiconductor market; while South Korea’s Kospi fell 4.8% and Kosdaq 4.3% on SK Hynix and Samsung losses tied to the same AI regulation concern, broader Asian markets without direct U.S. regulatory exposure outperformed sharply.

Hang Seng +3.7% on a day Nasdaq falls 2.2% is the global version of Thursday’s domestic split: U.S. AI regulatory risk is everyone else’s competitive opportunity. The 8% recovery from October 22 lows suggests Hong Kong had already priced its own headwinds and is repositioning on the view that U.S. chip regulation benefits Asian competitors.
