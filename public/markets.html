The Anchor · The Dip-Buy and the Oil Relief — Two Constructive Signals That Still Need Wednesday’s Earnings and October 28 to Hold

The Nasdaq at 26,389 on Monday after closing Friday at 26,140 is the dip-buy that last week’s 4.5% AI repricing created. The oil slide that accompanied it — Brent from $109.86 to $107.97 — is the macro relief that makes the dip-buy more defensible. Together they create the most constructive market environment in two weeks, with three caveats. The first caveat is Iran. Brent’s move from $109.86 to $107.97 is a 1.7% slide driven by a diplomatic proposal that has not been accepted and coexists with continued mine activity in the Strait. The oil market is pricing a probability of Hormuz relief, not Hormuz relief itself; if the proposal collapses or the mine incidents escalate, Brent reverses, the inflation signal the Fed is reading returns, and the AI dip-buy faces a second round of macro headwinds. The second caveat is earnings. Meta and Tesla report Wednesday, and Meta’s AI capital expenditure guidance is the single most consequential forward signal for AI equity valuations this quarter. If Meta signals continued aggressive AI infrastructure investment — maintaining or increasing its data center and GPU commitments — the AI repricing argument weakens, because the most sophisticated AI buyer in the world is doubling down. If Meta moderates, the repricing narrative extends and Thursday’s session opens under renewed pressure. The third caveat is the Fed. The October 28 announcement resolves the rate-uncertainty component of the current environment, and Waller’s non-escalation on Monday is the most recent signal that the pause expectation is intact. But it is a pre-meeting signal, not a decision. Bessent’s framing of September CPI as an energy-shock artifact is the Treasury’s diplomatic way of telling the Fed that the inflation it is reading is not structural — precisely the argument that justifies a pause rather than a hike. Whether the Fed agrees on October 28 is the week’s binary event.

The Nasdaq Composite gained 0.9% to 26,389 Monday and the S&P 500 rose to 7,727 as AI-linked technology stocks led the rebound from last week’s repricing, with the technology sector up 1.2% to lead all S&P 500 constituents as traders bought into data center operators, semiconductor names, and software companies that had declined most sharply during the week’s AI moat selloff; the Dow closed at roughly 52,050 and the Russell 2000 rose 1.0% to 2,748, with the small-cap index’s gains reflecting a broader risk-on tone that extended beyond the AI-specific dip-buying to include general growth positioning.

The Monday rebound following last week’s 4.5% Nasdaq weekly loss fits the classic dip-buy pattern: the triggering catalyst — three simultaneous AI model releases that the market read as evidence of competitive commoditization — is a medium-term thesis that requires sustained evidence to maintain its price impact, and after five sessions of selling on that thesis, the market’s short-term mechanics are primed for a countertrend session as traders who sold into the selloff take profit. Whether the rebound is structural — a genuine floor from which AI equity valuations stabilize — depends on Wednesday’s Meta earnings, which will either reinforce the AI-capex-continues narrative or confirm the repricing thesis with a moderated investment outlook.

Brent crude slid 1.7% to $107.97 and WTI fell to $97.40 as Iran’s Foreign Minister Araghchi said Tehran’s seven-day Hormuz reopening proposal remains on the table — even as the IRGC reported two oil tankers caught fire in the Strait after hitting mines — with Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent framing September’s inflation surprise as a direct consequence of the energy shock rather than broad-based price pressure, a characterization that positions any Hormuz easing as an inflation relief event rather than merely an oil supply normalization.

Bessent’s energy-shock framing is the week’s most consequential economic statement because it sets up the inflation data interpretation the Fed will use on October 28: if the September CPI spike was an energy artifact rather than a structural inflation acceleration, the Fed’s pause justification is stronger, because it is reacting to a supply disruption that markets are already beginning to price as reversible rather than a wage-price spiral that requires sustained rate action. The practical significance for equity markets is that Brent sliding from $109.86 to $107.97 — even two days before the Fed meets — validates Bessent’s framing in real time: the inflation that drove September CPI is already beginning to deflate as the market prices Hormuz reopening probability.

Fed Governor Waller’s Monday remarks preserved the rate-pause expectation established by Fed Chair Warsh’s earlier comments by declining to escalate October rate-hike probabilities, with the 10-year Treasury edging modestly to 5.11% from Friday’s 5.097% — a negligible move that confirms the AI equity selloff remains a pure valuation correction rather than a combined rates-and-valuation event; gold slipped 0.4% to $4,170 as the risk-on tone from equity dip-buying and oil easing reduced safe-haven demand, consistent with a market unwinding a sector-specific fear rather than pricing systemic risk.

The gold move is the market’s internal consistency check: when last week’s selloff was at its most intense, gold’s safe-haven bid was modest — a signal that institutional investors were not pricing systemic risk, just AI equity repricing. Gold falling 0.4% on Monday as equities rise confirms that the market still reads last week’s selloff as sector-specific rather than systemic, and that the rebound has internal logic. The 10-year at 5.11% is the single number that determines whether this week’s dip-buy can hold: if the 10-year moves materially above 5.2% before October 28, the rate-uncertainty component of the equity correction returns and the dip-buy reverses; if the 10-year holds near 5.1% and the Fed confirms the pause on October 28, the correction’s rate amplifier is permanently removed from the equation.
