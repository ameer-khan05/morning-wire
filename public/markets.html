Meta and Microsoft report Wednesday after close, with Amazon and Apple Thursday — the four data points that answer whether Alphabet's FCF deterioration is a sector pattern or a one-quarter outlier.

The FOMC meeting Wednesday arrives at a market defined by two simultaneous and conflicting forces: oil normalization (Brent ▼14.7% from Thursday peak, providing the most defensible inflation narrative of the conflict period) and AI capex anxiety (chip stocks ▼5-11% on concerns that hyperscaler spending is outrunning FCF). Powell's press conference language must navigate both: the oil-normalization story supports a hawkish hold with September genuinely data-dependent, while the chip selloff — if it represents a market pricing AI infrastructure investment slowdown — is a potential economic slowdown signal that the Fed would normally frame as reducing future rate pressure. The two forces are pulling in the same direction (toward hold, toward rate compression) by different mechanisms, which makes Wednesday's message coherent but also makes the language harder to frame without providing unintended signals about one vector or the other.

The FOMC meeting this week is the most consequential in several months, and not because the rate decision is uncertain — it isn't. The consequential element is Powell's framing on September: how much of an explicit lean toward hold, versus genuine data-dependence, does he signal? At Brent $85.87 and 4.49% on the 10-year, the data supports a clear hold framing. But the chip selloff introduces a wrinkle: if AI investment sentiment is deteriorating (which the chip complex is pricing), that is a capex-slowdown signal that a growth-conscious Fed might view as reducing the need for rate restraint more broadly. Powell does not want to conflate an AI-specific equity narrative with a macroeconomic signal. Navigating that distinction while giving clear September guidance is the technical challenge of Wednesday's press conference.

Gold's intraday trajectory Monday deserves specific attention as a signal for structural inflation-premium pricing. The metal fell through $4,000 to $3,999.80 in the morning session — the first breach of the $4,000 floor in seven weeks — but recovered to $4,055.70 by the close. The morning breach was geopolitical-premium deflation (oil falling sharply, Iran pause holding). The afternoon recovery to $4,055.70 was structural inflation-floor demand absorbing the geopolitical-premium deflation. The close above $4,055 (which puts gold above where it was on Friday's close of roughly $4,050) suggests the $4,000 floor held: geopolitical premium deflated, structural premium absorbed and then some. That reading — geopolitical premium fully out of gold, structural premium intact — is consistent with a medium-term gold price that revisits $4,000-$4,100 as a range rather than testing the $3,800-$3,900 territory that a structural-floor breakdown would imply.

The Q2 earnings context for Wednesday and Thursday is the strongest in years: the Q2 S&P 500 earnings beat rate is approximately 88% versus the 70% historical average, providing a fundamental backdrop that the Alphabet-driven narrative has overshadowed. 88% of S&P 500 companies beating estimates in a quarter where geopolitical headwinds were running for most of the period is an exceptionally strong result. When geopolitical premium exits the market and the fundamental picture becomes the primary lens, 88% EPS beat rates are the number that should govern the valuation narrative — and Wednesday's Meta and Microsoft disclosures will determine whether that fundamental strength extends to the AI-capex companies specifically.

The Federal Open Market Committee opened its July 28–29 meeting Tuesday with a hawkish hold broadly expected Wednesday — the relevant event is Chair Powell's press conference language on September, which addresses an oil price 14.7% below Thursday's conflict peak and a 10-year Treasury yield that has fallen 23 basis points in four sessions; at Brent $85.87 and UST 10Y at 4.49%, the "supply shock to assess rather than incorporate" framing is more defensible than at any point since the conflict began July 1, and September rate-hike odds have compressed substantially from Thursday's 80% peak as oil-normalization reduces the inflation-shock interpretation. The chip selloff adds a nuance: AI capex anxiety is a deflationary signal (investment slowdown) that Powell must distinguish from the oil-normalization story in Wednesday's framing.

At $85.87, Brent is now at or slightly below the pre-conflict $83-86 fundamental range — meaning the geopolitical premium that drove September hike odds to 80% last Thursday has fully deflated or slightly overcorrected. The oil-normalization story entering Wednesday's FOMC is therefore: supply shock arrived ($100.69 Brent Thursday), was assessed to be geopolitically sourced (not structural), and has partially reversed ($85.87, below the fundamental range). That sequence is the cleanest possible data pattern for a Fed that wants to hold September while keeping it data-dependent: the shock resolved itself in four sessions without requiring a rate response. The July CPI data (mid-August) remains the deciding input for September 16's meeting — and the question is whether oil at $85.87 entering FOMC makes Powell confident enough to say September hold is the base case, or whether he insists on pure data-dependence.

Monday's full session delivered a split tape that quantifies exactly how the market is pricing two simultaneous forces: the Dow gained 0.51% and Russell 2000 added 0.61% as oil-sensitive industrials, energy names, and rate-sensitive small-caps benefited from Brent's 11.3% full-session decline to $85.87 and the UST 10-year's additional 6 basis-point drop to 4.49%, while the Nasdaq lost 0.18% as the AI chip complex — Nvidia ▼5%, AMD ▼5.2%, Sandisk ▼11%, SK Hynix ▼7.5%, Micron ▼2.3% — dragged the technology-weighted index; gold recovered from its intraday breach of $4,000 ($3,999.80 morning low) to close at $4,055.70 (▲1.40%), and Apple overtook Nvidia as the largest US company by market cap as Nvidia's selloff reduced its capitalization.

The S&P 500's essentially flat close (▲0.02%) at 7,413.18 is the arithmetic outcome of two equally weighted forces: oil and rates pulling the index up (via energy, industrials, financials, rate-sensitive sectors) and chip stocks pulling it down (via technology concentration in the index). The Russell 2000's 0.61% outperformance versus the S&P 500's 0.02% quantifies small-cap's differential sensitivity to the rate-compression channel: small-cap companies carry proportionally more floating-rate debt than large-caps, making them more directly sensitive to the 23-basis-point 10Y decline over four sessions. Historically, small-cap outperformance in rate-compression environments of this magnitude has led broader market recovery by two to three weeks — but the AI-capex headwind is a countervailing force that the historical rate-compression pattern was not written against.

Quick hits

  • Gold's intraday pattern Monday — breach of $4,000 to $3,999.80 in the morning, recovery to $4,055.70 by session close — is the clearest illustration of the two components of the current gold price. The morning breach was geopolitical premium exiting: oil down 11.3%, Iran pause holding, the geopolitical-risk component of gold demand reduced. The afternoon recovery was structural inflation-floor demand absorbing the geopolitical exit and then some. The close at $4,055.70 (above Friday's close of approximately $4,050) confirms that Monday's oil decline was not a deflationary macro signal that structural gold buyers interpreted as reducing long-term inflation risk — it was a geopolitical-normalization signal that left the structural inflation thesis intact. Gold at $4,055 entering Wednesday's FOMC suggests bond markets and gold markets are aligned: inflation is not resolved, but the oil-spike component of it has largely deflated.
  • The Apple-Nvidia market cap reversal on Monday — Apple overtook Nvidia as the largest US company — is a consequence of the chip selloff but also a structural signal. Nvidia's position as the largest US company has been based on AI infrastructure demand driving a valuation multiple that assumes massive and sustained hyperscaler chip purchasing. Apple's position is based on a consumer device and services ecosystem with predictable FCF. The reversal in one session, driven by a single category of AI spending concerns (not a Nvidia-specific event), quantifies how much of Nvidia's market cap premium was priced on the assumption that the AI capex cycle would continue at its current rate indefinitely. Monday's 5% decline is the market repricing the probability that it will.
