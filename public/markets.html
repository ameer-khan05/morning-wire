Iran's ballistic missile attack on US troops, which US Central Command announced Tuesday, immediately reintroduced the inflationary oil variable Warsh needed to be absent: Brent surged 4.26% to $89.53, reversing the three-day normalization from Sunday's $96.78 conflict-peak to Monday's $85.87 trough in a single session, and UST 10-year yields rose 14 basis points to 4.63%. The S&P 500 closed Tuesday at 7,428.78 (▲0.21%), the Dow at 52,748 (▲1.03%), and the Nasdaq at 24,876.91 (▼0.22%) — with the Dow's gain reflecting strong earnings from non-tech names before the full impact of the Iran attack oil surge registered in futures.

Wednesday July 29 is one of the most information-dense single sessions in the 2026 calendar: FOMC rate decision and first Warsh press conference, Iran ballistic missile attack sending oil back above $89, CLARITY Act shelved triggering $670M in crypto liquidations, and Meta and Microsoft earnings after close. Four simultaneous market-moving events arriving in a 12-hour window, each capable of defining a trading session on its own, produce a volatility environment where the net direction is unclear but the range of outcomes is wide. Warsh's "September cuts on the table" and Iran's oil re-spike pull in opposite directions on inflation expectations; Meta and Microsoft pull in opposite directions on the AI capex-FCF question depending on whether they show Alphabet's pattern or a better outcome.

Warsh's first FOMC press conference as Fed Chair is important for what it didn't include as much as what it did. Gold's Tuesday decline to $4,020 (▼0.88% from Monday's $4,055.70) is a counterintuitive result given the day's Iran attack escalation, but reflects the day's sequencing: the FOMC hold and Warsh's "too high" inflation language arrived first, with gold falling on reduced uncertainty (hold confirmed, no hike surprise) and on the market interpreting Warsh's hawkish inflation characterization as reducing near-term rate-cut probability (which is bearish for non-yielding gold in nominal terms). The Iran attack oil surge then partially reversed the decline, but the close at $4,020 reflects gold's net response: FOMC hawkish-lean (bearish for gold) slightly outweighing geopolitical re-escalation (bullish for gold). The $4,000 structural floor — tested intraday Monday and held — was not approached Tuesday despite the FOMC hawkishness, suggesting structural gold demand at $4,000+ remains intact even in a higher-rate environment.

The S&P 500's 0.21% gain is the arithmetic split between Dow-style gains and Nasdaq-style losses, weighted by composition. Meta and Microsoft together report into this setup after close, with the Dow's FOMC-certainty gains potentially at risk if either company shows Alphabet-style capex expansion without FCF return.

Warsh eliminated Powell's forward-guidance convention at his first June FOMC, meaning markets must now price September probability from data, not from stated directional signals, making Wednesday's "on the table" statement less binding than similar language from Powell-era FOMC meetings.

The Iran attack's timing relative to the FOMC announcement is the day's central market event. Warsh delivered conditional dovishness (September cuts possible) premised on oil normalization continuing and inflation trending toward 2% from 4.2%. Iran's missile attack in the same session introduces oil back to $89.53 from the $85.87 level where that inflation narrative was most defensible. If oil holds above $88 into the August CPI release (mid-August), the 4.2% CPI reading becomes harder to interpret as trending to target — and Warsh's September-cut signal becomes more rhetorical than substantive. The Fed Chair who just defined his monetary philosophy as "inflation too high, no forward guidance" now faces a macro environment that has re-complicated his own September calculus within hours of his first press conference.

Quick hits

  • Kevin Warsh's elimination of forward guidance at his first June FOMC creates a structural market dynamic for the remainder of 2026. Under Powell, "data-dependent" forward guidance language anchored the rates market: when Powell said September cuts were possible, the market could price those cuts with a Federal Reserve communication commitment behind the signal. Under Warsh, "September cuts on the table" is a conditional possibility without a forward-guidance anchor — it's the Fed Chair's present assessment of what the data might support, not a commitment to communicate a direction. This distinction means the rates market must now price September probability more directly from incoming data (CPI, PCE, jobs) and less from Fed communication — a regime change that increases the sensitivity of rate expectations to each data print, starting with August CPI in mid-August.
  • Gold's counterintuitive decline on a day of FOMC and Iran attack is the market's clearest signal that the geopolitical-premium-to-gold relationship has compressed substantially from the conflict's early weeks. In early July, Iranian escalation reliably bid gold above $4,100 as safe-haven demand from the conflict premium added to gold's structural inflation floor. Tuesday's 0.88% decline despite Iran's ballistic missile attack suggests that the geopolitical bid for gold has partially normalized as the conflict has extended — markets have priced an ongoing conflict rather than a sudden-escalation premium. The $4,000 structural floor remains intact (not approached Tuesday), but the ceiling from geopolitical premium is lower than it was in early July, suggesting gold's near-term range is $4,000–$4,100 rather than the $4,050–$4,200 range that prevailed when the conflict-premium was higher.
