The Anchor · Walmart's EPS Miss Despite Revenue Beat Tells the Tariff Story — Margin Compression Is Happening, Consumer Volume Is Not Yet the Casualty

Walmart's second-quarter results carry two contradictory signals that together define the US consumer's position in the tariff and Hormuz-inflation environment of 2026. The revenue beat ($177.4 billion vs. $176.2 billion expected) and the comparable-store-sales outperformance (+4.6% vs. +4.0% expected) confirm consumer spending volume is holding — Americans are still shopping at Walmart at volumes above analyst expectations, even 51 days into a Hormuz closure and through sustained tariff cost increases. The EPS miss ($0.68 vs. $0.74 expected) — the first since May 2022 — tells the other half: Walmart is absorbing cost increases through margin compression rather than passing them fully to consumers, because its competitive position as the US's lowest-price mass retailer depends on maintaining price leadership even when input costs rise. The combination of revenue-beat and EPS-miss is the corporate finance version of "resilience under pressure" — Walmart is choosing to protect market share through competitive pricing while accepting the margin damage, rather than protecting margins by raising prices above the competition. The raised full-year guidance — offered despite the EPS miss — is the most important single data point for the macro debate. If Walmart believed the consumer was about to weaken materially in the second half of 2026, it would not raise guidance; the guidance revision implies management sees consumer spending sustaining through the rest of the year even in the current high-cost environment. That is the data point that argues against the most pessimistic stagflation scenarios that 50-plus days of Hormuz closure and near-decade-high Treasury yields would support. The EPS miss also has a Hormuz-specific component that separates it from standard tariff headwinds: energy and logistics costs, which are especially sensitive to oil prices, have risen in a Brent-$91 environment relative to what Walmart's cost model would have assumed at Brent-$82 (the pre-Hormuz-collapse level). The online double-digit growth continuation is the structural trend — e-commerce share of Walmart's revenue is rising, and online economics are different from in-store economics in ways that affect both revenue growth and margin structure. The Jackson Hole framing: Powell's August 28 remarks are now the week's primary pending macro catalyst, with two new data points the July FOMC minutes did not incorporate: the Iran-Oman in-principle Hormuz framework (Day 51 partial de-escalation = modest reduction in the Hormuz oil-inflation channel) and Walmart's raised full-year guidance (consumer resilience signal reduces the most pessimistic growth-slowdown scenario). Whether Powell uses Jackson Hole to soften the hawkish-at-the-margin minutes language — acknowledging the Iran-Oman progress and the consumer resilience data — or maintains the cautious July framework, will determine whether the September 16 cut is characterized as the beginning of an easing cycle or a one-and-done precautionary cut. That framing moves equity and bond markets more than the 25-basis-point cut itself.

The bond rally's catalyst — Treasury Department moves to ease long-end borrowing pressure — shifted the 10-year yield from 4.70% to 4.58% in a single session, the largest single-day yield decline since the FOMC minutes dropped the previous week. The 30-year yield's retreat from its near-two-decade high removes the most acute component of the equity market's valuation headwind, because long-duration equity multiples are most sensitive to long-end rate levels. If the 30-year yield's pullback is sustained through Jackson Hole, the equity risk premium calculation that was driving the three-day losing streak — sell equities, buy inflation protection — reverses.

Quick hits

  • Walmart's first EPS miss since May 2022 has a precedent worth examining. In May 2022, Walmart missed earnings for the first time in years as supply-chain disruption costs from the COVID aftermath — elevated shipping, labor, and inventory costs — compressed margins despite strong consumer spending. The recovery from that miss took two quarters, as Walmart worked through excess inventory and the supply-chain disruptions normalized. The 2026 version has a different cost driver (tariffs and Hormuz energy costs rather than post-COVID logistics) but a structurally similar pattern: volume intact, margins under pressure from supply-chain inflation. The question for Q3 is whether the Hormuz situation provides any relief — the Iran-Oman framework, if implemented, could moderate Brent's trajectory below $91, reducing Walmart's energy and logistics costs in the second half of the year.
  • The Treasury Department's "moves to ease long-end borrowing pressure" that catalyzed Wednesday's bond rally deserve attention as a policy tool distinct from Fed rate decisions. The Treasury manages its own issuance calendar — how much it borrows at which maturities — and shifts in that calendar can affect long-end yields independently of Fed policy. If the Treasury shifted some issuance from long-duration (30-year bonds) toward short-duration (bills and notes), it would reduce supply pressure on the long end, supporting bond prices and reducing long yields. That is a different tool from monetary policy, and its sustainability depends on whether the Treasury can sustain the short-end shift without creating front-end yield pressure. The bond market's reaction — the largest single-day yield decline since the FOMC minutes week — suggests the Treasury's action was credibly supply-reducing, but the duration of that effect is the question heading into Jackson Hole.
