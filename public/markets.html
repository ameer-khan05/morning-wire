The Anchor · Wednesday Is the Week's Pivot Point — Nvidia Earnings, PCE Data, and GDP Revision All Land Before Warsh Opens Jackson Hole Thursday

The macro week that opens Monday is the most consequential for US markets since the July FOMC minutes session. Four distinct catalysts — each capable of a 1-2% single-session equity move in either direction — arrive in compressed sequence: Monday's Iran sanctions package from Bessent (market still pricing the announcement), Wednesday's Nvidia Q2 earnings after the close (96% YoY growth expected, AI-infrastructure referendum), Wednesday's Q2 GDP revision and July PCE data (which determine whether the September rate-cut base case survives the week's inflation reading), and Thursday-Friday's Jackson Hole symposium with Warsh's first-ever keynote Friday morning. The sequencing matters: the market goes into Jackson Hole with Nvidia earnings and PCE already processed, meaning Warsh speaks with knowledge of both the AI capex cycle's pulse and the latest inflation trend. The base case for equities entering the week is cautiously negative — futures are down Monday on trade war and Iran sanctions — but the base case entering the week is not the same as the base case exiting it. A Nvidia beat, a benign PCE print, and a constructive Warsh keynote could recover Monday's futures decline by end of week. A Nvidia miss, a hawkish PCE, and a cautious Warsh would produce a week more severe than the bond-volatility week that preceded it. The US-Canada trade collapse is the structural development that most directly threatens the September rate-cut rationale. Canada supplies significant portions of US energy inputs (Canadian oil accounts for roughly 60% of US oil imports), lumber, agricultural products, and manufactured goods that have embedded in US supply chains. A 50% tariff on Canadian goods passed through those supply chains — some immediately through energy prices, some over 3-6 months through construction materials, food inputs, and component costs — creates an inflationary channel that arrives precisely as the FOMC is trying to cut rates. Carney's "dollar for dollar" retaliation means Canadian goods prices rise by 50% for US buyers while US export categories face equivalent Canadian tariffs, compressing both import costs and export competitiveness simultaneously. The inflation arithmetic for Warsh at Jackson Hole Friday is now: gold at $4,680 (geopolitical safe-haven), Brent oil at $92.70 (Hormuz premium), 50% tariffs on Canada (trade inflation), Bessent Iran sanctions (potential oil supply disruption). Each element individually could be managed in a rate-cut communication; all four simultaneously is the most complex single-address challenge in the Fed's post-2022 rate-cycle history.

Oil's 1% Monday decline (WTI to $86.16, Brent to $92.70) is notable in the context of the Iran sanctions announcement: markets are pricing the Bessent announcement as existing-disruption-levels-maintained rather than new-supply-shock, consistent with the reality that Brent at $92.70 already contains a significant Hormuz disruption premium. If the sanctions include measures that directly threaten additional oil supply or shipping access beyond current Strait closure levels, oil could reverse Monday's profit-taking decline in the same session. The gold safe-haven bid at $4,680 entering Monday suggests the market is not treating the Iran sanctions as an isolated event — it is treating them as an escalation in a geopolitical environment that has been consistently more disruptive than initial pricing assumed.

US-Canada trade talks collapsed Friday night with both sides citing contradictions between the written text of a potential deal and what Canada believed it had agreed to — a breakdown in negotiations with America's second-largest trading partner that triggered 50% tariffs on billions of dollars of Canadian goods (hockey sticks, building materials, liquors, clothing) and immediate Canadian retaliation at the same rate. Prime Minister Mark Carney called the breakdown a "miscalculation" by the Trump administration, describing its demands as "uneconomic" and "unfair." The US-Canada relationship — built over decades across NAFTA and CUSMA — has now produced its most significant bilateral rupture in the modern trading era, arriving as a direct additional inflationary pressure on an economy already absorbing Hormuz oil disruption costs and Iran sanctions at the same time.

The inflationary math of the Canada tariff is the specific channel that complicates Warsh's Jackson Hole task on Friday. Canadian crude oil represents approximately 60% of US crude oil imports — at a 50% tariff rate, the effective cost of the largest single source of US oil supply increases significantly, adding to the Brent crude premium already embedded from the Hormuz crisis. Canadian lumber is a major input into US housing construction; Canadian agricultural goods contribute to food prices; Canadian manufactured components feed auto and industrial supply chains. These are not abstract inflation risks — they are pipeline inflation inputs that will show up in CPI and PCE data in September and October, precisely when the FOMC is trying to justify its September 16 rate cut on the basis of inflation approaching the 2% target.

The 10-year Treasury yield at 4.69% Friday is the data point to watch as Monday's trading session develops: the combination of Canada tariff inflation and Iran sanctions creates dual upward yield pressure that could push the 10-year back toward Thursday's 4.73% peak and compress the equity multiple headroom entering Jackson Hole week.

The oil market's 1% profit-taking decline on Monday illustrates the market's current pricing framework for the Hormuz crisis: the disruption is real and priced, but absent a new specific escalation — a new vessel seizure, an Iranian military action against a coalition partner, or a sanctions announcement that directly targets new supply sources — the current price level reflects the market's equilibrium estimate of ongoing disruption risk rather than a new shock. The Bessent sanctions announcement this morning will be the test: if the package includes measures beyond the existing financial sanctions architecture (for example, secondary sanctions with severe penalties targeting Iranian oil buyers including Chinese state energy companies), oil could reverse Monday's decline in the same session. If the package adds to the existing architecture without a new escalatory mechanism, oil holds near current levels.

Quick hits

  • Russell 2000's relative sensitivity to the Canada trade collapse deserves attention. Small-cap US companies typically carry higher domestic revenue exposure than large caps, but they also carry more floating-rate debt and more direct supply-chain exposure to Canadian inputs in manufacturing and construction sectors. The 50% Canada tariff hits small-cap building materials companies, food processors, and manufacturers who use Canadian inputs more directly than the S&P 500 mega-caps with global supply-chain alternatives. The Russell 2000's performance through Wednesday will be a cleaner read on the Canada tariff's direct business impact than the S&P 500, which is dominated by companies with sufficient pricing power and supply-chain diversification to partially offset the tariff cost.
  • Dell also reports earnings this week alongside Nvidia, and the Dell result matters for the AI-infrastructure trade for a different reason: Dell is a major server distributor for AI compute that does not manufacture AI accelerators directly. A strong Dell result confirms that AI-infrastructure demand is flowing through the full hardware stack, not just concentrating in Nvidia's silicon layer. A weak Dell result in the same week Nvidia beats would suggest that Nvidia's accelerator sales are flowing to hyperscalers but not yet generating the downstream server, storage, and networking build-out that a broadly distributed AI-infrastructure cycle would produce. The Dell-Nvidia result pair is the most diagnostic data point of the week for distinguishing between concentrated AI-chip demand and distributed AI-infrastructure demand.
