The Anchor · 10Y at 5.02% — The Yield That Enters the FOMC Window at a New Cycle High

The 10-year Treasury yield at 5.02% on Tuesday is two basis points worse than Monday’s historic 5.00% breach — and it is the yield level at which the Federal Reserve meets tomorrow. For the FOMC, the 10Y at 5.02% is both a symptom and a signal: a symptom of the cumulative rate pressure that 92% hike odds have built into the market, and a signal of how the market is interpreting the inflation persistence that drives those odds. The two basis points between Monday’s 5.00% and Tuesday’s 5.02% are not individually significant, but the direction is: the yield is moving toward rather than away from the threshold level that compresses the equity risk premium to near zero at current S&P multiples. Goldman Sachs and HSBC both confirmed their September hike calls after last week’s August CPI print came in hotter than expected — not dramatically so, but sufficiently so that two major banks that had been more cautious revised to include a September hike. That revision is what took FOMC hike odds from 86.5% Monday to 92% Tuesday. The 92% reading is the most fully priced unannounced FOMC decision of this tightening cycle; at 92%, the question has shifted from whether the hike happens to what the statement says. The statement’s key variable is the pause-versus-continuation language: “meeting by meeting” language signals a pause is possible next meeting; “additional increases may be warranted” language signals continuation. The 10Y at 5.02% is the market’s pre-announcement read on which way that language goes. If the market expected pause language, the 10Y would be pulling back from 5.00%, not pushing above it. The oil market is the other variable entering Wednesday’s decision at a structurally worse level than last meeting’s assumptions. WTI at $103.69, up 2.27% on Tuesday, and Brent at $107.50 — both sustained by the Saudi East-West pipeline shutdown that has now been in effect for 48 hours without a reported repair timeline — represent an oil supply shock that the Fed’s existing inflation assumptions were not calibrated to. The Philly Semis -5.9% is the session’s most sector-specific move, but it is also the most potentially reversible: chip stocks selling off on an AI safety call can reverse when a cleaner macro backdrop emerges after the FOMC. The oil and yield pressures are less immediately reversible and more likely to be present in the Fed’s forward projections that accompany Wednesday’s decision.

Goldman Sachs and HSBC both confirmed their September hike calls following last week’s August CPI print that came in hotter than expected, cementing the 92% market consensus. The question the FOMC statement must answer tomorrow is not whether the 25bp hike happens — it is fully priced — but what the pause-versus-continuation language says about subsequent meetings.

The 2Y yield at a new cycle high of approximately 4.68% reinforces the same message — the short end, which is most sensitive to near-term rate path, is also moving in a continuation direction. For equity markets, the 10Y at 5.02% maintains the structural pressure that produced Monday’s selloff: Treasuries offering 5.02% risk-free return versus an S&P 500 at current multiples whose earnings yield is approximately equal changes the institutional allocation math in ways that do not reverse on a single day, regardless of what the FOMC statement says about future meetings.

WTI crude rose 2.27% to $103.69 Tuesday as the Saudi East-West pipeline remained shut for a second consecutive day with no repair timeline announced, and Houthi attacks on Saudi oil infrastructure continued, maintaining the simultaneous constraint on all three major Saudi export routes. Brent crude eased slightly to $107.50 from Monday’s $108.42 as traders weighed the absence of escalation against the continued supply disruption — but the physical supply constraint is still in place, and Yanbu’s buffer supply is reducing with each day the pipeline stays shut.

The oil market entering Tuesday has been in the pipeline-shut supply constraint scenario for approximately 48 hours without a repair timeline, diplomatic resolution, or Yanbu supply announcement that would change the physical timeline. The absence of escalation — Brent not going back to $110 Tuesday — reflects a market waiting for a repair or diplomatic signal rather than pricing further deterioration. The FOMC tomorrow is the secondary oil variable: a continuation signal in the statement language would push yields and the dollar higher, which has historically softened oil prices by about 1–2% all else equal — but “all else equal” is doing a lot of work when the Saudi pipeline remains shut. The Fed can raise rates; it cannot fix a drone-damaged pumping station.

US equities fell across all major indices Tuesday, with the S&P 500 shedding 0.50% to 7,619, the Nasdaq declining 0.80% to 26,121, the Dow dropping 0.30% to 52,416, and the Russell 2000 losing 1.60% to 2,880 — pressured by the same three-factor headwind from Monday: the 10-year yield at 5.02%, WTI crude at $103.69, and AI-sector selling that drove the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index down 5.9%. The VIX firmed to 17.10, up 7.95%, as volatility priced increases ahead of the FOMC decision tomorrow. The S&P 500 is now down approximately 1.30% from Friday’s post-CPI close over two sessions.

Tuesday’s decline is extending Monday’s rather than creating a new narrative: all three headwinds are continuations. The 10Y is two basis points worse than Monday; the oil supply disruption is 24 hours more persistent without a repair timeline; the AI-sector selloff added a new 5.9% semiconductor leg. The FOMC decision tomorrow is the event that can reset all three: a pause signal in the statement language could pull the 10Y down from 5.02%, which relieves some equity risk-premium pressure; and a cleaner macro backdrop after the Fed speaks could let AI-sector stocks recover some of the training-demand-risk premium the market priced on Amodei’s statement. What the FOMC cannot reset is the Saudi pipeline — that is a physical supply event independent of the rate path.
