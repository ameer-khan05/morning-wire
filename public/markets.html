WTI crude surged 4.22% to $82.15 Tuesday morning — a $3.33 gain from Monday's $78.82, the largest single-day oil move since the Iran conflict's February opening — as Trump's new demand that Iran compensate for all casualties reversed Hormuz deal optimism in a single morning and repriced the conflict premium from "deal imminent" to "deadlock risk entering UNSC vote"; Brent reached $88.04 (+4.94%), compressing the Brent-WTI spread slightly. The oil spike transmitted into safe-haven assets and away from risk assets simultaneously: gold rose to $4,402 (+1.70%) as geopolitical safe-haven demand combined with the inflationary expectations that higher oil generates, UST 10Y yields rose to 4.67% as the inflation signal pushed some rate-cut pricing out of the near-term rate curve, equities slipped modestly (S&P −0.17%, Nasdaq −0.42%, Dow −0.25%, Russell 2000 −0.17%), and Bitcoin fell to $63,900 (−1.97%) as the rate-cut narrative weakened alongside the macro tailwind. Monday's S&P record close of 7,766 is being held as the week's reference point — Tuesday's decline is small relative to Friday's +0.62% jobs-shock rally — but the oil context means tomorrow's CPI arrives in a more inflationary framing than it would have without Tuesday's $3.33 WTI move.

Tomorrow's July CPI at 8:30am ET (the same morning as the total solar eclipse) is now the most consequential single data point of the week, and Tuesday's oil move has made the CPI interpretation more complex than it would have been with oil still at $78.82. The July CPI measures prices collected through mid-August, which means Tuesday's WTI surge does not appear in the reported number — but it frames how the market reads any borderline print. A 2.7% reading with oil at $82 carries a different interpretation than a 2.7% reading with oil at $78: the former gets characterized as "benign data, but energy costs are already rising again for the August print." PPI releases Wednesday and jobless claims Thursday are secondary to CPI but will provide additional context for how the rate-path story develops after tomorrow's main event.

Tuesday's oil move is the market's most direct response to Trump's Iran compensation demand, and it is larger and faster than Monday's $78.82 open: WTI's $3.33 gain to $82.15 in a single morning represents the commodity market's repricing of the Hormuz deal probability from "on the verge" (Monday's FM Araghchi framing) to "sequencing deadlock with new conditions" (Tuesday's Trump-Iran FM exchange), with the conflict premium jumping from ~$5-6 above a fully-reopened scenario to ~$9-10 above it at $82.15 assuming a fully-reopened oil market would clear near $72-73. The WTI-to-Brent spread narrowed slightly to $5.89 ($88.04 − $82.15) as the geopolitical premium applied broadly to global crude benchmarks, consistent with a genuine supply-disruption risk pricing rather than a localized US crude premium. Equities context: S&P 7,753 (−0.17% from Monday's close of 7,766); Nasdaq 26,579 (−0.42%); Dow 53,902 (−0.25%); Russell 2000 3,036 (−0.17%). The equity market is not pricing a Hormuz deal collapse or a sustained oil-inflation shock — the declines are modest enough to characterize as "pre-CPI positioning" rather than "geopolitical panic." The gold and UST 10Y moves are the more informative signals: gold at $4,402 (+1.7%) and yields at 4.67% (up from 4.64%) show the market is simultaneously pricing safe-haven demand (gold up) and inflationary concern (yields up, rate-cut expectations partially reversed), the joint signal of a geopolitical-inflationary shock rather than a pure risk-off or pure inflation event. The key market relationship to watch on Tuesday is the WTI-Gold correlation. Gold rose 1.7% while WTI rose 4.2% — which means the geopolitical safe-haven signal (gold) is running at about 40% of the magnitude of the commodity-inflation signal (WTI). A higher Gold/WTI ratio (gold rising closer to WTI's pace) would suggest the market is reading the Iran escalation as a safe-haven event primarily; a lower ratio (gold rising much less than WTI) would suggest it is reading it as an inflation event primarily. Tuesday's 40% ratio — $4,402 gold on a $82.15 WTI — is squarely in "both simultaneously" territory, which is the correct read for a geopolitical-oil shock where the inflation and safe-haven channels are both active. Tomorrow's CPI determines which channel the market closes: a benign print closes the inflation channel, gold's gain moderates, and WTI retreats on deal-optimism restored; a hot print validates the inflation channel, gold extends, and WTI holds or rises further.

The week's data calendar after Tuesday's oil open: July CPI (Wednesday August 12, 8:30am ET); PPI Wednesday (secondary); jobless claims Thursday. The August 17 UNSC freedom-of-navigation vote (6 days) is the geopolitical catalyst that could change the oil price environment more significantly than any data release this week — a UNSC passage with broad support would de-escalate the conflict premium (WTI back toward $76-77), while a Russian or Chinese veto would validate Tuesday's $82 level as the new conflict-premium floor.

Tuesday's WTI crude surge to $82.15 — up $3.33 from Monday's $78.82, a 4.22% single-day gain — is the oil market's rapid repricing of Hormuz deal probability from the "on the verge" framing that Iran's FM offered on Monday to the deadlock-risk scenario that Trump's new compensation demand and Iran's FM refusal to lift-the-blockade-before-anything created by Tuesday morning; the $3.33 premium increase represents the commodity market's estimate of how much the deal probability declined in a 24-hour window, and it is large — Monday's $78.82 already carried a ~$5-6 conflict premium above the fully-reopened scenario, and Tuesday's $82.15 implies a ~$9-10 premium, more than doubling the deal-proximity signal in a single news cycle. Brent's parallel 4.94% rise to $88.04 confirms the global crude market is reading the development the same way, with the Brent-WTI spread narrowing slightly to $5.89 as both benchmarks repriced simultaneously rather than one leading.

The Trump compensation demand is analytically important for how the oil market should interpret it: if the demand is a negotiating bid (designed to trade down to a more realistic condition), oil at $82 is pricing excessive risk — the market would fall back toward $78-79 on confirmation that the demand is positional rather than literal. If it is a genuine new precondition that neither side can easily trade away — retrospective compensation for proxy-war casualties spanning decades has no precedent in any comparable conflict resolution — then $82 is the new conflict-premium floor entering the UNSC vote. The UNSC vote on August 17 is where this ambiguity resolves: a broad-majority resolution passage signals that the US has the multilateral framing to withdraw the blockade without conceding the compensation demand; a veto means the bilateral path is all that's left, and a bilateral deal requires addressing the compensation demand directly.

Tuesday's equity declines — S&P −0.17%, Nasdaq −0.42%, Dow −0.25%, Russell 2000 −0.17% — are modest enough relative to the magnitude of the oil spike (+4.22%) that the correct characterization is pre-CPI positioning rather than geopolitical panic: a market genuinely pricing a Hormuz deal collapse or a sustained oil-inflation shock would show equity declines of 1-2%+ alongside WTI at $82, not 0.17-0.42% across indices. The muted equity response tells you that the base case for equity investors is still "the oil spike is a geopolitical premium that the UNSC process or continued Oman mediation will resolve before it transmits into sustained inflation" — the same base case the bond market is expressing with a 3bp UST 10Y yield rise (4.64% to 4.67%) rather than a larger move. Monday's S&P record close of 7,766 remains the week's structural reference point; all four major indices are holding within 0.5% of Monday's levels, preserving the record without extending it before tomorrow's CPI data.

The CPI scenarios and their equity implications: a benign print (≤2.7% YoY) confirms the disinflationary trend, makes Tuesday's oil-spike inflationary concern look like a transient geopolitical premium, pushes the September rate-hike probability below 30%, and allows equities to recover toward 7,800 on the S&P — the next technical level above Monday's record. A hot print (≥2.9%), in the context of oil already at $82.15, produces the interpretation that inflation is warming from both the demand side (the July data) and the supply side (the oil signal) simultaneously — pushing hike probability above 50%, reversing Friday's dovish repricing, and making the S&P's 7,766 record the new resistance rather than a floor to hold above. Gold's 1.70% rise to $4,402 on Tuesday is a joint expression of the two channels that a geopolitical-oil shock activates simultaneously: the safe-haven channel (risk-off demand for gold as geopolitical tension rises) and the inflation-hedge channel (higher oil prices raising inflationary expectations, supporting gold as a store-of-value hedge). The Gold/WTI ratio — gold rising 1.70% versus WTI's 4.22% — being approximately 40% tells you the market is weighting these two channels roughly equally: geopolitical premium is the driver but inflation concern is co-present. UST 10Y yields rising 3bp to 4.67% confirms the inflation-hedge read: a pure safe-haven event (risk-off without inflation) would lower yields (bond buying); yields rising with gold rising means the market is simultaneously pricing safe-haven demand and inflationary expectations, which is the correct two-channel interpretation of a geopolitical oil shock. Monday's gold pullback to $4,328.63 (−1.65%) from Friday's $4,401.30 spike now looks like a brief interlude rather than a directional reversal — Tuesday's $4,402 puts gold modestly above Friday's high, establishing a new recent peak.

Tomorrow's CPI is where one of the two gold channels resolves: a benign print (≤2.7%) confirms that July inflation was contained, reduces the inflation-hedge component of gold's demand, and allows the geopolitical-safe-haven component to be the sole remaining active channel — gold would likely moderate toward $4,360-4,380 as the inflation bid fades while the Hormuz premium supports the floor. A hot print (≥2.9%) validates the inflation-hedge channel, and gold with both channels active — safe-haven demand and inflation-hedge demand simultaneously, with oil at $82 providing ongoing support — could extend significantly. Quick hits

  • The specific mechanism by which WTI's Tuesday move from $78.82 to $82.15 affects tomorrow's CPI interpretation deserves the same precise treatment as was given to Monday's smaller oil move. July CPI reflects prices collected through mid-July to mid-August — so gasoline prices in the August collection period that rose from the earlier June-July Hormuz premium are captured; however, Tuesday's new surge from $78.82 to $82.15 happens in the period after the July CPI's data collection window, meaning it does not appear in tomorrow's reported number. What it does do is add approximately 8% to current retail gasoline costs above the level that the July CPI captured — at a retail pass-through rate of roughly 90%, that translates to approximately $0.32 per gallon of additional gasoline price above what tomorrow's CPI will report. Energy economists will cite this delta (approximately 0.2-0.3 percentage points of CPI if sustained for a full reporting period) when analyzing tomorrow's print; a July CPI at 2.7% with WTI at $82.15 on the reporting day is being interpreted as "trailing indicator; forward-looking inflation is 2.9-3.0%." That forward-inflation characterization can move the rate-hike probability just as much as the reported number, which is why oil's Tuesday level matters for tomorrow's CPI interpretation even though it is not in the data.
  • The Russell 2000's −0.17% Tuesday decline is smaller than the Nasdaq's −0.42% — a reversal of the typical small-cap-underperforms pattern in risk-off environments. The Russell 2000's relative resilience on a day of significant oil spike suggests that small-cap investors are not yet pricing the oil-inflation scenario as a sustained earnings headwind for smaller companies (which are typically more domestic, more credit-sensitive, and more oil-exposed in relative terms than large-cap tech). If oil holds above $82 through next week, the Russell 2000 should underperform as the sustained cost-inflation signal reaches forward earnings estimates for domestic small caps. Tuesday's Russell 2000 holding close to Monday's 3,034.49 level is either a lagged response that will catch up on Wednesday's CPI, or it is the small-cap market correctly reading the oil spike as temporary — the same "temporary geopolitical premium" read that the equity market broadly is expressing through its modest declines.
