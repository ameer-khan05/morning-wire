Oil collapsed overnight — WTI to $79.54 (▼5.26%) and Brent to $83.32 (▼5.15%), below their pre-conflict price levels for the first time since the Iran conflict began February 28 — as financial markets priced the probability of a US-Iran peace agreement following Trump's Saturday announcement that planned strikes were canceled and talks would resume Monday; US equity futures advanced 0.63% (S&P 500), 0.59% (Nasdaq), and 0.49% (Dow) at the pre-market, while the 10-year Treasury yield eased 6 basis points to 4.52% and gold gained 1.67% to $4,111.50 as the combination of oil relief and persisting diplomatic uncertainty drove a simultaneous "peace trade" in oil and risk assets alongside a safe-haven bid in gold that reflects investor skepticism about whether the peace framework is durable. Iran said it is not in direct negotiations with the United States, is speaking only to Oman about the Strait of Hormuz, and that the strait remains restricted while US military pressure continues.

The oil collapse and the gold gain are, on the surface, contradictory signals: oil falls because peace is expected, gold rises because peace is uncertain. Both are correct simultaneously. Markets are pricing a peace probability that is meaningfully higher than it was Friday — enough to move oil 5-6% — but not a peace certainty that would eliminate the safe-haven bid entirely. The diplomatic gap between Trump's "deal is imminent" and Iran's "we are talking to Oman, not you" is the uncertainty premium that gold is capturing while oil prices the peace probability. Friday's US Jobs Report is the week's primary remaining macro catalyst after today's oil-Iran opening.

The oil collapse is the day's defining event, and its interpretation requires holding two things simultaneously. The price move is real — WTI below $80 is a threshold that has not been crossed since the conflict began, and it objectively improves the energy-CPI argument for a September rate cut. The diplomatic mechanism that produced the move is uncertain — Iran says it is not talking to the US, the Oman channel's status is unclear, and the history of 34 days of conflict includes two pauses that both preceded resumed escalation. Monday's market open will price both simultaneously: the genuine macro improvement from oil below $80, and the reversal risk from an Iran diplomatic framework that is not yet formally agreed. The gold gain captures the reversal risk; the futures gain captures the macro improvement. Both are correct. Which resolves into a confirmed peace deal this week determines whether Monday's oil move is permanent or temporary.

Gold's behavior on Monday morning deserves close attention because it is pricing a variable that oil is not. Oil is pricing the probability of a peace deal — it is a forward-looking commodity that moves on expected supply changes, and the expectation of Hormuz reopening is sufficient to drive a 5-6% move even without a confirmed agreement. Gold is pricing the uncertainty about whether the peace framework is durable — gold's safe-haven bid exists specifically because of geopolitical and diplomatic uncertainty, and uncertainty has not been eliminated by Trump's announcement: Iran's denial of direct talks and the Oman channel's unconfirmed status mean the geopolitical risk premium is reduced but not eliminated. The divergence between falling oil and rising gold is the market's precise articulation of "we believe peace is more likely but not certain" — a nuanced probability assessment that both assets are pricing correctly given the available information.

The 2-year yield is leading the front-end pricing — more sensitive to near-term rate expectations than the 10Y, the 2Y is the instrument that will most directly reflect changes in FOMC September probability as the week's data arrives. If Friday's Jobs Report is softer than expected and oil stays below $80 through the week, the 2Y would likely move first and lead the 10Y toward a further easing that compresses equity risk premiums and supports the current futures gains at the open.

WTI crude fell to $79.54 (▼5.26%) and Brent to $83.32 (▼5.15%) overnight — both below pre-conflict price levels for the first time since the US-Iran conflict began February 28 — in the largest single-session commodity move of the 34-day conflict, as markets priced the probability of a peace agreement following Trump's Saturday announcement; US equity futures reflected the oil relief with S&P 500 futures ▲0.63%, Nasdaq ▲0.59%, and Dow ▲0.49% at the pre-market open, though Iran's statement that it is speaking to Oman rather than directly to the US introduces a reversal risk that limits how confidently the oil move can be treated as structural rather than sentiment-driven: if the Oman channel fails and Iran resumes Hormuz restrictions, oil returns above $85 within one to two sessions, compressing the September rate-cut probability improvement that oil below $80 is currently providing.

The oil move's most immediate market consequence is the September rate-cut probability improvement. Three FOMC hike dissenters have explicitly cited oil's contribution to energy-CPI as their primary reason for maintaining the hawkish assessment — at $79.54 WTI, the energy-inflation argument weakens in a way that is publicly visible and difficult to maintain if oil holds below $80 through the next two weeks. Brent at $83.32 — below $85 for the first time since the conflict — eliminates the oil-over-$85 trigger that has been the clearest threshold associated with the "hawkish stay" assessment. If oil stays below $80 through Friday's Jobs Report and then Jobs data is softer than expected, the September cut probability would improve materially before August CPI — the most favorable pre-CPI environment for risk assets since the conflict began.
