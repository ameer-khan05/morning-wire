The Federal Open Market Committee meets Tuesday July 29 with a near-certain hold on the federal funds rate and Chair Powell's press conference as the primary market event — the question is not Tuesday's decision but whether Powell's language on Brent oil's inflation implications signals September as near-certain or data-dependent. If Powell incorporates $100+ Brent into the FOMC's updated medium-term inflation baseline, September probability re-accelerates from current elevated levels toward 70%+. If he describes the oil spike as a supply shock from an identifiable geopolitical event that the FOMC is assessing rather than incorporating, September probability compresses toward 50-60%, where a sub-consensus July CPI print due in mid-August could hold the rate flat. The UST 10-year yield at 4.68% — down 4 basis points from Thursday's 4.72% peak but still elevated relative to June levels — is the rate market's current baseline for September.

Powell's communication challenge at Tuesday's press conference is the simultaneous existence of two contradictory oil signals: Thursday's $100 crossing (a clear inflation signal) and Friday's 3.89% retreat (partial reversal on ceasefire news). A chair who addresses both signals simultaneously can frame the $100 crossing as an assessed event rather than a baseline update — which is the interpretation that compresses September odds. A chair who addresses only the crossing without acknowledging the retreat signals that the FOMC's baseline has updated to reflect $100+ oil regardless of Friday's move. The technical challenge is that $96.78 on Friday, while below $100, remains significantly above the $88-92 June range; "assessed" language at $96.78 is a harder argument than it would be at $91. That asymmetry is what keeps September hike odds elevated even after Friday's retreat.

Alphabet's 7% decline and Tesla's 14% decline on Thursday require separate interpretations. Alphabet's decline reflected the market's reassessment of whether AI infrastructure capex — the multi-hundred-billion-dollar investment cycle in data centers, custom chips, and cooling infrastructure — is producing free cash flow returns on a timeline that justifies current multiples. The debate is not whether AI infrastructure is valuable, but whether Q3 and Q4 2026 show the returns, or whether returns arrive in 2027-2028 and require current-quarter multiple compression to accommodate. Tesla's decline on margin guidance is a conventional margin concern (energy-input costs from elevated oil flowing through automotive manufacturing) that oil normalization resolves faster than the Alphabet problem. When oil normalizes, the Tesla concern eases; the Alphabet concern requires demonstrated FCF improvement. That distinction is what keeps the Nasdaq's recovery trajectory slower than the Dow's: Dow components benefit from oil normalization; Nasdaq's largest weights have a second problem that oil does not solve.
