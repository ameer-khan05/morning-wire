Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh delivered a hawkish first Jackson Hole keynote Friday, declaring that inflation is "still too high" and that the Fed "has more work to do" — language that sent September rate-hike odds surging to 55.7% on CME FedWatch, from roughly 35% the prior day, and triggered a broad equity selloff that partially unwound Thursday's Nvidia-led gains. The S&P 500 fell 0.30% to 7,715.47, the Nasdaq fell 0.50% to 26,411.07, the Dow fell 0.40% to 53,576.13, and the Russell 2000 fell 0.40% to 3,014.99. The 10-year Treasury yield rose to approximately 4.84% from Thursday's 4.77%. WTI crude slipped to $82.82 (▼0.86%) while Brent held at approximately $87.87 on continued Hormuz uncertainty. Gold fell to approximately $4,595 (▼0.71%) under the combined pressure of higher yields and rising rate-hike expectations. The FOMC blackout begins Saturday — no Fed communications until after the September 15 CLARITY vote and the September 16 rate decision.

Warsh's speech was the most consequential single Fed communication since the November 2022 Powell pivot — not because of its content in isolation, but because of its timing. It lands 18 days before the CLARITY Act cloture vote, 19 days before the FOMC rate decision, and after a week in which Thursday's Nvidia $96 billion earnings validated the AI infrastructure thesis and a 3.7% PCE reading complicated the rate-cut case simultaneously. Warsh resolved the policy ambiguity unambiguously: inflation is too high, the Fed may hike. Markets had to reprice.

The Anchor · Warsh's Jackson Hole Keynote Resolves the Week's Central Ambiguity — and the Resolution Is Hawkish

Thursday's edition identified the ambiguity entering Friday's Warsh keynote: 3.7% PCE had complicated the rate-cut case, but Warsh's preferred scenario — validating AI investment and signaling continued accommodation — was still the prior consensus. Friday's speech resolved it. "Inflation is still too high, the Fed has more work to do" is not an ambiguous statement for a Fed Chair who holds the September meeting in 19 days. It is the clearest possible signal that the September cut is not coming, and that the path from here is flat-to-hiking rather than flat-to-cutting. The 55.7% rate-hike probability now embedded in futures markets is the pricing of that signal. The bond market moved first — the 10-year Treasury yield rose from 4.77% Thursday to approximately 4.84% Friday — and the equity market followed, with the Nasdaq giving back 0.50% of Thursday's Nvidia-fueled 1.50% gain. The macro backdrop for the remainder of 2026 now has a settled shape that was absent entering this week. Warsh's speech, combined with Thursday's 3.7% PCE reading, establishes the following context: inflation is running at 170 basis points above the 2% target on the Fed's preferred gauge, the new Fed Chair has declared price stability the "predominant focus," and the probability that the rate path from here leads up rather than down is now above 50%. For equity markets, this is a discount-rate problem rather than a growth problem — Nvidia's $96.22 billion Q2 result confirmed that AI-driven growth is real and accelerating, but the discount rate applied to that growth's long-duration earnings trajectory has just risen. Tech stocks that trade on 2028-2030 AI earnings — the high-multiple cohort that drove the Nasdaq's +1.50% Thursday — are the most sensitive to that discount rate shift, which explains why Friday's selloff was most pronounced in the Nasdaq. Gold's 0.71% decline reflects the same repricing: at 4.84% 10-year yields in a rate-hiking environment, the opportunity cost of holding non-yielding gold rises. The Hormuz context modifies but does not change this picture: WTI and Brent are broadly steady on continued uncertainty about whether Iran's conditions will ever be met, but the Warsh speech is the dominant market variable, not oil. FOMC blackout begins Saturday — the markets will process the week's events without further Fed guidance until the October meeting previews the final shape of the rate cycle.

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh delivered a hawkish first Jackson Hole keynote Friday, declaring that inflation is "still too high" and the central bank "has more work to do" — reversing the September rate-cut consensus that had been the market base case entering the week. CME FedWatch September rate-hike odds jumped to 55.7% from roughly 35% Thursday; some analysts placed the probability of a hike by October or December at above 70%. Markets initially edged higher on Warsh's comment that "the economy appears to have strengthened," then turned lower as the cumulative hawkish message settled. The Nasdaq fell 0.50% to 26,411.07, the S&P 500 fell 0.30% to 7,715.47, and short-term Treasury yields rose several basis points. The FOMC blackout begins Saturday — the last major market input before the September 15 CLARITY vote and the September 16 FOMC decision is now in the rear-view mirror.

Warsh did not imply an imminent September hike, but he came as close as a sitting Fed Chair typically does to signaling that the rate path is up rather than down when the data justifies it. The July PCE reading of 3.7% year-over-year — 170 basis points above the 2% target — is the data that justifies it. Historically, the Fed has cut rates with headline PCE at 3.7% only when facing a growth-side crisis (recession risk, banking stress, pandemic) that outweighs the inflation concern. No such crisis exists in the current data. Warsh's speech is the market's signal that the Fed has absorbed that logic and will act accordingly.

Iran's Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said Friday that returning to diplomatic negotiations "isn't impossible," calling it a new opening following "creative discussions" with Qatari officials — while simultaneously laying out conditions for Hormuz reopening that require the US to lift its naval blockade, withdraw military forces from the region, pay war reparations, and release frozen Iranian assets. CNN reported the same day that the US says it is "not talking to Iran" — a direct contradiction of Tehran's diplomatic framing that illustrates the gap between Iran's public posture and actual negotiating engagement. Mine-clearing operations have not begun on Day 59 of the crisis, and the Strait's commodity vessel throughput remains at just 5 per day on Tuesday versus the pre-crisis average of roughly 15 per day. Brent crude held at approximately $87.87, essentially unchanged from Thursday, as markets balanced Iran's diplomatic softening against the absence of any concrete progress.

The Hormuz market read has become more nuanced in the past 48 hours. Wednesday's Iranian-Omani deal announcement sent Brent from $89+ toward $85.91; Thursday's IRGC complications sent it back to $87.65; Friday's diplomatic language from the FM held it at approximately $87.87. The market is pricing a roughly 25-30% probability of meaningful Hormuz progress in the near term — enough to cap the oil premium but not enough to materially reduce it. The gap between Tehran's FM saying "diplomacy isn't impossible" and CNN's confirmation that Washington is "not talking to Iran" is the core market risk: if either side changes its posture — US engages or Iran withdraws — the commodity price reaction will be immediate.
