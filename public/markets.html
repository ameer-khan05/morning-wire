The Anchor · Reading Thursday’s Market — Why the 10-Year at 5.11% Hit the Russell 2000 Harder Than the Nasdaq

The 10-year Treasury yield hitting 5.11% — its highest since 2007 — triggered a uniform equity sell-off but not a uniform one. The Russell 2000 fell 1.55%; the Nasdaq fell 0.54%. That 1-percentage-point gap is the rate sensitivity map of the US equity market in a single session. Small-cap companies in the Russell 2000 carry substantially more floating-rate debt than large-cap multinationals in the Nasdaq. Their bank loans, revolving credit facilities, and leveraged buyout financing structures are priced at a spread to the federal funds rate or to SOFR — which means that every 25 basis points of rate hike translates directly into higher interest expense on their existing debt loads without requiring any deterioration in their underlying business. A company with $500 million in floating-rate debt at a 250-basis-point spread over SOFR currently pays approximately $31.25 million per year in interest; if October’s 75.3%-probability hike materializes, that becomes $32.5 million — a 4% increase in interest expense on unchanged revenue. Across thousands of Russell 2000 companies, that arithmetic is the structural reason small-caps are more sensitive to rate hike probability spikes than Nasdaq mega-caps, which typically have fixed-rate investment-grade bond financing at longer maturities. The Nasdaq’s 0.54% decline is the mega-cap equivalent: tech companies at this scale carry enough cash and credit capacity that near-term rate moves primarily affect their equity valuations through the duration channel — higher discount rates, lower present values — rather than through direct cash flow impact. The 10-year’s move from 4.913% on Wednesday to 5.11% on Thursday is 19.7 basis points in a single session — driven by Williams’s comment but amplified by the global bond rout that followed. When US yields spike, Japanese investors who had purchased US Treasuries for yield receive a signal that future US bond issuance will carry higher coupons, making their current holdings less attractive. Japan’s 10-year hitting its highest since 1996 is the overseas transmission of Thursday’s Fed signal: the global bond sell-off that followed Williams’s comment reflects how interconnected sovereign bond markets are when the world’s most important central bank signals a tightening bias. Oil’s move above $100 for Brent is a separate story with a separate driver. Iran’s airline shutdown entering its second day without any diplomatic breakthrough is the price-relevant signal: the market had already re-rated away from Tuesday’s Rubio overture hopes; Thursday adds another day of confirmed pressure escalation to the accumulated supply risk premium. Brent at $101 is the market’s current pricing of the Iran supply risk under Operation Economic Outcast’s sustained pressure campaign. Gold’s 0.94% decline — as the risk-free rate rises to 5.11% — is the opportunity cost channel at work. At 5.11%, the 10-year Treasury yields more than gold in absolute terms and with less volatility; the allocation case for gold as an income-replacement store of value weakens at high real risk-free rates, and some of the geopolitical hedge premium that drove gold to $4,391 on Tuesday unwinds as the market focuses on rate dynamics rather than summit outcomes.

The 10-year Treasury yield spiked 19.7 basis points to 5.11% on Thursday — its highest level since 2007 — after New York Fed President John Williams said in a speech that it would be “reasonable” to expect another rate hike before year-end, driving October hike odds to 75.3% and December hike odds to 58.6% and triggering a global bond sell-off that pushed Japan’s 10-year yield to its highest since 1996. The 30-year Treasury simultaneously rose to its highest since 2004, with the full yield curve repricing upward as the market revised its terminal rate expectations.

Williams’s comment is the precise type of signal that triggers outsized bond market reactions: it came from a voting FOMC member with a reputation for signaling consensus rather than minority views, on a day when the global yield rout was already a live transmission risk across sovereign markets. Japan’s 10-year at its highest since 1996 is the overseas feedback loop: higher US yields attract capital toward Treasuries, reducing demand for Japanese government bonds at existing yields, and Japanese investors holding US Treasuries receive a signal to reassess duration. The global bond rout is the US rate signal propagating across the sovereign fixed income universe in real time.

All major US equity indices fell Thursday as the 10-year yield spiked to a 19-year high, with the S&P 500 shedding 0.66% to 7,706, the Nasdaq declining 0.54% to 26,936, the Dow losing 0.74% to 51,512, and the Russell 2000 falling the hardest at 1.55% to 2,862 — a 1-percentage-point gap between the Russell and Nasdaq that reflects exactly how differently floating-rate debt obligations affect small-cap companies versus investment-grade mega-caps, with rate hike expectations directly translating into higher interest expense for the leveraged small-cap balance sheets that dominate the Russell.

The 24-hour reversal in Russell-versus-Nasdaq performance is the clearest evidence that Wednesday’s Russell outperformance was summit-driven and Thursday’s underperformance is rate-driven. Wednesday: small-caps optimistic about domestic summit outcomes, outperform. Thursday: Williams signals another hike, floating-rate exposure hits small-caps hardest, underperform by 1 percentage point. The same companies that benefited from the summit confidence thesis gave back those gains plus additional decline when the rate environment repriced sharply. Both signals are real; the rate signal simply overrides the summit signal in size.

Brent crude crossed $100 per barrel for the first time since July — rising 1.51% to $101.00 — and WTI climbed 1.95% to $98.28 as Iran’s airline shutdown entered its second consecutive day under Operation Economic Outcast without any diplomatic breakthrough, confirming that the supply risk premium has a structural foundation rather than a temporary catalyst basis. Secretary Rubio’s UNGA overture toward Iran diplomacy, which had briefly discounted oil prices earlier in the week, has produced no visible progress across 48 hours.

Brent above $100 re-establishes the $100 level as the structural oil market proxy for Middle East geopolitical risk under the current US-Iran conflict framework. The pattern from the past two weeks is now clear: oil falls when diplomatic progress signals emerge and rises when those signals fail to materialize into actual talks. After two signal-failure cycles in five days — Tuesday’s Rubio overture bounce now fully reversed — the market is effectively pricing in no near-term diplomatic resolution to the supply constraint. The combination of the Iran supply risk with the Fed-driven risk-off move produces an unusual environment: oil rising on the same day equities fall, which breaks the typical risk-on correlation.
