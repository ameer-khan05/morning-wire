US equities fell for a third consecutive session: S&P 500 ▼0.17%, Dow ▼0.20%, Nasdaq ▼0.28%, Russell ▼0.15%, with the 30-year Treasury yield hitting its highest level in nearly two decades — the bond market's statement that inflation duration risk is repricing faster than the growth slowdown that would normally accompany falling equities.

Brent crude rose to $91.52 — its fourth consecutive daily gain and highest level since May — as Hormuz Day 50 produced no diplomatic progress and the UAE's detection of two Iranian ballistic missiles added a new escalation premium to oil prices. Gold held at $4,498.50 (+0.41%), maintaining the stagflation-risk configuration of rising gold, rising yields, and falling equities that has characterized the past three sessions. Walmart reports Thursday, providing the week's most important consumer health data in a 50-day Hormuz cost environment.

The Anchor · FOMC July Minutes Are a Hawkish-at-the-Margin Read That the Bond Market Was Already Pricing — the 30-Year Yield at a Near-Two-Decade High Is Not a Reaction to the Minutes; It Is a Preview of the Hormuz-Oil-Inflation Channel

The FOMC July minutes confirmed what the bond market had been pricing for two weeks: the committee, as of July, was not in a rush to cut rates aggressively, with members describing inflation risks as remaining two-sided and calling for a gradual pace of easing. The September 16 rate decision is now almost certainly 25 basis points — the minutes close the door on a 50-basis-point cut while confirming that the first cut of the cycle is coming. For equities, the minutes are a marginal negative: a smaller September cut means less monetary support for valuation multiples in a higher-yield environment. The 30-year Treasury yield's movement to its highest level in nearly two decades is a more consequential signal than the minutes themselves — it reflects the bond market's view that inflation will be persistent for longer than the Fed's July framework assumed, driven by an oil-price channel (Brent $91.52, up from Tuesday's $88.77 and rising further) that the July meeting could not have priced. The critical context: the July FOMC meeting was conducted before the Hormuz ceasefire expired on August 17. The minutes therefore describe a Fed thinking about inflation in a world where the Islamabad MOU was still operative and the Hormuz closure — at the time of the July meeting — still had a ceasefire framework around it. Since the July meeting, the ceasefire has expired, Trump has threatened Oman, no replacement framework is under negotiation, and Brent crude has risen through $91. The minutes' hawkish elements look more justified — and potentially insufficiently hawkish — when read in that updated context. Gold's persistence near Tuesday's record ($4,498.50, +0.41%) while the 30-year yield also rises is the stagflation-risk signal configuration: an asset (gold) that rises in both inflation and deflation scenarios, combined with a yield curve (30Y at decade high) that is pricing inflation duration, and equities that are declining because the growth-inflation tradeoff is tilting toward inflation rather than growth. The VIX's decline to 14.25 (▼2.60%) is the anomaly: it suggests options markets are pricing the Iran risk as persistent and priced rather than acute and escalating — a distinction that is correct in political terms (the situation is persistent, not solved) but that may underweight the probability of a specific escalation event that the UAE missile detection on Tuesday was. The Jackson Hole significance: Fed Chair Powell's remarks at Jackson Hole on Friday August 28 are the next major market-moving Fed communication event after today's minutes. Jackson Hole is where the Fed has historically used academic conference settings to signal major policy shifts or recalibrations — the "substantial progress" language on tapering in 2021, the "unconditionally committed" inflation language in 2022. In 2026, the question for Jackson Hole is whether Powell addresses the Hormuz-oil channel explicitly and whether he modifies the September cut guidance that today's minutes have now established. A Jackson Hole speech that does not mention Hormuz would be interpreted as the Fed treating oil's move as temporary; a speech that does mention Hormuz and its inflation implications would be read as the committee considering a more hawkish path than today's minutes imply. The market's September cut pricing will reprice in either direction based on the Jackson Hole tone.

Walmart's Thursday earnings report is the week's most actionable fundamental data point for the real economy. Fifty days of Hormuz closure has raised energy and input costs across the supply chain; Walmart, as the US's largest retailer with the broadest consumer exposure across income levels, will report whether those elevated costs are being absorbed by margin compression, passed through to consumer prices, or dampening consumer spending volume. The guidance Walmart provides on its consumer outlook through the end of 2026 — covering the period in which the Hormuz crisis's economic damage compounds further — is the data point that will determine whether "stagflation" remains a risk scenario or becomes the consensus characterization of the US economy heading into Q4 2026.

WTI crude rose to $85.47 (+0.65%). The four-session gain is not noise: it is oil markets pricing the extended duration of a disruption that has now entered its 50th day with no active diplomatic framework and new escalation signals rather than de-escalation ones.

The $95 Brent threshold is the critical level for the Fed's September rate decision: above $95, the oil-CPI pass-through becomes acute enough to meaningfully complicate a September cut, because easing monetary policy into accelerating headline inflation (driven by energy) is the textbook definition of policy error. At $91.52 — 4% below $95 — the market is within four sessions of sustained gains reaching that threshold if the current trajectory continues. The UAE missile detection Wednesday adds a new escalation premium that was not in the market on Tuesday morning; the question is whether that premium is a one-day adjustment or the beginning of a sustained repricing toward the physical supply disruption scenario that oil markets have been pricing as a tail risk.

Quick hits

  • The 30-year Treasury yield at a near-two-decade high deserves unpacking. The 30-year is the bond market's clearest statement about inflation over the very long run — institutional investors who buy 30-year bonds are expressing a view about what average inflation will be over the next three decades, and demanding a yield that compensates them for that expected inflation plus a term premium for the duration risk. A near-two-decade high on the 30-year in August 2026 reflects the accumulation of three inflation risk factors: Hormuz oil disruption (energy CPI pass-through), FOMC minutes that were hawkish-at-the-margin (monetary easing is slower than previously expected), and a fiscal deficit trajectory (US federal deficit remains structurally elevated) that bond investors are pricing as a persistent issuance pressure on the long end. The 30-year's movement is more informative than the 10-year in this context — the 10-year is heavily influenced by near-term rate cut pricing (Sept 16 decision), while the 30-year reflects the beyond-Fed-cycle inflation view.
  • VIX at 14.25 (▼2.60%) when the UAE detected Iranian ballistic missiles is a disconnect worth noting. The VIX measures 30-day implied volatility on the S&P 500 based on options pricing — it captures what options buyers are paying to hedge against equity swings over the next month. A 2.60% VIX decline on a day when a new escalation event (UAE missile detection) and hawkish Fed minutes both hit the tape is unusual. The most plausible interpretation: options markets have shifted from pricing Iran as a potential acute crisis event (VIX spike) to pricing it as a persistent background condition (lower VIX, higher oil). That shift is correct as a characterization of the current situation — the crisis is chronic not acute — but it may underweight the probability of a specific trigger event (a US-Iran military exchange, a Hormuz chokepoint incident) that would reprice volatility rapidly. Cheap hedges against that tail risk exist at current VIX levels.
