US equities finished mixed Friday after October’s Producer Price Index delivered a bifurcated signal: monthly PPI at 0.3% — below the 0.4% consensus — drove a gap-up open that lifted the S&P 500 0.8% to 7,592 and the Dow 0.8% to 52,297, while the year-over-year rate hit 6.5% — the highest since November 2022 — pushed the 10-year Treasury yield to 5.14% and hammered the Russell 2000 2.6% lower to 2,764 as rate-sensitive small caps absorbed the full force of yields crossing 5%; Nasdaq slipped 0.1% to 25,366 as Nvidia continued its post-CEO-resignation decline; Brent crude surged 4.6% to $95.54 as Iran’s Revolutionary Guards claimed a strike on a US radar installation in Bahrain and Houthi forces attacked Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline and Yanbu oil facility, while WTI rose 3.4% to $88.18 — both crude benchmarks now at their highest levels in over a year; gold fell 2.6% to $3,891 — a 13-month low — as the 10-year at 5.14% overwhelmed safe-haven demand on a day that provided ample reason to seek safety.

Friday’s session is a 5% yield story: the 10-year crossing 5.14% is the single variable that explains the Russell —2.6%, gold’s 13-month low, Bitcoin’s 200-day break, and the divergence between large-cap (up) and small-cap (down) performance. At 5%, rate-sensitive assets pay the full price of the Fed’s next move.

The Anchor · The 5% Yield Threshold — When Rate Pressure Becomes Structural Market Stress

The 10-year yield at 5.14% is not just a number on a chart. It is the benchmark rate against which every other asset class prices its risk and return. At 5.14%, the financial arithmetic changes — and Friday’s market session is the clearest single-day illustration of exactly how. October PPI told two different stories in one report. The monthly story — wholesale prices up 0.3%, below the 0.4% consensus — gave the equity market what it wanted: a suggestion that inflation’s monthly trajectory is bending. Stocks gapped up at the open. The S&P briefly touched 7,631 in early trading. The Dow was up over 1% an hour into the session. The annual story — wholesale prices up 6.5% year-over-year, the fastest pace since November 2022 — changed the bond market’s assessment. The 10-year yield, which had been at 4.99% on Thursday, moved through 5.00% and reached 5.14% by midday. That yield move is not a symbolic event. It is a financial stress transmission mechanism with direct, measurable effects: Russell 2000 small caps — which carry more bank debt, less pricing power, and more domestic-only revenue than large caps — fell 2.6% from their open level as the market repriced their cost-of-capital exposure. Bitcoin broke its 200-day moving average and triggered $1.47 billion in liquidations. Gold fell to a 13-month low of $3,891, with Treasuries at 5.14% capturing the safety capital that would otherwise flow to precious metals. The S&P and Dow held their gains because large-cap indices are dominated by mega-cap technology and multinational industrials with international revenue bases that partially offset the domestic rate exposure. But the Russell —2.6% and the 10Y at 5.14% are more informative about where financial stress is arriving than the S&P ▲0.8% surface number.

October PPI delivered a bifurcated signal Friday: the monthly rate at 0.3% came in below the 0.4% consensus, triggering a gap-up open that pushed the S&P 500 to an intraday high above 7,631 and the Dow above 1% gains early in the session, before the year-over-year rate at 6.5% — the highest since November 2022 — changed the bond market’s assessment and drove the 10-year yield to 5.14%; the final session closes — S&P 500 7,592 (▲0.8%), Dow 52,297 (▲0.8%), Nasdaq 25,366 (▼0.1%), Russell 2000 2,764 (▼2.6%) — reflect the composition of the PPI impact: large-cap and diversified indices retained the monthly-beat gains while rate-sensitive small caps were sold on the YoY concern and the resulting 10-year move; nine of eleven S&P sectors ended positive, with energy and defensives leading and technology lagging as Nvidia extended its post-CEO decline.

The PPI divergence between monthly and annual is the reporting dynamic that shapes December FOMC expectations: a cooling monthly print suggests inflation’s trajectory is not accelerating, which is good for financial conditions; a 6.5% annual rate means the absolute level of wholesale inflation is still the highest in four years, which is what the Fed is targeting. The market’s response — gap up on the monthly, yield move on the annual — is the correct two-part read of a report that is simultaneously better and worse than the same number read in isolation.

Brent at $95.54 is two days of physical disruption, not a risk premium. Thursday’s tanker fires established that the Hormuz mine threat is real and active. Friday’s IRGC Bahrain claim and Houthi Saudi attacks established that the conflict is widening geographically from the strait to the Arabian Peninsula’s land-based energy infrastructure. For every oil importer — Europe, Japan, South Korea, India — the two-day Brent surge from $91.32 to $95.54 is an energy cost escalation that feeds directly into the inflation picture the Fed is already watching from the PPI report released the same day.

Gold fell 2.6% to $3,891 on Friday — its lowest level in 13 months — in a remarkable failure of safe-haven demand on a day that included an IRGC Bahrain radar strike claim, Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabian oil infrastructure, Bitcoin’s 200-day moving average breach, and a broad equity selloff in rate-sensitive sectors; gold’s failure to rally despite acute geopolitical and financial market stress is the clearest possible illustration of what the 10-year at 5.14% does to safe-haven capital flows: at 5.14%, US Treasuries are simultaneously safe, liquid, and generating 5% annual income — every dollar of flight-to-safety capital that enters Treasuries is a dollar that does not enter gold; the Kitco morning report noted that gold was rebounding from an intraday low as PPI’s monthly beat offered some relief, but the 10-year yield’s move to 5.14% overwhelmed any recovery.

Gold at $3,891 on a day with Iranian drones targeting Bahrain, Houthi missiles targeting Saudi pipelines, and $1.47 billion in crypto liquidations is the definitive empirical test of the high-yield safe-haven competition: Treasuries won. At 5.14%, the government bond market absorbs every crisis premium that gold would otherwise capture, because it offers both safety and income. Gold’s safe-haven function reemerges when real rates turn negative — when inflation exceeds Treasury yields. At 5.14% yields against 6.5% YoY PPI, real rates are marginally negative, but not enough to shift the safe-haven preference from Treasuries to gold at today’s price levels.

The Russell 2000’s -2.6% session on a day that S&P gained 0.8% is the market’s most direct signal about where financial stress is concentrated in the current rate environment. It is not in the mega-cap AI names, whose earnings growth can outpace rising cost of capital. It is in the 2,000 smaller companies that make up the domestic-oriented economic base — the ones whose borrowing costs go up immediately when the 10-year rises, whose profit margins compress first when input costs rise, and whose customers are most sensitive to the disposable-income effects of inflation at 6.5% year-over-year. That is the part of the economy the December FOMC hike will hit hardest.
