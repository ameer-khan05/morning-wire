Tuesday’s market has two simultaneous stories: the chip retracement — proportionate, fast, and predictable after a governance‑driven 31% rally — and the Hormuz commodity shock, which has nothing to do with Nvidia and everything to do with a geopolitical escalation that sent the Brent–WTI spread to its widest level in months.

The Anchor · The Proportionate Retracement — What It Means That Nvidia Fell 9% the Day After Rising 31%, and That the Nasdaq’s Retracement Was Proportionate to a Governance Event

Nvidia rose 31% on Monday. It fell 9% on Tuesday. The Nasdaq gained 6.51% and gave back 1.57%. These are not contradictory market signals — they are the same signal in two directions. The proportionality of Tuesday’s retracement is the most important piece of market information in the two-day sequence. A governance-driven rally — one triggered by a CEO reinstatement rather than a demand thesis change — should produce a partial retracement when investors digest the precise size of the governance premium they paid. Monday’s 31% gain was the market paying back the governance discount that accumulated during Huang’s 11-day absence. Tuesday’s 9% decline is the market asking: how much of that 31% was a genuine demand re-rating versus a governance catch-up? The answer appears to be: approximately 9 percentage points were catch‑up beyond the fair governance premium. That is a very specific judgment, and it happened in one trading session. What the market did not retrace: the Dow held its green, with defensive sectors outperforming technology. That sector rotation says the market is not simply reversing Monday — it is distinguishing between the AI governance story (reversing) and the broader economic picture (not reversing). The Hormuz crisis runs entirely independently: a 4.60% Brent surge on a day when tech is selling off is the commodity market pricing a supply disruption that has nothing to do with Nvidia. Both stories are real on Tuesday. The market is pricing both simultaneously, not choosing between them.

The day’s sector rotation — tech down, defensives up — is the market fine-tuning what it thinks Monday’s governance event was worth. A governance premium, not a demand premium, is what the board’s reinstatement of Huang should have been worth. Tuesday’s retracement says the market initially overpaid for it on Monday and is now marking it back toward the right size. The Dow’s green close confirms this is not a broad risk‑off day: it is a chip-specific rerating, not a macro event.

Brent crude surged 4.60% to $90.64 — its largest single-day gain in months — after Iran’s Revolutionary Guard Corps Navy said three to four oil tankers caught fire after striking naval mines in the Strait of Hormuz, with Iran vowing the waterway would remain closed until seven conditions are met; CNBC reported that oil prices were holding near three-month lows as rising supply had been offsetting Hormuz threats, but the tanker mine attacks tipped the balance sharply toward supply disruption premium, with Al Jazeera reporting Hormuz oil traffic is already down 40% from normal levels; WTI crude eased only 0.29% to $79.94 — a divergence from Brent’s 4.60% surge that reflects Brent’s direct exposure to Middle East crude transit while WTI prices US domestic production less affected by Hormuz logistics; and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced fresh Iran sanctions Tuesday in the administration’s direct response, with Benzinga noting the sanctions announcement had contributed to futures declines before markets opened.

The Brent–WTI spread widening is the oil market’s precise expression of where the Hormuz risk falls: on internationally-traded crude that transits the strait, not on US domestic production. A $10.70 Brent–WTI spread — with Brent at $90.64 and WTI at $79.94 — reflects the market’s assessment of the geopolitical premium that now attaches to non-US oil supply. If Iran sustains the mine campaign, that spread stays wide. If the mines are cleared or Bessent’s sanctions produce a negotiated opening, it narrows.
