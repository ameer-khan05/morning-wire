A Russell advance of this magnitude entering a Jobs Report is historically consistent with small-cap positioning for a soft labor market print — the rate-sensitive segment of the market is front-running the rate-cut scenario that a soft Jobs Report would confirm.

WTI crude's 6% collapse to $75.32 on Tuesday — the largest single-day oil decline since the US-Iran conflict began on February 28 — is the session's most consequential market event because it changes the energy channel's relationship to FOMC policy: at $75 WTI, energy is no longer contributing positively to CPI inflation and is instead likely to contribute negatively, reversing the argument that sustained high energy prices require the FOMC to maintain higher-for-longer rates to contain energy-driven inflation; this shift does not require Friday's Jobs Report to validate — it changes the inflation-path arithmetic independently of labor market data. The Hormuz deal catalysts were specific: Trump's "possible today" comment, Bessent's "may come this week" confirmation, and CNBC's Wednesday morning reporting that the US has signaled the strait is "free and open" — three escalating statements in 18 hours that markets interpreted as a preliminary arrangement already in effect.

Gold's ▲1.01% move to $4,075.26 alongside the 6% oil collapse is an analytically important divergence from the co-movement pattern that characterized the Iran conflict's early sessions (when both oil and gold rose together on geopolitical risk). The divergence — gold up, oil sharply down — is consistent with a dollar-weakening dynamic: as Hormuz reopening becomes priced as a working assumption, the safe-haven demand that elevated the dollar during the conflict's peak uncertainty periods fades, weakening the dollar and supporting gold in dollar terms even as oil falls on the direct supply-expectation repricing. Gold at $4,075 is not a risk-off signal in this context — it is a dollar-signal embedded in a risk-on session.

Quick hits

  • The Dow's two-day, 1,814-point advance (from 53,178 on Monday to 54,085 on Tuesday, then from 54,085 to Wednesday's implied continuation) is worth contextualizing against what the Dow measures. The Dow Jones Industrial Average is a price-weighted index of 30 large US companies selected to represent the overall economy — it includes industrials (Caterpillar, 3M), financials (Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan), consumer (McDonald's, Walmart), healthcare (UnitedHealth, Johnson & Johnson), and technology (Apple, Microsoft, Salesforce). A broad Dow advance of this magnitude is typically more meaningful than a tech-concentrated Nasdaq advance for reading the underlying economy, because the Dow's sectoral breadth means that the advance is not driven by a single sector rerating. Tuesday's Dow ▲1.72% alongside a Nasdaq ▲2.58% confirms that the session's drivers — oil-channel rate repricing (benefits Dow's rate-sensitive industrials and financials) and AI earnings (benefits Dow's tech components) — were simultaneously lifting both indices rather than the tech sector pulling the overall market.
  • The gold-and-oil divergence deserves specific attention because it signals something about the Hormuz deal's market interpretation that is not visible in the equity or bond numbers alone. During the conflict's most acute risk-on/risk-off sessions, gold and oil moved together: when geopolitical risk was elevated (threat of renewed strikes, diplomatic breakdowns), both oil and gold rose as safe-haven assets and supply-risk premia priced simultaneously. Tuesday's session inverted that relationship — oil fell 6% while gold rose 1%. The inversion tells you that the market's interpretation of the Hormuz deal signal is not merely "reduced risk" (which would cause gold to fall alongside oil) but specifically "supply shock ending + dollar weakening," which is the combination that causes oil to fall on supply expectations and gold to rise as the safe-haven dollar premium exits. This is a more bullish gold signal than a simple risk-off/risk-on rotation would produce, and it is consistent with a market pricing the end of a dollar-strengthening geopolitical episode rather than the beginning of a new risk-off phase.
