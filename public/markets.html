All four major US equity indices snapped a two-session losing streak Thursday as the president's characterization of Wednesday's airstrikes against Iran as "short-lived" in scope provided a geopolitical de-escalation signal that pulled the 10-year Treasury yield back to 4.75% from its 4.80% crisis high and restored risk appetite across asset classes — the S&P 500 gained 0.57% to 7,648.82, the Nasdaq climbed 0.64% to 25,998.68, the Dow rose 0.80% to 52,923.50, and the Russell 2000 jumped 1.13% to 2,953.24. Nvidia surged 3.35%, Meta gained 2.38%, and Berkshire added 1.10% as the Salesforce–Anthropic Claudeforce announcement combined with the broader risk-on sentiment to push AI-adjacent names significantly higher in Thursday's session. Gold slid to $4,527.63 (▼0.45%) as the safe-haven bid that Wednesday's Iran escalation had revived partially unwound on the de-escalation signal. WTI held above $90 at $90.80 (▲0.80%) and Brent remained near its crisis high at $95.52 (▲0.73%), with the oil market's resilience suggesting the energy market is pricing the Hormuz war's duration rather than reacting to the "short-lived" framing on the same time horizon as equities.

Thursday's session exposes a divergence that will define the next 7 trading days: equities are pricing the Iran "short-lived" signal as genuine de-escalation, while oil is pricing the Hormuz crisis as structurally ongoing. The two markets can't both be right, and August CPI on September 10 — seven days from Thursday — will determine which narrative the rate market endorses heading into the FOMC decision on September 16.

The Anchor · The Equity–Oil Divergence — When Two Markets Price the Same War Differently

The 10-year Treasury yield falling from 4.80% to 4.75% on Thursday is not just the bond market pricing lower inflation expectations — it is the bond market interpreting the president's "short-lived" statement as a meaningful reduction in the war-risk premium that has been embedded in yields since Wednesday's escalation. Equities followed: when the 10-year falls, rate-sensitive growth stocks get incremental relief, and the Nasdaq's 0.64% gain reflects both the macro benefit of lower yields and the sector-specific benefit of the Claudeforce announcement driving AI-adjacent names. Gold's 0.45% decline is the clean safe-haven reversal — the war-severity premium that drove gold up 1.04% on Wednesday partially unwound as the presidential framing suggested the escalation might be contained. All three of those moves — yields down, equities up, gold down — tell the same story: the market believes, at least on Thursday, that the Iran confrontation is cooling. Oil's refusal to tell the same story is the session's most important contrary signal. WTI at $90.80 (▲0.80%) and Brent at $95.52 (▲0.73%) are not moving in the direction that a genuine "short-lived" de-escalation would produce. The energy market is pricing Hormuz supply disruption as structural — not as a spike that the president's framing can reverse. On Day 65 of the Hormuz crisis, with 64 days of confirmed supply disruption behind it and two rounds of direct US-Iran military exchanges in three days, the oil market's structural pricing may simply reflect a longer time horizon than equities can easily price in a single session. The gap between the equity narrative (de-escalation) and the oil narrative (structural disruption) will be resolved by facts, not by framing. If Thursday's "short-lived" characterization holds — no further US strikes, Iranian retaliation limited, diplomatic engagement begins — then oil will follow equities lower. If the strike campaign continues through the weekend or next week, equities will follow oil's structural assessment. The August CPI on September 10 is the first scheduled data point that makes this divergence consequential for the rate path: if Brent at $95 has already fed through to August CPI in a way that forces a September rate hike regardless of diplomatic framing, the equity market's Thursday narrative will be challenged. Until September 10, both narratives remain live. The Russia-Iran C430L missile disclosure adds a dimension the equity market has not yet fully priced: the weapons technology underpinning Iran's military capability in the Hormuz war was developed with direct Russian engineering support. That changes the diplomatic calculation for Washington — de-escalation with Iran now requires navigating Russian technology transfer simultaneously — and makes a quick "short-lived" resolution structurally harder than a purely bilateral US-Iran dynamic would be.

All four major US equity indices advanced Thursday, snapping a two-session losing streak, as the president's characterization of Wednesday's Iran airstrikes as "short-lived" in scope combined with a pullback in the 10-year Treasury yield to 4.75% — from Wednesday's 4.80% crisis high — to provide both the geopolitical and rate-environment relief that rate-sensitive technology stocks needed after two days of pressure. The S&P 500 added 0.57% to 7,648.82, the Nasdaq climbed 0.64% to 25,998.68, the Dow rose 0.80% to 52,923.50, and the Russell 2000 surged 1.13% to 2,953.24. Nvidia led Thursday's session at ▲3.35%, with Meta gaining 2.38% and Berkshire adding 1.10% as the Claudeforce announcement and broader AI sentiment drove AI-adjacent names significantly higher.

The Russell 2000's 1.13% gain — the strongest of the four major indices — reflects small caps' heightened sensitivity to the domestic rate environment: when the 10-year eases 5 basis points, small companies with floating-rate debt and domestic revenue exposure benefit proportionally more than large-cap multinationals with diverse funding structures. The Nasdaq's 0.64% gain is more modest than the Dow's 0.80% in percentage terms, reflecting that rate-sensitive technology stocks partially reversed Wednesday's session but did not fully recover it — consistent with the 10-year easing only 5 basis points of the prior day's 8-basis-point increase. The session's gains are Iran-signal-dependent: if the "short-lived" framing proves inaccurate and the strike campaign resumes, Wednesday's dynamics will re-emerge.

WTI held above $90 at $90.80 (▲0.80%) and Brent remained near its crisis high at $95.52 (▲0.73%) on Hormuz Day 65, with the energy market declining to price Thursday's Iran "short-lived" framing as a meaningful supply-risk reduction — the most significant divergence in Thursday's market session. The oil market is pricing the Hormuz supply disruption as structural: 65 days of confirmed disruption, two rounds of direct US-Iran military exchanges in three days, and no visible diplomatic channel capable of restoring pre-crisis Strait throughput on a near-term timeline. Presidential framing does not change those structural facts, and the energy market's pricing reflects that distinction.

Gold slid to $4,527.63 (▼0.45%) as the safe-haven bid that Wednesday's Iran escalation had revived partially unwound on the de-escalation signal — a partial reversal of Wednesday's 1.04% gain, but not a full normalization. Gold remains above the level it occupied before Wednesday's wedding strike and Iranian retaliation — indicating that some residual war-severity premium persists even as the immediate geopolitical fear has eased. The divergence between equity markets (pricing the "short-lived" signal as genuine de-escalation) and oil markets (pricing structural Hormuz disruption) is the key tension heading into August CPI on September 10: if Brent stays near $95 through that release, the oil-driven inflation channel into CPI will dominate the rate-path calculation regardless of whether the equity market's diplomatic optimism proves correct.

Thursday's market session tells two stories simultaneously, and only one of them can be right. Story one: equity markets, bond yields, and gold are all pricing the presidential "short-lived" framing as genuine de-escalation — equities gain, yields ease 5bp, gold slides as safe-haven unwinds. Story two: oil markets are pricing the Hormuz crisis as structurally ongoing — Brent stays near $95, WTI holds above $90, no significant move lower despite the same Iran signal. These two stories reflect different time horizons. Equity markets react to sentiment and signals on a session-by-session basis; oil markets price the physical supply consequences of geopolitical events over weeks and months. The energy market's message is that 65 days of Hormuz supply disruption and two rounds of direct US-Iran military exchanges have established a structural supply constraint that a presidential statement does not reverse. The equity market's message is that the immediate tail risk of further severe escalation has been reduced by the framing. Both can be simultaneously true for a few days. The August CPI report on September 10 is where the divergence becomes consequential: if Brent's persistence near $95 has already fed through to August CPI readings in a way that makes a September rate hike effectively unavoidable, the equity market's diplomatic optimism will be tested against hard inflation data. That test is seven days away.
