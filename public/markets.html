October’s consumer price index came in hotter than expected for a third consecutive month — core CPI rising 0.3% month-over-month, topping estimates again, with overall prices up 0.4% — sending the S&P 500 down 0.8% to 7,477, the Nasdaq down 1.1% to 25,130, the Dow down 0.7% to 51,929, and the Russell 2000 down 0.9% to 2,804, with all 11 GICS sectors declining as Federal Reserve December rate-hike odds surged to 87.3% per CME FedWatch and Governor Christopher Waller said a hot inflation print could push him to advocate for a 50-basis-point increase; the 10-year Treasury yield jumped 11 basis points to 4.97%; oil extended Monday’s crash with WTI falling to $86.00 and Brent to $87.50 as Abu Dhabi day 1 talks produced no ceasefire, though oil’s continued decline suggests the market is pricing eventual ceasefire progress rather than demanding an immediate announcement; gold fell 0.8% to $4,076 as rate-hike fears weighed on the safe-haven metal.

Tuesday’s markets session is a CPI-correction story. Monday priced two optimistic scenarios simultaneously: inflation cooperating and peace talks succeeding. The CPI corrected the first assumption. At 0.3% core for three consecutive months, inflation is not cooperating, and the Fed is not done. The Abu Dhabi talks remain open and oil’s continued decline shows the market has not abandoned the peace scenario — but the CPI data arrived first and priced the inflation reality before the geopolitical story could develop.

The Anchor · Core CPI +0.3% Third Month — The Inflation Trend That Changes the December Calculus

Three consecutive months of core CPI coming in at or above 0.3% month-over-month is not an anomaly — it is a trend. And a trend that persists through October — the last inflation reading before the December 8–9 FOMC meeting — puts the Fed in a position where not raising rates requires an explicit justification rather than a presumption. The October CPI report shows core prices up 0.3% month-over-month — topping estimates for the third straight month — and overall prices up 0.4%, with grocery prices posting their biggest monthly gain since April and electricity continuing its run of substantial increases. Governor Christopher Waller said a hot inflation report could push him toward advocating for a 50-basis-point December hike, up from the 25bps that the market had been pricing as the base case. CME FedWatch put 25bps at 87.3% and 50bps at 12.7%, a significant move from the 77% 25bps probability at the start of the week. The 10-year Treasury yield jumped 11 basis points to 4.97% — a material single-session move that reflects the bond market pricing the Fed more aggressively into the December meeting. The equity market’s 0.8%–1.1% decline is the equities expression of the same repricing: higher rates reduce the present value of future earnings, and three straight hot CPI months make higher rates more certain. Monday’s rally was partly pricing inflation cooperation that Tuesday’s data denied.

October’s Bureau of Labor Statistics consumer price index report showed core CPI — excluding food and energy — rising 0.3% month-over-month, topping estimates for a third consecutive month and showing no sign of the deceleration that the Fed would need to justify pausing in December; overall CPI rose 0.4%, with grocery prices posting their biggest monthly gain since April and electricity adding another substantial increase — two line items with direct consumer impact that reinforce the political urgency of the inflation picture alongside the financial one; the S&P 500 reversed Monday’s 2.2% gain, falling 0.8% to 7,477 as all 11 GICS sectors declined, with the Nasdaq leading the retreat at –1.1% to 25,130 and the Dow falling 0.7% to 51,929; the index moves were broadly proportional to sectors’ sensitivity to interest rate expectations, with high-multiple growth and technology most affected.

Three consecutive months of core CPI at or above 0.3% month-over-month is a 3.6% annualized pace — above the Fed’s 2% target by a margin that cannot be dismissed as noise. The October report is the last inflation reading before the December 8–9 FOMC meeting, which means the Fed is making its December decision against a backdrop where inflation is running hot, labor market data has been mixed, and geopolitical uncertainty has not resolved. The base case is now a hike.

CME FedWatch put the probability of a 25-basis-point Federal Reserve rate hike at the December 8–9 FOMC meeting at 87.3% on Tuesday — up from 84% Monday and 77% a week ago — with 12.7% now priced for a 50-basis-point hike, up from 0% last week; Fed Governor Christopher Waller told reporters that a hot inflation print could push him toward advocating for the larger move, a statement that the market interpreted as a credible signal that the December decision remains live at the larger increment even if 25bps is the consensus; the 10-year Treasury yield jumped 11 basis points to 4.97% on the CPI data — a material single-session bond market move that reflects the market repricing the Fed’s December trajectory; a December 25bps hike would bring the target federal funds rate to 4%–4.25%, and a 50bps hike would bring it to 4.25%–4.5%.

The 10-year yield at 4.97% — 11 basis points above Monday’s close — is the bond market’s single-session verdict on what a third consecutive hot CPI month means for December. If it closes above 5.00% this week, it establishes a new range that equities will have to discount. The gap between where the 10-year is now and where it needs to be to slow equity repricing is small, and the next CPI-equivalent catalyst — November inflation data — does not come until December 10, one day before the Fed’s December 11 decision.

Oil falling on day 1 of Abu Dhabi talks with no ceasefire is the market telling two stories at once: it believes the talks will eventually succeed (hence continued oil pressure) and it is not willing to hold elevated prices on the day 1 no-announcement outcome. That is a bullish interpretation of the geopolitical picture — peace priced before it is confirmed — and it sets up a sharp reversal if day 2 produces nothing on Wednesday.

Gold fell 0.8% to $4,076 on Tuesday as rate-hike fears weighed on the non-yielding safe-haven metal — a typical dynamic when real interest rate expectations rise sharply, since gold competes with Treasuries as a safe-haven asset and higher rates make Treasuries more attractive relative to gold; all 11 GICS sectors declined on Tuesday, with energy leading the decline as WTI fell 2.5% and oil-related equities followed, and technology also among the weaker sectors as rising rates hit high-multiple growth stocks harder than the broader market; the broad-based decline contrasts with Monday’s broad-based rally — both sessions featured all-11-sectors moves, but in opposite directions — and the symmetry of the reversal reinforces the reading that Monday was pricing optimistic scenarios that Tuesday partially corrected on the CPI data alone, with the geopolitical and peace process variables still unresolved.

Gold down 0.8% on a day when equities are falling 0.8%–1.1% is the rate-hike mechanism at work: when real rates rise sharply, gold loses its relative safe-haven advantage over Treasuries. The 10-year at 4.97% is the bond market pricing December aggressively, and gold is responding to that rate level rather than to the geopolitical de-escalation signal that was actually gold-supportive on Monday. Rate expectations and geopolitics are pulling in different directions, and Tuesday, rates won.
