The Anchor · 85.5% and Monday — What the FOMC Week Opens With

The 16-point jump in CME FedWatch's FOMC rate-hike probability over Saturday and Sunday — from 69.4% at Friday's close to 85.5% on Sunday — means Monday's session opens with the most certain hike pricing of this tightening cycle heading into the last full trading day before the decision. At 85.5%, the 25 basis point hike for Tuesday is not merely the base case; it is the near-consensus expectation. The equity market closed Friday relieved by an in-line CPI. The oil market closed at $107.40 on the Houthi Mokha foothold. The rate market has since added 16 points of hike probability. Monday's session will have to reconcile all three of those facts simultaneously. The Friday CPI relief sent equities up 1% on average; the subsequent 16-point jump in hike probability would, under normal conditions, put some of that relief under pressure. But the relationship between rate-hike probability and equity performance has changed since Kevin Warsh took over the Fed: markets have largely stopped treating individual hike increments as negative events and have begun trading the statement signal more directly. A 25bp hike at 85.5% probability is already so fully priced that announcing it on Tuesday will produce minimal immediate equity reaction; the reaction will come from the statement. A pause signal — "meeting by meeting, data dependent" without forward guidance — would allow the Q4 repricing that Friday's CPI relief had been anticipating: rate cuts visible in early 2027, equity multiple expansion possible, crypto opportunity cost declining. A continuation signal — "additional increases may be warranted" — would confirm that the cycle has more hikes ahead, keeping the opportunity cost of equities high relative to Treasuries at 4.89% and non-yielding crypto at $77,243. Japan's $88 billion foreign securities sale to fund its record yen intervention adds a Treasury supply dimension to the Monday context: Japan's Finance Ministry data shows Tokyo sold approximately the equivalent of $88 billion in foreign holdings through August 26 in a jointly confirmed US-Japan operation. The joint confirmation — unusual for currency intervention — signals a coordinated Allied approach to the yen depreciation that has been threatening Japan's purchasing power amid the same oil and rates environment that is weighing on every other major economy. The 10-year Treasury's hold at 4.89% through the weekend, against Japan's forced Treasury sales and an 85.5% hike probability, suggests the Treasury market has largely absorbed the supply. Monday's session will clarify whether that absorption holds into the FOMC week.

CME FedWatch's probability of a 25 basis point rate hike at Tuesday's FOMC meeting has surged to 85.5% as of Sunday — up from 69.4% Saturday and approximately 65% at Friday's close — a 16-point jump over the weekend that marks the highest hike probability reading of this tightening cycle and makes the 25bp decision as close to consensus as any unannounced FOMC outcome gets. The data case for hiking has strengthened through the weekend without any new releases: CPI at 3.4% annually, a hot PPI print Thursday, 162,000 August payrolls beating expectations, $107.40 Brent maintaining elevated energy costs, and Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole commitment to fighting inflation all remain on the table. The hold argument — that current data doesn't justify tightening — has become structurally untenable at 85.5% probability.

With the hike itself fully priced at 85.5%, Monday's session and Tuesday's announcement are a two-stage event: Monday prices the statement distribution (pause versus continuation), Tuesday announces the hike and provides the statement that resolves that distribution. The statement's language is the remaining uncertainty. A pause signal — "meeting by meeting, data dependent" without forward guidance — would allow the market to begin pricing a Q4-into-2027 rate cut trajectory, which would be structurally positive for equities and crypto. A continuation signal — "additional increases may be warranted" — keeps the elevated yield environment as the base case and extends the pressure on non-yielding assets. At 4.89% on the 10-year Treasury, the opportunity cost of equities and crypto versus risk-free Treasuries is already significant; a continuation signal would maintain that spread through year-end. Monday's session is the last full pricing window before Tuesday resolves it.

The dual chokepoint threat that elevated Brent to $107 on Friday remains intact entering Monday's session.

The structural oil premium from dual-chokepoint risk is not priced on a daily news cycle — it is priced on the expectation that both threats persist until a specific military or diplomatic event resolves them. The Mokha counter-offensive that would reduce the Bab el-Mandab threat has not materialized through the weekend. The Iran ceasefire that would reduce the Hormuz threat has not materialized through the BRICS summit. Monday's session therefore opens with Brent's structural baseline at $107, a FOMC rate decision in three days that makes tight money the near-term macro environment, and an equity market that recovered Friday's CPI relief on the same day Brent surged to $107. Japan's Finance Ministry data revealed that Tokyo sold approximately $87.8 billion in foreign securities — including US Treasuries — as part of a record ¥15.4 trillion ($98.6 billion) yen intervention operation through August 26, in what both Japan and the United States confirmed as a rare joint currency intervention to prop up the yen against its ongoing depreciation. OMFIF described the US involvement as providing "unusual support," and Al Jazeera reported the joint confirmation of the operation — the first confirmed coordinated yen intervention since the post-2008 era. The 10-year Treasury yield holds at 4.89% through the weekend, suggesting the market has absorbed Japan's Treasury supply without a material rate spike.

The US-Japan joint currency intervention is a significant structural development for the Treasury market: when Japan sells US Treasuries at scale to fund yen support, it adds supply to the market at a moment when the Fed is also reducing its balance sheet. The fact that 10-year yields held at 4.89% through a weekend following Japan's $88 billion sell-down suggests either that the market absorbed the supply efficiently, or that the joint US participation included a commitment on the pace of US balance-sheet reduction that partially offset the Japanese sales. The confirmation of US Treasury involvement is also a signal about Allied economic coordination: Washington accepted supply-side pressure on its own bond market to help a key strategic partner defend its currency against the rate-and-energy-cost pressure that is affecting every major developed economy simultaneously. Japan's ¥15.4 trillion intervention is the record because the yen depreciation pressure is the largest it has faced since the 1990s — driven by the same FOMC tightening that is setting 85.5% hike odds on CME FedWatch for Tuesday's decision.
