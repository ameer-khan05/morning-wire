Thursday’s markets session is two stories colliding in the same session: the Nvidia CEO resignation driving a tech-specific selloff just as bond markets reopened with rate pressure intact, and the Hormuz tanker fires driving the largest Brent premium over WTI in months. Neither story is resolved by Friday.

The Anchor · Nvidia’s Plunge — When the Most Important Stock in AI Loses Its CEO

Nvidia is not just a stock in the S&P 500. It is the most important hardware company in the AI era, the largest external customer for TSMC’s advanced node capacity, and the company whose GPU architecture determines what AI is computationally possible to build today. When it loses its founding CEO to a spy scandal, the market reprices not just Nvidia’s management risk but its role in the entire AI infrastructure thesis. Nvidia’s shares fell sharply Thursday — a double-digit percentage decline — on a combination of the leadership shock, the national security investigation, and the question of what Alice Huang may have learned through Jensen Huang about Nvidia’s AI hardware export strategy and China-restricted GPU roadmaps. CFO Colette Kress as interim CEO is a signal of operational stability, not strategic vision — and the market reprices strategic vision separately from operational continuity. The Nasdaq — heavily weighted toward AI and semiconductor stocks — fell 0.5%. The S&P 500 — more diversified — fell 0.3%. The Dow — with higher industrial and financial weight — fell 0.3% as well. The Russell 2000 rose 0.9%, confirming that small-cap stocks with minimal AI hardware exposure are genuinely benefiting from a rotation away from mega-cap AI names. The bond market’s simultaneous reopening at 4.99% added the second headwind: without Wednesday’s holiday closure holding yields static, the rate pressure from Tuesday’s CPI data resumed. Thursday’s session combines a sector-specific shock with the macro pressure that was always there, waiting for the holiday to end.

Nvidia shares fell sharply — in double-digit percentage territory — on Thursday after the company’s board accepted CEO Jensen Huang’s resignation following the disclosure of his affair with an alleged Chinese spy and a concurrent relationship with an Nvidia vice president, with the selloff carrying the Nasdaq 0.5% lower to 25,404 and the S&P 500 0.3% lower to 7,531 — reversing most of Wednesday’s AI-earnings-driven rebound in a single session; the Dow fell a more modest 0.3% to 51,878 while the Russell 2000 rose 0.9% to 2,837, confirming a rotation out of large-cap AI hardware names into small-cap sectors with less Nvidia exposure — the inverse of Wednesday’s AI-led concentration; Nvidia’s selloff is a management-event discount, a national-security-risk premium, and a strategic-uncertainty repricing simultaneously: Colette Kress’s appointment as interim CEO stabilizes the operational picture but does not address the two questions the market is repricing — what did Alice Huang learn, and who leads Nvidia’s long-term AI hardware vision?

Wednesday’s AI earnings rebound (CoreWeave +10.7%, Nvidia +2.9%) and Thursday’s Nvidia leadership shock are bookends of the same AI infrastructure story told in two sessions: on Wednesday, the demand thesis is validated by earnings; on Thursday, the supply-chain security of that same infrastructure is questioned at the CEO level. The market spent Wednesday buying AI conviction and Thursday revising it. The net of two sessions is a Nasdaq approximately flat from Monday’s open — a lot of movement for no net progress.

The US bond market reopened on Thursday after Wednesday’s Veterans Day closure, with the 10-year Treasury yield ticking up 2 basis points to 4.99% — moving in the direction that Tuesday’s hot October CPI data had established, with December 25-basis-point Fed rate-hike odds at approximately 87.3% — and removing the structural relief valve that had contributed to Wednesday’s equity rebound; without the bond market’s holiday closure, there would have been no Wednesday equity bounce of the magnitude that occurred — the AI earnings surprise from CoreWeave would have still been positive, but rate repricing throughout the session would have offset more of it; the 10-year at 4.99% is the number to watch approaching 5.0%, a level that the market has historically treated as a psychological threshold with ripple effects through equity valuation multiples and crypto opportunity-cost calculations; the next significant bond market data point is PPI on Thursday afternoon, followed by November CPI on December 10 — the last inflation reading before the December 11 FOMC decision.

The 10-year at 4.99% combined with the Nvidia CEO shock and Hormuz tanker fires is a rare session where both the idiosyncratic risk (Nvidia) and the macro risk (rate repricing) and the geopolitical risk (Brent) all moved against equities simultaneously. Usually at least one of those vectors offers a partial offset. Thursday offered none. The VIX moving sharply higher is the market’s expression that all three moved in the same direction at the same time.

Brent crude surged 3.7% to $91.32 on Thursday — its largest single-day gain in months — after Iran reported that two oil tankers caught fire after striking naval mines in the Strait of Hormuz, with Iranian forces attributing the fires to vessels entering a prohibited zone while WTI crude simultaneously fell 1.5% to $85.31 — widening the Brent-WTI spread to $6.01, its highest level this year; the tanker fires are the most significant physical disruption to Hormuz shipping since the conflict began, arriving in a week when monitoring firms already counted 37 ship attacks in and near the strait — the highest monthly total since the war started — and the United States and Iran continue to dispute whether US naval mine-clearing operations have succeeded; the Brent-WTI spread widening reflects the geographic specificity of Hormuz disruption: Brent prices seaborne crude that transits the strait, while WTI prices US domestic production that does not, and the spread is a direct measure of how much of a Hormuz premium markets are pricing into globally traded oil.

Brent at $91.32 represents a qualitative shift in the oil market’s Iran risk premium: a week ago the market was pricing Iran as a risk management exercise; now it is pricing it as a realized supply disruption. The tanker fires are physical evidence of what 37 ship attacks in a month produce: eventually the statistical risk converts into actual cargo on fire in the strait. For every oil importer in Europe and Asia, $91.32 Brent is a significant energy cost escalation that feeds directly into inflation projections the Fed is already watching closely.

Gold was roughly flat at $3,994 (▲0.1%) on Thursday — caught between two competing forces that largely offset each other: the safe-haven demand generated by the Hormuz tanker fires and the Nvidia national-security shock, pulling toward gold, and the opportunity cost of holding a non-yielding asset against a 10-year Treasury at 4.99%, pulling away from it; gold’s failure to rally meaningfully on a day that includes a Hormuz supply disruption, a major AI hardware CEO spy scandal, and a broad equity selloff says something specific about the current rate environment: at 4.99%, Treasuries are capturing what would otherwise be safe-haven flows into gold, because they offer both safety and a 5% return that gold cannot match; gold at $3,994 is approximately $115 below its October 28 high of $4,109, and three consecutive days below $4,000 — Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday — have established a temporary ceiling at that round number; the structural bull case for gold — that persistent inflation will ultimately erode the real value of dollar-denominated assets — remains intact, but is not visible in the daily price action while real yields are this elevated.

Gold flat on a day with Hormuz tanker fires and a Nvidia spy scandal is the clearest single-day illustration of what high real rates do to gold’s safe-haven function: they redirect flight-to-safety flows into government bonds, which offer both safety and income at 5%. Gold wins the safe-haven competition when equities are falling and bonds are also yielding poorly. When bonds yield 5%, gold has to clear a higher bar to attract incremental capital from safety-seeking investors.
