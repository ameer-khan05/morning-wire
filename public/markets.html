Quick hits

  • The specific mechanics of the jobs report's rate-policy transmission to equities warrant an explicit map for readers who are accustomed to "good news is bad news" when it comes to labor-market data and Fed policy. The normal "good news is bad news" regime applies when the Fed is fighting inflation: strong jobs → strong wage growth → inflationary pressure → Fed must keep rates high → equity valuations suppressed. This regime applies when rate hikes are still required. The current configuration has shifted: with inflation approaching the Fed's target range and the labor market softening, the Fed's dual mandate (price stability + maximum employment) now has both components pointing in the same direction — inflation down, employment softening — which makes a rate hike harder to justify on either mandate leg. When rate hikes become the minority view, the "good news is bad news" transmission breaks down and weak labor-market data becomes directly positive for equities through the discount-rate channel: lower expected rates → lower discount rate → higher present value of future earnings → higher equity prices. Friday's record S&P close on a −23,000 jobs print is exactly this mechanism operating.
  • The 16-percentage-point single-session move in September rate-hike probability (58%→42%) is a large move for a single data point. Context: before Friday, the 58% hike probability reflected a market that was still uncertain about the inflation trajectory following the Iran conflict's energy-CPI spike; the rate-hike majority view was based on the premise that if energy prices stayed elevated and labor markets stayed tight, the Fed might need to hike to prevent wage-price spiral dynamics. The −23,000 payrolls print removes the "tight labor market" leg of that argument — it is difficult to maintain that the labor market is too tight to support rate cuts when employers are cutting jobs. Whether 42% hike probability accurately prices the September meeting depends entirely on August CPI and PCE data, which arrive before September 16. If inflation data confirms disinflation, hike probability falls further toward 20-25% (near-certain hold or cut). If inflation data is mixed, 42% persists. If inflation data is hot, hike probability returns to majority territory and reverses much of Friday's rally. The jobs report is necessary but not sufficient for the full rate-pivot thesis — it removes the labor-market objection without addressing the inflation question.
