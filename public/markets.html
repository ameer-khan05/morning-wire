Wednesday’s market delivers the week’s clearest macro signal: PCE data at the wrong level on the wrong week — before Thanksgiving, with markets already on edge from a dual energy shock — produced the Nasdaq’s worst single-day performance since October and its worst November on a cumulative basis since 2008.

The Anchor · The PCE Verdict — What It Means That Inflation Data Narrowed the Fed’s December Room on the Same Day the Nasdaq Entered Its Steepest November Since 2008

PCE data does not close the door on December. It narrows the hallway. The Federal Reserve’s preferred inflation gauge — PCE, the Personal Consumption Expenditures price index — came in above consensus expectations Wednesday morning for October. The specific figures matter less than the direction: the Fed needs PCE trending toward its 2% target to justify the rate cut path that equity markets have been pricing since late September. Wednesday’s data says PCE is not trending the right way in October. That does not guarantee a December hold — FOMC members have consistently said they would look at the totality of data — but it moves the probability of a December cut lower and the probability of a hold higher. Markets do not wait for the FOMC meeting to price that new probability; they price it immediately. The Nasdaq fell 3.98% and the S&P fell 2.69% beginning in the first 30 minutes of trading. That is not a delayed reaction to PCE data — it is an immediate re-pricing of the December rate-path probability. The tariff proposals added a second pressure: new tariffs on semiconductor imports raise production costs for the AI infrastructure companies that are the heaviest Nasdaq components. Both shocks hit the same sector — technology — on the same day, compounding the effect. The Dow held positive because defensives don’t care about semiconductor tariffs and benefit from rate stability: utilities and healthcare are indifferent to the chip tariff story and face less earnings-multiple compression from a Fed hold than growth technology does. Wednesday’s sector divergence is not random — it is the precise market expression of which parts of the economy are hurt by a Fed-hold-plus-tariff combination and which are not.

The November-since-2008 comparison is about accumulation, not any single day.

Brent crude rose to $91.89 (▲1.38%) and WTI climbed to $82.14 (▲2.75%) after Houthi forces struck Saudi Arabia’s Ras Tanura oil refinery — 550,000 barrels per day of refining capacity, the kingdom’s largest — with Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree claiming the strikes caused fires, NASA satellite imagery confirming thick black smoke across 600 square kilometres, and The National reporting Saudi air defenses had intercepted four drones at Ras Tanura in the Eastern Province; the Houthis also claimed strikes on Riyadh’s financial district in three phases, extending Wednesday’s attack beyond the refinery alone; and the Ras Tanura attack compounds the Strait of Hormuz mine campaign that sent Brent to $90.64 on Tuesday, with the two-day cumulative effect now a confirmed dual-supply disruption from both Iran (Hormuz mines) and Yemen (Houthi refinery strike) that has pushed Brent above $91 — its highest level in months — with WTI’s faster Wednesday gain (▲2.75% versus Brent’s ▲1.38%) reflecting the market pricing upstream supply disruption more broadly, narrowing the Brent-WTI spread from Tuesday’s $10.70.

The Brent-WTI spread narrowing on Wednesday — from $10.70 to roughly $9.75 — reflects a subtle shift in the energy market’s risk geography. When the shock is exclusively Hormuz transit, Brent is most affected because it prices internationally-traded crude transiting the strait. When a Houthi attack hits a Saudi refinery, the downstream impact includes WTI-priced crude that feeds into the same global supply chain. Wednesday’s WTI outperforming Brent says the market is pricing upstream supply disruption more broadly, not just transit risk.

Gold’s 2.12% decline on a day when equities also fell is the week’s most counterintuitive market signal. Traditional safe-haven logic says: equities down, gold up. Wednesday says: equities down but dollar up (PCE-driven), which means gold down despite equity weakness. The dollar’s strength on inflation data overwhelmed gold’s safe-haven bid. For gold holders expecting the asset to hedge equity-market risk, Wednesday is a reminder that the hedge works best when the dollar is weak or stable — not when inflation data drives a risk-off episode with a dollar bid.
