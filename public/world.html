The Anchor · Larak Island — The Strike That Changed the Hormuz Crisis

For 61 days, the Hormuz crisis operated within a bounded framework: Iran attacked commercial shipping and disrupted Strait throughput; the U.S. protected vessel transit, enforced naval presence, and conducted countermeasures against mines and launch platforms in the Strait; both sides avoided direct territorial engagement. That framework ended Sunday night when U.S. forces struck two Iranian rocket launchers on Larak Island — territory inside Iran — after intelligence confirmed the IRGC was preparing to launch rockets carrying sea mines into the Strait shipping lanes. Larak Island is Iranian sovereign territory. The U.S. strike on it is qualitatively different from every prior U.S. military action in the 61-day crisis. Iran's response — retaliatory strikes on U.S. military positions in Jordan and the Al Minhad Air Base in the UAE — confirms that Iran is treating the Larak strike not as a Strait engagement but as an attack on Iranian territory that requires a territorial response against U.S. infrastructure in the region. The escalation ladder has advanced. The question is where it stops. The U.S. struck a launch platform that was actively preparing a sea-mine operation — a proportionate, preemptive, target-specific military action by conventional military standards. Iran's response targeted U.S. military infrastructure in two countries — Jordan and the UAE — but not U.S. naval assets in the Strait or U.S. territory. Both sides have conducted operations that are escalatory relative to the prior 61-day baseline but that remain short of a full military confrontation. The 48 hours after a direct exchange of this type are critical: if both sides treat Sunday-Monday as a bounded exchange — a warning sent and received — the crisis may de-escalate toward the managed-passage framework that Iran and Oman have been negotiating. If Iran conducts further strikes on U.S. assets or the U.S. conducts follow-on strikes in Iran, the crisis enters a new and significantly more dangerous phase. Brent at $90.69 is pricing the uncertainty, not the outcome.

U.S. forces struck two Iranian rocket launchers on Larak Island late Sunday after intelligence confirmed the IRGC was preparing to launch rockets carrying sea mines into the Strait of Hormuz shipping lanes — the first direct American military strike on Iranian territory in over a month, and the most significant U.S. military action of the 62-day crisis. Iran retaliated Monday morning by striking U.S. military positions in Jordan and targeting the Al Minhad Air Base in the UAE, the largest U.S. military hub in the Gulf region. The exchange elevated the Hormuz crisis from a Strait proxy conflict to a direct territorial military engagement: U.S. forces struck Iranian soil; Iran struck U.S. military infrastructure in two sovereign countries outside Iran.

The military logic of the U.S. strike is preemptive and specific: the Larak Island launchers were actively being prepared for a sea-mine rocket launch that would have posed an immediate threat to commercial shipping in the Strait. The strike removed that threat before it was executed. Iran's response — targeting U.S. military infrastructure in Jordan and the UAE rather than naval assets in the Strait or U.S. territory — suggests a calibrated retaliation designed to impose costs on the U.S. without triggering a U.S. counterstrike against Iranian nuclear or energy infrastructure. Whether both sides treat this as a bounded exchange depends on what happens in the next 48 hours. Brent at $90.69 prices the uncertainty.

President Trump confirmed Monday that the United States has struck an agreement with Venezuela to control more than 65 billion barrels of Venezuelan oil, saying the deal would more than double American oil reserves and substantially lower gasoline prices for Americans amid the Hormuz supply disruption. Venezuela holds the world's second-largest proven oil reserves; the deal, if executed, would represent the most significant energy supply realignment in decades. The International Criminal Court separately issued arrest warrants for both Vladimir Putin and Benjamin Netanyahu — a simultaneous action that thrusts the ICC into an institutional crisis: Western governments that have backed the court's Ukraine work (the basis of the Putin warrant) are also the primary supporters of Israel (the basis of the Netanyahu warrant), positions that are now formally irreconcilable.

The Venezuela oil deal and the ICC warrants are both significant individually; together, they illustrate the degree to which the Hormuz crisis and the multiple concurrent conflicts of 2026 are reshaping geopolitical relationships at a structural level. The Trump administration is signing a major energy deal with a government it previously sanctioned; the ICC is issuing warrants for the leader of a U.S. ally; and the international order's key institutions — the court system, the alliance structures, the energy supply chains — are being reorganized simultaneously under the pressure of multiple conflicts. The details of the Venezuela arrangement — how U.S. control is operationalized, what Venezuela receives in return, what happens to U.S. sanctions — have not been disclosed.

Russian strikes killed approximately 40 people in Ukraine Monday, continuing the sustained aerial campaign against Ukrainian territory that followed three consecutive nights of drone attacks on Kyiv. The strikes target civilian infrastructure as well as military positions, maintaining the pressure campaign that Russia has escalated since late August. In the occupied West Bank, dozens of Israeli settlers entered a Palestinian home in Qusra while residents were inside; Israeli security forces later dispersed the rioters but made no arrests. Prime Minister Netanyahu condemned the settler violence, saying the attackers were "breaking the law" and "harming Israel's international standing" — a public condemnation that came without reported criminal accountability.

The Ukraine and West Bank stories arrive on a day dominated by the Iran escalation and tend to be read against that backdrop — but each has independent significance. Russia's aerial campaign against Ukraine has intensified through August despite the Ryabkov signal last week of conceptual flexibility on negotiations; the military pressure and the diplomatic signal are running on separate tracks, as they did throughout the Hormuz crisis. The West Bank settler violence — the second significant incident this month — occurs as the Netanyahu government faces ICC arrest warrants issued Monday, placing Israeli internal security decisions under renewed international scrutiny. Asian flood-related death tolls continue rising across affected countries, a humanitarian emergency receiving reduced international attention amid the major conflict escalations.
