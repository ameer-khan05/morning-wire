The Anchor · The Summit Without Ukraine — Putin’s Three-Party Proposal Excludes Kyiv From Its Own Settlement, and Trump’s Openness Makes That Exclusion Credible

Putin’s proposal for a Trump-Xi-Putin summit is not a peace initiative — it is a framework for great-power management of a smaller-state conflict, where the outcome is determined by three parties and delivered to the fourth. Ukraine’s exclusion is the proposal’s defining feature, not a logistical detail. The mechanism of the proposal is straightforward: Putin gains a negotiating forum that by design excludes the party most opposed to the territorial concessions he wants. Trump gains a summit with Xi and Putin that frames him as the architect of a historic geopolitical realignment — a framing with significant domestic political value ahead of midterms. Xi gains influence over a European security arrangement in exchange for acting as a mediating host, extending Chinese foreign policy reach into a conflict that has been primarily defined by US-European dynamics. The three parties have different specific interests but share a general interest in a settlement that does not require any of them to make costly concessions — because the costly concessions will be made by Ukraine. Sybiha’s formulation is precise: “A trilateral meeting between the leaders of Russia, China and the U.S. would do only the opposite: encourage Putin to continue this war.” This is not diplomatic language — it is a structural analysis. If Putin can obtain a settlement through a three-party forum that excludes Ukraine’s veto, he has no incentive to make any concession that Ukraine would require. The framework rewards continued military pressure by maintaining the pressure that forces a settlement on the stronger party’s terms. Iran’s deliberate-delay revelation arrives the same day as the summit proposal and illuminates the same underlying logic: both Putin and Tehran are using diplomatic engagement not to move toward resolution but to manage the negotiating environment in their favor while continuing to apply pressure. The Iranian revelation that Tehran has been prolonging talks specifically to weaken Trump ahead of midterms is the most explicit statement of this strategy that has appeared in public reporting — and it changes how every previous Iranian diplomatic gesture must be reread.

Russian President Putin formally proposed a three-way summit with Trump and Xi Jinping to negotiate an end to the Ukraine war — potentially at November’s APEC summit in Shenzhen, China — in a proposal first mentioned in a Putin-Trump phone call on October 9 and confirmed by the Kremlin; Trump said he was open to attending if the meeting covers denuclearization and may also address Ukraine; Ukrainian Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha condemned the framework, warning that “a trilateral meeting between the leaders of Russia, China and the U.S. would do only the opposite: encourage Putin to continue this war.”

The summit proposal’s diplomatic structure is more consequential than its location: a Trump-Xi-Putin meeting on Ukraine’s future that excludes Ukraine is a great-power management framework, not a peace negotiation. Sybiha’s critique identifies the structural flaw precisely — if Putin can obtain a favorable settlement through a three-party forum that does not require Ukraine’s agreement, he has no incentive to offer the concessions Ukraine would demand. Trump’s openness, combined with Xi’s role as proposed host, gives the summit enough institutional momentum to be treated as a credible scenario rather than a negotiating gesture, which is why Sybiha’s response was structural analysis rather than diplomatic deflection.

Iranian FM Araghchi declared Tuesday that Iran and Russia have never worked “as closely” as they are now, while Iranian officials separately told Reuters that Tehran has been deliberately prolonging nuclear talks with Washington specifically to weaken President Trump politically ahead of US midterm elections — and that Iran is preparing for renewed US and Israeli military attacks rather than pursuing a deal; the revelations reframe Monday’s Hormuz reopening proposal as a deliberate tactical maneuver rather than a genuine diplomatic opening, sending Brent to $110.67 at five-month highs as the market repriced the entire Hormuz-relief scenario.

The Reuters report is the week’s most consequential single intelligence disclosure because it changes the interpretive framework for every Iranian diplomatic gesture since the war began. If Tehran has been strategically prolonging talks to maintain political pressure on Trump — using negotiations as a tool of pressure rather than resolution — then the analysis of Monday’s Hormuz proposal changes entirely: it was not a moderation signal but a leverage management tactic, designed to create temporary oil relief in markets while maintaining Iran’s operational position in the Strait. The Iran-Russia “closer than ever” declaration compounds this reading: a deepened Iran-Russia military and political alignment, paired with a deliberate delay strategy aimed at US domestic politics, represents a coherent adversarial strategy rather than a series of disconnected tactical decisions.

The drone strike and the summit proposal are structurally related in Ukraine’s strategic situation: Russia is applying continuous military pressure — both through direct strikes and by forcing Ukrainian forces to operate without reliable communication and advance warning — while simultaneously pursuing a diplomatic framework that excludes Ukraine from its own settlement negotiation. The combination is a classic military-diplomatic squeeze: degrade the adversary’s operational position through military pressure while negotiating a political settlement that removes their ability to resist. Ukraine’s only leverage in this framework is European support and US assistance — both of which the Starlink throttling allegation and the delayed assistance reports suggest are less reliable than the previous framework assumed.

France’s curfew response illustrates the government’s core strategic dilemma: the tools available to stop riots — police presence, movement restrictions, arrest of perpetrators — do not address the economic conditions that generated the protests.
