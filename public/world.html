The Anchor · Two-Front Energy Disruption — What Happens When Both Hormuz and the Bab el-Mandab Are Contested Simultaneously

The oil market's prior assumption — that the Bab el-Mandab Strait could partially absorb the Hormuz disruption — has been invalidated Wednesday by Houthi attacks on Saudi energy infrastructure and continued Houthi interdiction of the strait itself. The strategic logic is straightforward: after Iran blockaded the Strait of Hormuz, which carries approximately 20% of global oil supply, the Bab el-Mandab Strait — connecting the Red Sea to the Gulf of Aden, through which Saudi Arabia routes significant alternative export volume — became more important as a relief valve. The Houthis, who are Iran-backed, are now contesting that relief valve directly, creating a two-front disruption that eliminates the partial escape route the market had been pricing since the Hormuz conflict began. The compound effect of simultaneous Hormuz and Bab el-Mandab disruption on global oil supply is not simply additive; it is multiplicative in its market impact. The prior oil price structure — Brent at $95-97 on Hormuz disruption alone — reflected a partial Hormuz disruption (enforcement was announced but not fully operational) with the Bab el-Mandab as an uncontested alternative. Wednesday's $100 Brent reflects a more complete disruption scenario: enforcement in the Hormuz exclusion zone plus active Houthi interdiction of the alternative route. The two choke points are not independent — they are coordinated Iranian-axis disruption of both primary and secondary routes simultaneously, which is the scenario that energy market analysts had been pricing as a low-probability tail risk. It is now the current situation. Iran's parallel diplomatic track — talks with Oman on a safe-passage framework — is real and ongoing, but Tehran has been explicit: the talks are about establishing an exemption regime for specific vessels transiting with Iranian permission, not about reopening the Strait to normal commercial transit. The distinction is crucial for market pricing. A safe-passage exemption regime changes which specific tankers can move, not the structural supply constraint; it creates a tiered access system, not a resolution. The WEF's framing of the US-Iran war as "deepening into an energy, fiscal and political crisis" captures the compound nature of the disruption: energy prices feeding domestic US inflation (August CPI tomorrow), fiscal pressure from elevated defense spending and strategic petroleum reserve management, and political pressure on the Trump administration from both the escalation itself and the rate-hike consequences if August CPI confirms the inflationary thesis.

Brent crude broke $100 per barrel Wednesday — the highest level since the Hormuz conflict began in the spring — as Iran's maritime exclusion zone moved from Monday's announcement into apparent enforcement and Houthi forces simultaneously attacked Saudi Arabia's Aramco Jazan refinery, forcing the closure of airports in Jazan and Najran and extending the supply disruption from the Strait of Hormuz to the Bab el-Mandab Strait, the secondary oil export route that had been absorbing some Hormuz displacement. The two-front disruption removes the partial pressure-release mechanism the market had been relying on: the prior assumption that Bab el-Mandab could partially substitute for a disrupted Hormuz has been directly challenged by Houthi attacks on the infrastructure supporting that alternative. The World Economic Forum describes the US-Iran war as "deepening into an energy, fiscal and political crisis" — the fiscal and political dimensions reflect the domestic US consequences of sustained $100 oil, including the August CPI reading that arrives tomorrow and the September 16 FOMC rate decision that follows.

The Houthi campaign against Bab el-Mandab is Iran-axis strategy at a level above a single state actor. The Houthis — Iran-backed — are contesting the same alternative shipping route that the oil market identified as the Hormuz bypass, suggesting a coordinated approach to maximizing supply disruption: if Hormuz is blocked, contest Bab el-Mandab; if Bab el-Mandab is disrupted, both primary routes are unavailable simultaneously. For the Hormuz conflict's oil market impact, the distinction between "both routes disrupted" and "one route disrupted with a partial alternative" is significant: it moves the market from pricing a constrained supply scenario to pricing a deeply constrained one with no near-term geographic release valve, short of US Navy enforcement that has not been publicly authorized at that operational level.

Iran is engaged in talks with Oman to establish a safe-passage framework for specific vessels transiting the Strait of Hormuz — but Tehran has explicitly warned that this "does not mean reopening the waterway," marking the diplomatic track as a tiered-access negotiation rather than a conflict-resolution process. The distinction is commercially and strategically significant: a safe-passage exemption regime creates a class of vessels that can move through the Strait with Iranian permission, which affects which specific tankers can operate but does not restore normal commercial shipping volume or remove the supply constraint that is driving Brent above $100. Oman's role as intermediary is consistent with its prior function as a US-Iran diplomatic back-channel; the talks indicate both parties see value in sub-conflict diplomatic management even as the military confrontation escalates. Fitch separately removed Qatar from its negative rating watch, citing eased risks to LNG facilities — a partial regional stabilization that contrasts sharply with Wednesday's Saudi-Houthi escalation.

Ukraine's 72-hour Kyiv strike pause, which Putin ordered on Saturday September 5 and which expired on Monday September 7, has not produced a confirmed follow-on ceasefire framework or confirmed return to open Russian strikes as of Wednesday's reporting — the uncertainty is itself informative. The shuttle's "refreshed" proposal, worked on by US envoys with UK, French, and German NSA input, exists but has not been publicly detailed, and Russia's demand for eased US economic sanctions as a precondition for further engagement remains the stated price for genuine engagement. The sanctions-easing precondition is politically difficult for the Trump administration before the midterms, making the shuttle's momentum uncertain despite the structural progress made in having three allied NSAs join the final meeting.
