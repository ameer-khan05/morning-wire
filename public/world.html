Iran said Monday it is not in direct negotiations with the United States — and that it is speaking only to Oman about a new arrangement for the Strait of Hormuz — directly contradicting Trump's claim that a deal is "imminent" and that talks would resume Monday, creating the defining diplomatic gap of Day 34: markets are pricing peace probability (WTI ▼5.26% to $79.54, below $80 for the first time since February 28) while the governments of the United States and Iran are describing two entirely different diplomatic situations to their respective publics; PBS News separately reported that Iran and the US had "reached an initial deal to extend the ceasefire and open the Strait of Hormuz, but challenges remain" — a description consistent with an Oman-mediated framework in progress, in which the two governments' public statements can both be technically accurate: the US has agreed to a framework, Iran is negotiating the implementation through Oman without publicly acknowledging a deal with Washington.

The Oman channel is the most important variable to understand today. Oman mediated the early-stage negotiations that eventually produced the 2015 JCPOA — they have a track record of successful US-Iran back-channel facilitation, and both governments trust the Omani framework as a mechanism that lets each side describe the same negotiation in different terms for domestic audiences. Whether the Oman channel's current process produces a formal Hormuz reopening framework in the 14 days before the UNSC nuclear deadline is the question the oil market, the bond market, and 34 days of accumulated geopolitical risk are waiting to answer.

Day 34 (Mon Aug 3): Iran's government said Monday it is not in direct negotiations with the United States and that it is speaking to Oman about a new arrangement for the Strait of Hormuz — declining to characterize Saturday's Trump announcement as a bilateral deal or to confirm any agreed framework. Trump: said Monday that talks with Iran will resume today and that a deal on the Strait of Hormuz is "imminent," claiming there is "an agreement to follow" on denuclearization. PBS News: "Iran and the US reach an initial deal to extend the ceasefire and open the Strait of Hormuz, but challenges remain" — language consistent with a framework brokered through an Oman intermediary that neither government is publicly describing in identical terms. The Oman channel: Oman is the most credible US-Iran back-channel available. Oman facilitated the secret preliminary negotiations between the Obama administration and the Rouhani government that preceded the 2015 JCPOA, without either government publicly acknowledging the other as a direct counterpart during the early-stage process. The same mechanism — Oman as intermediary, both governments negotiating the framework without a public bilateral acknowledgment — is the architecture consistent with today's Iranian statement ("we are talking to Oman") and Trump's ("talks resume today, deal imminent") simultaneously. If the Oman channel is functioning, both statements can be technically accurate and compatible. Oil market consequence: WTI at $79.54 (▼5.26%) — below $80 for the first time since Feb 28 — suggests markets are pricing a high probability of a Hormuz framework emerging from the Oman channel, even without confirmation of a direct US-Iran agreement. Brent at $83.32 (▼5.15%) reflects the same probability pricing. The reversal risk: if the Oman channel fails and Iran resumes strait restrictions, oil returns above $85 within one to two trading sessions, reasserting the geopolitical premium that has been the primary oil-market variable for 34 days. UNSC deadline: August 17 — 14 days. The Security Council session was designed to force a resolution on Iran's nuclear program (enrichment at 84%+, IAEA access suspended). If the Oman channel produces a Hormuz framework before August 17, the UNSC session changes character: instead of an enforcement confrontation, it receives a progress report on a framework in progress. The session may still address the nuclear components of any deal, but it would not be the enforcement crisis it was designed to produce if no framework exists. US midterm elections: 3 months away. Trump's political clock creates urgency that is independent of the diplomatic one — a peace deal before midterms is a significant political asset, and every week of continued conflict is a liability in an election environment where US gas prices have been elevated 38% since February 28. The Oman channel architecture matters more than either government's public statement. What Iran and the US say for their respective domestic audiences about whether they are "talking directly" is a political framing question, not a diplomatic substance question. The substance question is whether the Oman channel is producing a formal framework — with specific Hormuz traffic restoration terms, a timeline for IAEA access restoration, and a nuclear verification mechanism — that both governments can accept. If that framework exists in draft form through Oman, the PBS "initial deal, challenges remain" framing is accurate and the oil market's 5-6% move is correctly pricing the framework's eventual formalization. If the Oman channel is producing nothing substantive, the oil move is sentiment-driven and will reverse. Monday's diplomatic dispatches will begin to clarify which scenario is correct.

Kuwait and Bahrain's defense cooperation invocation decisions remain outstanding. Both governments condemned Day 31's Iranian strikes on their sovereign military bases (Ahmad al-Jaber Air Base in Kuwait, Sheikh Isa Air Base in Bahrain hosting the 5th Fleet headquarters); neither has invoked its defense agreement as of Monday morning. The ongoing peace framework process appears to be providing diplomatic cover for deferring the invocation decision — if a deal emerges before the invocation is formalized, the governments can point to the diplomatic resolution as the reason they did not need to invoke, avoiding the escalation that invocation would have triggered while maintaining their public condemnation. The 5th Fleet headquarters damage assessment at Sheikh Isa — power generators and navigation systems hit — is ongoing.

Iran's Monday statement — that it is not in direct negotiations with the United States and is speaking to Oman about the Strait of Hormuz — and Trump's Monday claim that a deal is "imminent" and talks resume today are contradictory on the surface but compatible if the Oman channel is functioning as an intermediary: under the Oman-mediated framework, Iran negotiates with Oman rather than with Washington directly, the US negotiates with Oman, and Oman brokers the framework — a structure that lets each government maintain its preferred domestic political framing (Iran: "we are not talking to America"; Trump: "talks are happening and a deal is coming") while the same substantive negotiation proceeds through Oman's facilitation. PBS News's report of "an initial deal to extend the ceasefire and open the Strait of Hormuz, but challenges remain" is precisely the language consistent with an Oman-brokered framework in progress — "initial deal" suggests agreed-in-principle terms, "challenges remain" suggests implementation details or nuclear clause specifics are unresolved.

The Oman channel's historical precedent matters. Oman facilitated the secret preliminary negotiations that produced the preliminary 2013 framework that led to the 2015 JCPOA — a process that lasted more than a year and involved US and Iranian officials meeting in Muscat without either government publicly acknowledging bilateral talks. The current Hormuz framework has a much tighter timeline than the JCPOA process — Trump's "rapid deal" language and the UNSC August 17 deadline create a forcing function for resolution in days, not months. If the Oman channel can produce agreed Hormuz reopening terms on that timeline, the mechanism is credible. The key question is whether Iran's conditions — which historically include some form of sanction relief, nuclear framework acknowledgment, or security guarantee — can be agreed through Oman at the pace the diplomatic clock requires.
