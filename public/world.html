The asymmetric framing — "on the verge" from Tehran, "not finality" from Washington — reflects the two governments describing the same negotiating state from politically different vantage points, with Iran's public optimism serving a domestic audience that needs to see progress after 41 days of closure and the economic costs it entails, while US caution manages domestic expectations about the nature of any concessions involved in reopening.

Hormuz Day 41 (Monday August 10): Iran's Foreign Minister Araghchi told reporters that a deal is "on the verge of being finalized," making explicit what Saturday's US News reporting had characterized as "close but not enough to open the waterway by itself." The specific advancement cited by Araghchi: Iran and Oman have agreed on the coordinates for commercial shipping lanes through the Strait — a technical agreement on the geographic routing mechanism that both Oman's Foreign Ministry ("positive and constructive at technical and political levels") and Iran's reporting characterized as the key outstanding item in the bilateral framework. US Secretary Rubio's "progress made, but not finality" framing differs from Iran's "on the verge" in a way that is consistent with describing the same factual state from different political positions: Araghchi needs to characterize progress as near-complete to justify 41 days of closure to an Iranian domestic audience experiencing economic pain from the blockade; Rubio needs to characterize the same progress as incomplete to justify continued pressure and avoid appearing to have capitulated to Iran's demands. Both characterizations can be simultaneously accurate: the technical framework (routing coordinates) may be agreed while the political authorization mechanism (how the US blockade is unwound without characterizing it as concession) remains unresolved. Iran's IRGC conditions — "fully accept" all Iranian conditions before the Strait reopens — remain in public force even as Araghchi describes deal proximity; the IRGC's public position has not been withdrawn or softened, which means it either reflects a genuine divergence between the IRGC's threshold and the Iranian government's diplomatic position, or it is a planned negotiating floor that the private deal will supersede without the IRGC publicly recanting. Fortune's prior reporting that a concession on Iranian Hormuz control has "already been made" in the framework negotiations suggests the latter interpretation: the private deal landscape is narrower than the public positions, and the IRGC's "fully accept" threshold is the public floor above the private settlement point. The key analytic question entering the August 17 UNSC vote is not whether the technical deal framework exists — Araghchi's "on the verge" and the Oman coordinates confirmation suggest it does — but whether the US has the political mechanism to implement it. The Trump administration can characterize blockade withdrawal as a conditions-based drawdown (not concession to Iran) only if it has the framing that a UNSC resolution provides: "we are complying with our international obligations under a UN freedom-of-navigation mandate." Without the UNSC resolution, withdrawal is characterized as capitulation. With it, withdrawal is characterized as implementation of multilateral law. The Russia-China veto risk is therefore not just a diplomatic complication — it is the mechanism that determines whether the Trump administration can politically afford to implement the deal that Araghchi says is already on the verge of being finalized.

The Mecca Joint Defence Pact — signed Saturday-Sunday (August 8-9) by Turkey, Saudi Arabia, and Pakistan — continues generating diplomatic reaction in European capitals focused on the Turkey-NATO compatibility question. Turkey's official framing that the pact is "fully compatible with NATO obligations" has been accepted without formal challenge by NATO's Brussels headquarters, but European allies are watching what operational obligations Turkey could face if Iran-backed proxies intensify pressure on Saudi Arabia in ways that invoke the pact's mutual-defense clause. Turkey's defense establishment is carefully managing the framing to ensure the pact is seen as complementary to NATO membership rather than as a competing security commitment.

The August 17 UNSC freedom-of-navigation vote — 7 days away — is the institutional mechanism that will determine whether the Trump administration has the political framing it needs to implement the Hormuz deal that Araghchi says is "on the verge." The political logic is precise: a UNSC resolution mandating freedom of navigation through the Strait provides cover for blockade withdrawal to be characterized as "compliance with a binding UN Security Council mandate" rather than "capitulation to Iran's demands" — a framing distinction that is the difference between a diplomatically viable deal and a politically untenable one in the US domestic environment. Without the UNSC resolution, withdrawal is bilateral and therefore characterizable by critics as direct concession; with it, withdrawal is multilateral and therefore characterizable as rule-of-law compliance. General Caine's private assessment that the US needs an "off-ramp" (disclosed August 9) — and that available military escalation options could backfire — provides the military-leadership cover that any resolution allowing concession-adjacent outcomes requires: a president accepting unfavorable terms can cite military leadership's recommendation that escalation is inadvisable.

Russia and China's veto risk turns on the resolution's specific text: both countries may characterize a freedom-of-navigation resolution as legitimizing US naval operations in the Persian Gulf, which neither wants as a precedent. Resolution language that focuses on commercial shipping rights and humanitarian access — framed as an international law matter rather than a validation of US military presence — may attract Russian and Chinese abstention (the most the US can realistically hope for) rather than a veto. An abstention allows the resolution to pass with 9 or more affirmative votes; a veto forces the bilateral-only path that gives the Trump administration worse framing options. The 7-day diplomatic window is focused on whether that text can be agreed.

The Mecca Joint Defence Pact — signed by Turkey, Saudi Arabia, and Pakistan over the weekend of August 8-9 — continues generating diplomatic analysis in European capitals as the week opens, focused primarily on the Turkey-NATO compatibility question that Turkey's official "fully compatible with NATO obligations" framing has not fully resolved for European allies watching how it functions in practice; NATO's Brussels headquarters has not issued a formal challenge to Turkey's pact participation, which gives Turkey the procedural space it needs to maintain both commitments — NATO membership and the Mecca Pact — without forcing a choice. The practical question European allies are monitoring: if Iranian-backed proxies attack Saudi infrastructure at a level that Turkey's government characterizes as invoking the Mecca Pact's mutual-defense clause, what would Turkey's actual response look like, and how would it interact with Turkey's Article 5 obligations if that response drew a counter-response that endangered Turkish forces?

Pakistan's inclusion in the pact adds a dimension that receives less analysis than Turkey's NATO membership: Pakistan is a nuclear-armed state, and its mutual-defense commitment to Saudi Arabia introduces nuclear deterrence into the Gulf security architecture for the first time through a formal treaty commitment rather than an informal understanding. The deterrence implication for Iranian-backed actors considering escalation against Saudi territory is material: where previously only the US nuclear umbrella (extended through US force posture in the Gulf) provided Saudi Arabia with some implicit nuclear deterrence, the Mecca Pact creates an explicit treaty-based deterrence relationship with Pakistan's independent nuclear capability. This does not mean nuclear weapons would be used in response to proxy-level attacks — the threshold for nuclear use is significantly higher — but it changes the escalation calculus for any actor planning attacks against Saudi infrastructure or territory.

Quick hits

  • The specific text that Iran and Oman agreed on for shipping lane coordinates deserves clarification, because "coordinates agreed" can mean different things at different levels of technical specificity. At the minimum level, "coordinates agreed" means the two governments have agreed on the general routing corridors — north-south transit lanes, separation scheme boundaries, exclusion zones near Iranian naval facilities — that would be used for commercial shipping once the Strait reopens. At a more detailed level, it could mean agreed GPS waypoints for the specific inbound and outbound traffic separation scheme (TSS) that commercial vessels would follow under normal COLREGS navigation rules. The International Maritime Organization (IMO) manages the Hormuz TSS under normal circumstances; a reopening would need to reestablish that TSS with coordinates that both Iran and Oman agree represent their sovereign rights. The fact that coordinates are "agreed" between Iran and Oman suggests the IMO-level technical work is substantially complete — which is the precondition for the Strait's physical reopening to actually function, rather than being a political announcement without operational content. Coordinates agreed means ships have a route; blockade withdrawn means ships can use it. Both conditions are required for a functional reopening.
  • The humanitarian and economic cost of 41 days of Hormuz closure is accumulating in ways that add pressure to both sides to reach a resolution. Iran's economy is the most directly affected: the blockade on Iranian ports has reduced Iran's oil export revenue to near-zero for the duration of the closure, freezing the foreign exchange inflows that Iran's import-dependent economy requires. The Iranian rial's continued depreciation against hard currencies during the closure period reflects this: each day of closure is a day of import-cost escalation for Iranian consumers. For Gulf shipping, the alternative routing through the Red Sea (and Suez Canal) versus the previous direct Hormuz transit adds approximately 7-10 days to Asia-Europe shipping times, adding freight cost that is ultimately absorbed by end-consumers of manufactured goods. Both of these pressures operate independently of the diplomatic process and both favor resolution, which is why the 41-day closure has produced more genuine deal progress than some analysts expected — the economic cost of continued closure is real for both sides in ways that the immediate post-conflict dynamics did not make obvious.
