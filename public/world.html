President Trump said Friday at a campaign event in Garden City, New York that he plans to declare the Strait of Hormuz "a territory of the United States" after the war with Iran ends — a statement without legal precedent under UNCLOS or international maritime law, where sovereignty over the Strait is shared between Iran and Oman under the right of transit passage framework established after the 1979 Iranian Revolution, and where no international legal mechanism permits a third state to unilaterally claim territorial sovereignty over an international waterway. The statement arrived 24 hours before Sunday's diplomatic preparations for Monday's UNSC ceasefire vote — forcing a new calculation on Russia and China, already deciding whether to abstain from a resolution intended to pressure Iran, who must now weigh whether to support a multilateral mechanism that a senior US official has just suggested the US will permanently convert into a US territorial claim once the conflict ends. The United States currently maintains a naval blockade of the Strait through Operation Vela Nova; Trump's statement implies the naval presence is not temporary but a territorial assertion-in-progress.

The Strait of Hormuz enters Day 47 of closure Sunday with Iran reiterating that the waterway will not reopen until US offensive military operations end — no breakthrough has been reported in the negotiations that began in June with both sides signing a memorandum of understanding, with the most recent reporting indicating the two sides remain at odds over the terms of ending their five-month war. The UNSC votes Monday on a revised ceasefire resolution that has been reworked since Friday's UAE-ADNOC vessel attacks; the Oman diplomatic back-channel and the Joint Chiefs-IRGC communication line both remain open as the primary off-ramp architecture, though the UAE-ADNOC attacks on Friday have raised questions about whether Iran's civilian diplomatic track and its military operational decisions are coordinated.

Trump's "US territory" statement is the most geopolitically consequential remark on the Hormuz crisis since the conflict began — not because it is achievable (it is not, under any existing framework of international law) but because of what it signals about the US negotiating position and what it requires every other party to the crisis to recalculate. Iran's negotiating position through Oman has been that it will reopen the strait when the US ends its offensive military operations and provides security guarantees that post-war Iran will not face regime-change pressure. Trump's statement saying the US intends to claim sovereignty over the strait after the war ends tells Iran that the US objective is not to restore the pre-war status quo but to permanently subordinate Iran's economic lifeline to US territorial control — a negotiating position that is incompatible with any ceasefire Iran's leadership can domestically characterize as something other than total defeat. If Trump's statement is a negotiating tactic designed to create maximum leverage for a deal on less maximalist terms, it is the highest-risk version of that strategy: it hardens Iran's domestic political constraints against compromise at exactly the moment the UNSC vote is intended to create political space for Iran to accept de-escalation as a multilateral outcome rather than a US demand. If Trump's statement is a serious policy intention, it has removed the political conditions under which Iran can reopen the Strait without appearing to capitulate to territorial subjugation — and the Oman framework's viability depends entirely on Iranian civilian leadership's ability to separate the diplomatic track from the president's campaign statement. Russia and China's UNSC abstention calculus — which had been oriented around their economic interest in oil price stability and their commercial relationships with the UAE — must now include the scenario in which their abstention helps pass a resolution that the US subsequently uses as a foundation for a territorial claim they will oppose. If Russian or Chinese strategic interests in preventing US permanent Hormuz control outweigh their economic interest in lower oil prices, the calculation shifts from abstention to veto. That is the most diplomatically destabilizing interpretation of Trump's statement, and it is the one that Sunday's diplomatic preparations in New York are working to contain. The Oman framework context as of Sunday: the negotiations that began in June — when both the US and Iran signed a memorandum of understanding — are the most advanced diplomatic framework the conflict has produced. The Oman framework's terms, as reported, involve a phased reopening of the Strait in exchange for de-escalatory measures from both sides, with Iran's primary condition being that the US end offensive military operations before Hormuz reopens. The framework's viability depends on both parties' civilian leadership having operational control over military decisions — a condition the UAE-ADNOC attack on Friday raised questions about from Iran's side, and Trump's "US territory" statement raises questions about from the US side. Sunday's most important intelligence is not what diplomats say in New York but what signals emerge from Tehran about whether Oman remains a valid channel and whether Iran's civilian leadership is distancing itself from Friday's ADNOC attack as an operational error rather than a deliberate policy decision.

The Strait carries approximately 20% of the world's oil supply; 47 days of closure has forced global shipping to reroute at significantly higher cost and time, drawn down strategic petroleum reserves in consuming nations, and raised insurance premiums for Gulf-adjacent shipping to their highest levels since the tanker wars of the 1980s. CNN's August 15 update confirmed that Hormuz traffic remains at near three-month lows as the world continues burning through oil stockpiles rather than receiving new supply through the closed strait. Sunday's diplomatic preparations for Monday's UNSC vote are the most consequential pre-vote period since the crisis began — and Trump's statement made them significantly more complicated.

President Trump said Friday at a campaign rally in Garden City, New York, supporting Republican gubernatorial candidate Bruce Blakeman, that he plans to declare the Strait of Hormuz "a territory of the United States" after the war with Iran ends — a statement without legal precedent under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, under which the Strait of Hormuz is classified as an international waterway subject to the right of transit passage, not subject to territorial sovereignty by any state other than Iran (which controls the eastern shore) and Oman (which controls the western shore). The US cannot acquire territorial sovereignty over the Strait through military victory over Iran because Oman's sovereign territory on the western shore is not part of the conflict, and because UNCLOS does not permit territorial claims over international transit waterways regardless of military outcome. The statement's practical importance is therefore not as a policy road map — it has no legal implementation path — but as a signal to every party that the US administration's stated post-war objective includes permanent control of the world's most critical energy chokepoint.

The diplomatic consequences of the statement operate at multiple levels simultaneously: Iran's negotiating position through Oman — which has been that the strait will reopen when the US ends its offensive military operations — is now required to contemplate a scenario in which reopening the strait as part of a ceasefire deal is followed by a US territorial claim on the same waterway, making any ceasefire deal Iran negotiates a precursor to permanent US control rather than a restoration of the pre-war status quo. Russia and China, whose expected abstention from Monday's UNSC resolution was calculated on the basis of economic interest in lower oil prices and commercial interest in Gulf stability, must now weigh whether abstaining supports a diplomatic process that the US has announced it intends to use as a pathway to Hormuz sovereignty — a goal directly contrary to both countries' energy security interests.

If the ADNOC attack was authorized with awareness of the Oman negotiations, it signals that Iran's political leadership has concluded ADNOC escalation serves the overall negotiating strategy; if it was an IRGC operational decision that outran civilian oversight, the Oman track's viability depends on Iranian civilian leadership constraining the military before Monday's UNSC vote. The Joint Chiefs' back-channel communication with IRGC counterparts — established during the current crisis — is the US's most direct tool for assessing which interpretation is correct before Monday's vote.

The 47-day Hormuz closure has produced economic disruption at a scale that Foreign Affairs Forum analysis has characterized as the most consequential single chokepoint event since Britain and France's Suez Canal seizure in 1956 — a comparison that understates one critical difference: Suez was resolved in approximately 10 days under US economic pressure on the UK and France, while Hormuz has now passed 47 days without resolution because the US is not applying pressure on itself to de-escalate but is rather the primary military actor in a bilateral conflict with Iran. CNN's August 15 update confirmed that Strait traffic remains at near three-month lows as consuming nations continue drawing down strategic petroleum reserves rather than receiving new Hormuz-transited supply. The International Energy Agency's August oil market report indicated that 47 consecutive days of closure has begun visibly affecting forward demand projections — the first confirmation that the supply disruption is producing demand destruction at an observable scale.

The economic toll compounds daily: shipping insurance premiums for Gulf-adjacent routes have reached their highest levels since the tanker wars of the 1980s; the Suez and Cape of Good Hope rerouting alternatives add 12-14 days and $2-3 million per voyage in additional cost; and the strategic petroleum reserve drawdown by the US, Europe, Japan, and South Korea is consuming buffers that were not at full capacity after the 2022-2023 energy crisis. The Vela Nova enforcement floor — which has held Brent at or above $85 since Operation Vela Nova began on Day 41 — is the market's best evidence that the US Navy's commitment to maintaining commercial vessel access has stabilized one side of the oil price equation; Monday's UNSC vote is the best available evidence that the diplomatic side is still being pursued.

Quick hits

  • Trump's "US territory" statement warrants a precise international law note. Under UNCLOS Article 37-44, international straits — including Hormuz — are governed by the right of transit passage, which grants all ships and aircraft the right to navigate and overfly without obstruction. This right cannot be suspended by the bordering states (Iran and Oman), and the legal framework does not contemplate third-state territorial acquisition of international straits. The US could, in theory, negotiate a bilateral agreement with Oman granting US basing rights on Omani territory adjacent to the Strait; the US could maintain its current naval blockade position through military force; but neither of these outcomes is what "territory of the United States" means under US or international law. The statement is legally incoherent as a policy pathway — which is why its significance is entirely as a signaling event rather than an operational announcement.
  • The UAE-ADNOC attack context, now two days old, has a developing dimension that will affect Monday's UNSC vote. When the UAE publicly accused Iran of the ADNOC attacks and used the word "piracy" Friday, it became a direct party to the conflict for the first time — the Gulf's most commercially cautious state, with the most to lose from Gulf instability, now on record as an attack victim rather than a neutral bystander. The UAE's role at the UNSC changes accordingly: the Emirates can now speak as a direct victim rather than an observer, giving its diplomats standing to argue for a resolution based on Gulf state sovereignty rather than US-Iran conflict management. If the UAE's transformation from bystander to victim changes enough votes on the Council — specifically, if it moves any of the states that would otherwise abstain or support Iran to a firmer pro-resolution position — Monday's vote outcome could be more decisive than the pre-attack analysis projected.
