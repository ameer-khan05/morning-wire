The Anchor · The US-Iran War's Second Phase — Two Rounds of Strikes Establish a Campaign, Not a Signal

There is a categorical difference between one military strike and two strikes in three days. One strike is a signal — a bounded action designed to communicate a threshold and await response. Two strikes in three days is a campaign — a sustained military operation with operational objectives (degrade radar, eliminate mine-laying capability) that runs regardless of diplomatic response or absence thereof. Wednesday's US airstrikes against Iran establish the second category. Monday's Larak Island exchange crossed the threshold of direct US-Iran military confrontation for the first time in the 2026 crisis. Wednesday's second round of strikes confirms that crossing that threshold did not produce a diplomatic pause — it produced a continued strike tempo. That is the most important fact about Wednesday's session. The civilian casualties at the wedding compound the political dimension. A US military strike killing five civilians at a wedding is not just a tragedy in the moment — it is the type of incident that reshapes the political coalition supporting the strike authorization in the US Congress, in allied capitals, and in the public discourse that determines how much operational freedom the military retains to continue the campaign. The Iranian retaliation — drone and missile volleys on US bases in Jordan and Bahrain — represents Iran's operational response to the second round: not de-escalation, but matching the sustained-strike tempo with a sustained retaliation tempo. Bloomberg's characterization of the fighting as "intensifying" reflects both the escalation from Monday's single exchange to Wednesday's second exchange, and Iran's parallel drone-and-missile campaign that targets US basing infrastructure rather than limiting retaliation to symbolic targets. The Hormuz commerce disruption track continues independently. The Day 64 tanker picture — supply disruption persisting alongside direct military confrontation — establishes that Iran has not linked commerce disruption to the diplomatic or military negotiating track. It is a separate pressure instrument running in parallel, not a bargaining chip withdrawn when the military exchange escalates. Brent crossing $95 intraday is the oil market's integrated assessment of both tracks: $95 prices a sustained military campaign, not an episodic geopolitical spike. The difference matters for how long the oil premium persists. Episodic spikes reverse when the triggering event ends. Campaign-phase oil premiums persist as long as the campaign continues — which, with no diplomatic channel visible and both sides sustaining strike tempos, could be weeks. The September 10 August CPI will determine whether the $95 Brent level has already fed through to inflation expectations in a way that makes a September rate hike politically and economically unavoidable, or whether the timeline allows the market a one-data-point reprieve before the September 15-16 decision window.
