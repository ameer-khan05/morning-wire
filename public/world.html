A 10-day ceasefire proposal — assembled by Pakistan, Qatar, Egypt, and Turkey and presented to the United States and Iran — remains on the table as Day 25 of the Iran conflict opens Saturday with neither side accepting the framework; Iran's Foreign Ministry confirmed receiving "proposals and ideas from mediators aimed at reducing tensions" while the Trump administration said it is studying the proposal and Trump views Iran as "getting more serious"; Yemen's Houthis separately declared a formal maritime blockade of Saudi Arabia, claiming operational control of the Bab al-Mandeb Strait and warning that vessels transporting Saudi cargo may be targeted anywhere within Houthi operational reach — with commercial ships already rerouting away from the strait — while Brent crude retreated 3.89% to $96.78 on Friday as some of the week's geopolitical premium partially unwound; and Day 25 brings the nuclear compliance deadline to August 17, 23 days away, with Iran's Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei still not having appeared publicly and the IRGC retaining operational control of a military campaign now inclusive of the Houthi formal blockade as a qualitatively new strategic dimension.

The 10-day ceasefire proposal is the most concrete diplomatic framework since the July 4 ceasefire collapsed — and its architecture is more complex. The proposal would return both sides to their pre-July 9 positions and use the truce period to negotiate a Hormuz "middle corridor" for commercial vessel transit between Omani-controlled and Iranian-controlled waters, separating commercial shipping from the military confrontation that has driven Brent above $100. Neither side accepting nor rejecting the framework within 24–48 hours of its presentation is consistent with diplomatic process: Iran confirmed receiving it without committing, and the US studying it rather than dismissing it is the diplomatic posture that keeps the proposal alive. The Houthi formal maritime blockade of Saudi Arabia is the week's most significant escalation in terms of structural shipping risk: commercial shipping is already rerouting from the Bab al-Mandeb, which means the market impact is occurring without the Houthis having yet targeted or seized any vessels. The enforcement question — whether the Houthis have the operational capability to execute against Saudi-bound vessels at scale — is what separates a formal declaration from a functioning blockade. Friday's Brent retreat to $96.78 from $100.69 suggests markets are treating the blockade as declared but not yet enforced, while the ceasefire proposal is providing enough diplomatic signal to partially unwind the week's premium.

Pakistan, Qatar, Egypt, and Turkey jointly presented the United States and Iran with a proposal for a 10-day ceasefire that would return both sides to their positions held before July 9 and use the truce period to negotiate a "middle corridor" through the Strait of Hormuz — a channel between Omani-controlled and Iranian-controlled waters through which commercial vessels could transit safely without entering the military confrontation zone. Iran's Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei confirmed Tehran received "proposals and ideas from mediators aimed at reducing tensions" without providing further details. The Trump administration said it is studying the proposal, with Trump stating he believes Iran is "getting more serious" about ending the conflict. The US exit strategy for the conflict could come either diplomatically or through military resolution, according to Trump.

The Hormuz middle corridor concept is the diplomatic innovation embedded in this proposal that distinguishes it from the July 4 ceasefire architecture. The July 4 agreement was a kinetic pause — both sides stopped shooting for 72 hours; it did not address the Hormuz transit question that is the central commercial and economic driver of Brent pricing. A corridor that separates commercial shipping from the Iran-US military confrontation could reduce the oil supply risk premium even during an ongoing conflict. Pakistan's second mediation attempt is structurally more ambitious than the first, incorporating Egypt's commercial expertise (Egypt operates the Suez Canal, which connects to the Red Sea above the Bab al-Mandeb) and Turkey's NATO-adjacent diplomatic positioning alongside Qatar's existing back-channel relationship with Iran.

Houthis Declare Formal Maritime Blockade of Saudi Arabia — "Siege for a Siege" · Claimed Bab al-Mandeb Operational Control · Ships Rerouting · ~5% Global Crude Oil Flow at Risk · Enforcement Not Yet Executed · Brent $96.78 ▼3.89% on Partial Relief Blockade Houthis: formal maritime blockade Saudi Arabia (Jul 20) · Yahya Saree: "siege for a siege" · scope: Saudi cargo vessels targeted anywhere in operational reach · Bab al-Mandeb: ships rerouting · ~5% global crude · enforcement: not yet executed · Brent $96.78 ▼3.89% — market treating as declared, not enforced

Yemen's Houthi movement declared a formal maritime blockade of Saudi Arabia on July 20, with military spokesperson Yahya Saree announcing it as "a siege for a siege" — framing it as retaliation for Saudi air and maritime blockades of Yemen that have been in place for roughly a decade. Under the declaration, vessels loading or discharging cargo at Saudi ports are banned, and vessels transporting Saudi cargo may be targeted anywhere within Houthi operational reach — a scope that extends beyond the Bab al-Mandeb Strait itself to the full Red Sea and Gulf of Aden. Commercial shipping companies have already routed vessels away from the Bab al-Mandeb even though the Houthis have not yet targeted or seized any vessel under the formal declaration. Approximately 5% of global crude oil flow — about 4.1 billion barrels annually — passed through the strait in 2024.

The distinction between a formal blockade declaration and an enforced blockade is what Friday's Brent retreat to $96.78 reflects. Commercial ships rerouting is a market-impact outcome that occurs from a declaration alone — shippers and insurers do not wait for a vessel to be targeted before changing routing. But the Houthi track record is threats followed by action, which means the enforcement question is a matter of when rather than whether, absent a ceasefire. The practical enforcement capacity — whether the Houthis have sufficient naval and missile assets to interdict commercial vessels at scale across the Red Sea and Gulf of Aden — is the operational constraint that limits how fully a formal blockade translates into a functioning one. Saudi Arabia's ability to reroute oil exports via the East-West pipeline to the Red Sea coast or entirely around Africa is the physical backstop that prevents a blockade declaration from immediately becoming an oil supply emergency.

Day 25 of the Iran conflict brings the nuclear compliance deadline to August 17 — 23 days away — with Iran's Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei still not having appeared publicly since taking the position, leaving the IRGC in operational control of a military campaign that now includes the formal Houthi maritime blockade of Saudi Arabia as an additional front. Iran's nuclear enrichment remains at 84%+ purity with IAEA access suspended, and any ceasefire framework capable of addressing the August 17 deadline requires Supreme Leader authorization — which cannot be provided by the IRGC alone. President Trump has said the US exit strategy for the conflict could come either diplomatically or through military resolution, and Secretary Rubio has kept the diplomacy channel open while strikes continue. The 10-day ceasefire proposal does not directly address Russia's confirmed role in planning Iranian operations, which the July 4 framework also failed to incorporate.

Mojtaba Khamenei's continued absence from public view is the central political uncertainty in the ceasefire architecture. Any framework that Pakistan, Qatar, Egypt, and Turkey can negotiate with Iran requires someone with Supreme Leader authority to say yes on the Iranian side. The IRGC is an operational body; it cannot commit Iran to a political agreement binding on the state. Pakistan's mediation team has presumably made contact with Iranian interlocutors who claim to represent decision-making authority — but the authoritative signal the diplomatic community is watching is a public Khamenei appearance, which would signal that the Supreme Leader is functional, engaged, and in a position to authorize a political framework. His continued absence at Day 25 keeps that critical question open. The 23-day clock to the nuclear deadline gives the current diplomatic window a specific endpoint: if no framework is accepted and August 17 arrives with enrichment at 84%+ and IAEA access still suspended, the crisis enters a qualitatively different phase.
