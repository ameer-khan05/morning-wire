The Anchor · BRICS Day 1 — What India's Chairship Faces When Two Members Are at War With Each Other

The 18th BRICS Summit in New Delhi is the most internally conflicted BRICS gathering since the bloc's founding. India's chairship theme — "Building for Resilience, Innovation, Cooperation and Sustainability" — describes a set of aspirations that are structurally undermined by the same geopolitical conflict that defines the week's broader news environment: the Iran war has placed two BRICS members, Iran and the UAE, on opposite sides of an active armed conflict, with the UAE having suspended trade and financial relationships with Iran in August after Iranian missile attacks. India cannot adjudicate that conflict from the chair. What it can do — and what Modi's diplomatic approach throughout 2026 has suggested it will attempt — is manage the session so that the BRICS outcome document acknowledges the division without fracturing the bloc over it. The foreign ministers' meeting in May already demonstrated the floor: they could not agree on a joint communique, so India issued a chair's statement that acknowledged differences while calling for an early return to diplomacy. The heads-of-state summit faces the same choice at a higher level. Bloomberg's framing — "Iran turns to BRICS summit as US war deepens its isolation" — is accurate but partial. Iran is not the only party using BRICS instrumentally: China is using it to demonstrate an alternative multilateral architecture that operates outside Western sanctions frameworks; Russia is using it to signal that its own isolation narrative is not universally held; India is using it to demonstrate its capacity to host a strategic global gathering despite its simultaneous economic ties to the US, Israel, and Iran. What BRICS can specifically offer Iran beyond diplomatic symbolism is limited but not trivial: energy trading arrangements that circumvent SWIFT, back-channel diplomatic communications between Pezeshkian and leaders who have influence over the US-aligned response, and a public-record signal that Iran is not isolated from the world's largest emerging-economy bloc. What BRICS cannot offer is a ceasefire mechanism — only the US and Israel can stop operations, and neither is a BRICS member. Al Jazeera described the summit as potentially "the only place another Iran ceasefire can be built" — the most that means in practical terms is that BRICS members with influence over both sides can use the summit to seed a back-channel ceasefire conversation. Whether that happens in New Delhi on Saturday and Sunday is the summit's most consequential unknown. Sweden's election on September 13 — the same day the BRICS summit concludes — runs the parallel Western democratic sequence: if the Red-Green bloc's polling lead holds, Sweden will begin transitioning to a left-leaning government in a European security environment defined by the same Iran war and Middle East escalation the BRICS summit is attempting to address from the Global South's side.

The 18th BRICS Summit opened Saturday in New Delhi with Iranian President Pezeshkian attending the September 12-13 gathering hosted by Indian Prime Minister Modi — Iran's highest-profile multilateral diplomatic engagement since the February US-Israeli strikes — as Bloomberg frames it: "Iran turns to BRICS summit as US war deepens its isolation." The summit's agenda covers intra-BRICS trade and investment, payments and supply-chain cooperation, energy (smart grids, sustainable aviation fuel), and digital agriculture. But the UAE's decision to suspend trade and financial transactions with Iran in August — after Iranian missile attacks — means two members of the same bloc sit on opposing sides of an active armed conflict that India must chair around without fracturing the summit.

India's diplomatic approach throughout 2026 has been to maintain economic relationships with the US, Israel, the UAE, and Iran simultaneously while chairing a summit that includes all of the above except the first two. That is a structurally difficult position: the BRICS outcome document must either acknowledge the conflict and the member split (as the May foreign ministers' meeting did via chair's statement) or produce diplomatic language vague enough that both Iran and the UAE can sign it. Al Jazeera's description of the BRICS summit as "the only place another Iran ceasefire can be built" is accurate in one sense — it is the only forum where leaders with influence over Iran, and leaders with influence over the US-aligned response, are physically present simultaneously. Whether that proximity translates into a ceasefire conversation, and whether that conversation produces anything operational, are separate questions. The summit concludes Sunday.

Sweden Votes Tomorrow (Sep 13) — Red-Green Bloc 50-52%, Tidö Coalition 44-47% · Riksdag + 21 Regional + 290 Municipal Elections Simultaneously · Tightest Race Since 2022 · Sweden Democrats Anchor Right Coalition Votes Tomorrow Sweden (Sep 13, tomorrow): Riksdag election (349 seats) + regional (21) + municipal (290) → simultaneous → Red-Green bloc (Social Democrats + Left + Greens): 50-52% → Tidö coalition (Moderates + Christian Democrats + Liberals + Sweden Democrats): 44-47% → multiple poll averages (Verian, Demoskop, Infostat, Novus, Aug 20-Sep 2): consistent Red-Green lead → SD: anchor of right coalition → tightest race: electorate volatile into polling day → results: expected Sep 13 evening CET → new government: formed over following weeks

Sweden's general election — covering the 349-seat Riksdag plus 21 regional councils and 290 municipal councils simultaneously — is tomorrow, September 13, with the Red-Green bloc polling at 50-52% and the Tidö coalition (Moderates, Christian Democrats, Liberals, and the Sweden Democrats) at 44-47% across multiple polling firms in late August and early September. The current right-wing minority government has governed with the support of the Sweden Democrats since 2022; a Red-Green victory would end that arrangement and produce a left-leaning government for the first time since the 2022 election reversed the prior center-left period. The margin is within polling uncertainty — the tightest major European election of the autumn 2026 season.

The stakes of the Swedish election extend beyond domestic governance. Sweden joined NATO in March 2024 and is now a full Alliance member whose government's posture on Russia, the Middle East, and European security will affect NATO's consensus calculations. A Red-Green government would govern differently from the current right coalition on several issues where European left-right divisions are sharpest: immigration policy, the balance between NATO commitments and humanitarian engagement in the Middle East, and the EU's approach to sanctions policy. The election runs on the same day the BRICS summit concludes in New Delhi — two simultaneous geopolitical sequences at opposite ends of the spectrum, one tracking the Global South's response to the Iran war, the other tracking Western Europe's domestic political direction. Results are expected Sunday evening Central European time.

The Houthi territorial foothold in Mokha — seized Friday in the largest Houthi advance since 2022 — holds through Saturday morning with no reports of a coalition counter-offensive to dislodge the position, leaving Brent crude at $107.40 from Friday's close. The dual chokepoint structure that Friday's seizure established remains intact: Iran's naval exclusion zone at the Strait of Hormuz and the Houthi shore-based positions now within 50 miles of the Bab el-Mandab. Friday's additional escalations — the IRGC's 20-ballistic-missile strike on a US airbase in Jordan (the first IRGC ballistic attack on Jordanian soil) and US CENTCOM's announcement of permanently disabling three Iranian oil tankers — have produced no reported counter-movements through Saturday morning, as both sides absorb Friday's exchanges.

The absence of Saturday counter-offensive activity is itself informative: the coalition's military posture after the Mokha seizure is not immediately escalatory, suggesting Friday's exchange established a new equilibrium rather than triggering a rapid military response cycle. For Brent crude, this means $107.40 is the baseline entering Monday's session rather than a transient spike. A counter-offensive against Mokha — which the UAE and Saudi Arabia would likely support and which US naval forces would be positioned to assist — would be the market event that begins a Brent reversal; its absence keeps the price where it is. The BRICS summit's Day 1 in New Delhi is the diplomatic parallel: if Saturday's summit produces any credible ceasefire signal, that signal would be the other mechanism by which the oil premium might begin unwinding. Both scenarios remain possible; neither has materialized as of Saturday morning.
