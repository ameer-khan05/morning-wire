Hormuz Day 39 (Friday August 7): Iran and Oman announced the finalization of the bilateral dual-lane routing framework — the geographic coordinates for the two-lane system (one Oman-flag commercial lane, one Iranian-flag lane) have been set, and Iran's parliament is advancing a traffic-regulation bill that would provide the domestic legal authorization for the arrangement to operate. Bloomberg and ABC News both confirmed the framework finalization Friday morning. The complication, stated explicitly by Iran's Deputy Foreign Minister, is that the Strait of Hormuz will not reopen while the United States maintains its naval blockade on Iranian ports — a blockade that Iran characterizes as an existential economic constraint and that the US characterizes as enforcement of the maximum-pressure sanctions regime reinstated following the February Iran-US escalation. CNN's diplomatic analysis described the situation as "not one Trump wants": the bilateral Iran-Oman framework is a mechanism Iran can control, since it defines the terms on its own sovereign waters and with a friendly neighboring state, rather than operating under a UNSC resolution that would formally establish freedom-of-navigation norms across the Strait under international law. The US preference — a multilateral UNSC-backed reopening that sets binding precedent — has been outmaneuvered by Iran's bilateral strategy, but Iran's own reopening is blocked by the same US pressure the bilateral strategy was designed to sidestep. UNSC vote: scheduled August 17 (9 days). Resolution language being negotiated. If the UNSC votes for a freedom-of-navigation resolution, it creates either a framework for US blockade withdrawal or a clear international-law violation by Iran for keeping the Strait closed despite a binding UN mandate. If the resolution fails (Russia/China veto scenario), the bilateral path remains the only mechanism in play. WTI $77.08, Brent $82.15 — oil has fallen 7% from the conflict peak but has not priced full reopening, consistent with a market that sees the framework as progress but the US naval blockade as a durable impasse. The practical assessment: the dual-lane framework is the closest the Hormuz situation has come to a defined reopening mechanism in 39 days of closure. Its limitation is that it describes how traffic would move rather than what makes traffic move. The US naval blockade is the final obstacle because no framework can be implemented while Iran's ports are under a US-enforced shipping denial — the commercial shipping companies that would use the Oman lane cannot transit a Strait adjacent to US naval operations against Iranian ports without insurer liability complications that make the routes commercially non-executable. The framework is real. The reopening is still contingent.

The Zelensky-China development adds a second major world story to Saturday's edition: Ukraine's president has shifted toward direct engagement with Beijing, seeking to activate China's position as a brake on Russian military-industrial capacity in exchange for Ukrainian post-war reconstruction participation opportunities for Chinese firms. The strategic logic is that China has been the most consequential external variable in the Russia-Ukraine war because Beijing has maintained formal neutrality while being Russia's primary economic lifeline; Ukrainian engagement with China is an attempt to change that calculation by offering China a stake in the post-war order that Russia cannot match. Trump's administration has not endorsed the Zelensky-China engagement, and the relationship between Kyiv and Washington has been strained by disagreements over the ceasefire terms negotiated earlier this year.

The Hormuz dual-lane framework finalization on Friday represents the most concrete progress since the bilateral Iran-Oman channel was established on July 28 — but it does not represent reopening, and the specific language from Iran's Deputy Foreign Minister makes the remaining gap explicit: the Strait will not reopen while the United States maintains its naval blockade on Iranian ports, regardless of how complete the bilateral routing framework becomes. The framework describes the physical configuration of traffic once reopening is authorized; it does not constitute the authorization. Iran's position is that the authorization requires the blockade to be lifted first; the US position is that the blockade is leverage for a multilateral UN-backed resolution; and the bilateral framework Iran has built with Oman is precisely the kind of arrangement that sidesteps the multilateral structure the US needs as the political framework for lifting the blockade. CNN's analysis of the impasse — "not the framework Trump wants" — correctly identifies the tension: the bilateral deal is a diplomatic success for Iran precisely because it demonstrates that Hormuz can be resolved without a US-designed international framework, which reduces US leverage at the UNSC but doesn't resolve the US-Iran naval confrontation itself.

The parliamentary dimension adds a genuine complication that is separate from the US-Iran diplomatic standoff. Iran's parliament drafting a traffic-regulation bill is the domestic political authorization process that any Iranian executive agreement requires under Iran's constitutional structure — the Supreme Leader's office can direct foreign policy, but commercial maritime regulations have parliamentary authority implications. The bill's progress this week (drafting stage, not yet passed) suggests the timeline for domestic authorization is weeks, not days. If the parliamentary process completes before the UNSC vote on August 17, Iran will have both a bilateral framework and domestic legal authorization in place — and the sole outstanding issue will be whether the US naval blockade constitutes an acceptable condition for Iran's reopening announcement. If the parliamentary process takes longer, the UNSC vote and the parliamentary process converge on overlapping timelines with different resolution mechanisms.

The August 17 UNSC vote on a freedom-of-navigation resolution is the next hard deadline in the Hormuz timeline and will determine whether the multilateral architecture or the bilateral architecture becomes the operative resolution framework. The resolution as drafted calls for the restoration of free commercial passage through the Strait of Hormuz under international maritime law norms — language that Russia and China may characterize as a mechanism for legitimizing US naval operations in the Persian Gulf and may veto on those grounds, despite both countries having significant commercial shipping interests in Hormuz reopening. The veto scenario: if Russia and China vote no, the bilateral Iran-Oman framework remains the only mechanism, the US blockade remains the only leverage, and the conflict enters a prolonged standoff phase that oil markets have not fully priced (WTI at $77 implies a market that expects eventual resolution, not indefinite closure). The passage scenario: if the resolution passes, it creates political cover for the US to characterize blockade withdrawal as compliance with international law rather than capitulation to Iran, which is the domestic political framing that any US concession on the naval blockade would require.

WTI's week tells the resolution story more cleanly than any single diplomatic statement: from the week open through Wednesday's $74.79, the market priced progressive deal certainty; Thursday's $77.29 (▲3.4%) priced the Iran-parliament complication; Friday's $77.08 settled on the jobs-report macro override. The week's net move — approximately −3.6% from open — reflects genuine deal progress that has not been reversed despite the US-blockade impasse. The conflict premium remaining at approximately $3-4 above fully-reopened scenario pricing represents the market's residual probability that the blockade impasse extends beyond the August 17 UNSC window. The implication: each subsequent week of framework progress without blockade resolution is another week of $77 rather than the $73-74 that full reopening would likely price.

Ukrainian President Zelensky's shift toward direct engagement with China — seeking to activate Beijing as a brake on Russian military-industrial capacity rather than treating China as a passive neutral broker — represents a meaningful reorientation of Kyiv's diplomatic strategy that reflects the constraints Ukraine faces entering the second half of 2026: Western political support, while not withdrawn, shows signs of fatigue; Trump's administration has been vocally critical of the cost of Ukraine support; and the ceasefire that has held since April has not progressed toward a peace settlement that Ukraine finds acceptable in terms of territorial and security guarantees. The strategic logic of the China pivot is that Beijing is the only external actor with simultaneous credibility in both the Moscow and Kyiv diplomatic channels — China is Russia's primary economic lifeline (trade volume has tripled since 2022 Western sanctions), but China has also maintained formal neutrality, not provided lethal weapons to Russia, and signaled that a prolonged European war creates instability in global supply chains that China's export-dependent economy does not benefit from. Ukraine's offer: post-war reconstruction opportunities for Chinese infrastructure and manufacturing firms in exchange for Beijing actively pressuring Moscow to accept territorial terms that Kyiv can ratify.

The Trump-Zelensky relationship dimension is relevant context: Trump has described the Ukraine conflict as expensive, has characterized Zelensky as an inadequate dealmaker relative to what the US has invested, and has not committed to a new military-aid tranche since June. Zelensky's China pivot is partially a hedge against the scenario where US support becomes conditional or withdrawn, and partially a strategic move to activate the only large-power leverage against Russia that remains underused. European allies — Germany, France, Poland — are watching the Zelensky-China engagement carefully because a significant Chinese role in Ukraine reconstruction carries its own geopolitical implications (Chinese infrastructure investment in Eastern Europe is a separate concern from China's value in pressuring Russia). The ceasefire that began April 8 is holding as of Saturday, but peace negotiations have stalled on territorial and security-guarantee questions that neither Russia nor Ukraine has yet moved on publicly.

Quick hits

  • The US naval blockade's role as the final Hormuz obstacle deserves a structural explanation because it has been reported primarily as a diplomatic disagreement but operates as a commercial logistics problem. Commercial shipping companies and their insurers will not commit vessels to transit routes adjacent to active naval operations against a port they may need to use — the liability exposure from operating under a US-enforced shipping denial regime is too significant for standard maritime insurance policies to cover without sovereign guarantees that no insurer provides commercially. The Iran-Oman bilateral dual-lane framework describes a physical routing mechanism, but it cannot become commercially operational while the US naval presence makes the insured transit commercially non-executable. This means the gap between "framework finalized" and "commercial shipping resumes" is not a 24-hour announcement gap — it is a multi-week operational gap that requires (at minimum) clear insurance-market guidance from Lloyd's of London and equivalent bodies that the routes are insurable, which requires the US naval blockade to be verifiably lifted before the first commercial vessel transits under the new framework. Oil markets at $77 are already partially pricing this delay — the conflict premium remaining above full-reopening pricing reflects the time-to-commercial-operation lag even in the optimistic scenario where the US-Iran blockade question resolves favorably at or after August 17.
  • The Zelensky-China engagement timing is not coincidental relative to the US domestic political context. Trump's re-election coalition includes a significant "America First" faction that views continued Ukraine support as a European burden that the US should not disproportionately carry; within the Republican caucus, Ukraine-skeptic voices have become more vocal since the ceasefire (the argument being: ceasefire achieved, US should wind down its exposure). Zelensky's pivot to China is partly a signal to Washington that Ukraine has strategic alternatives — that Kyiv is not solely dependent on US goodwill — which is a negotiating posture as much as a genuine diplomatic reorientation. The Trump administration has not commented publicly on the China engagement. European capitals have noted it carefully. Russia has described it as evidence that Zelensky lacks loyalty to his patrons — which is characteristically the response of a party that does not want the other side to gain diplomatic options.
