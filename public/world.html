The Anchor · Day 63 Tanker Attack — Hormuz Has Two Tracks and Both Are Escalating

Monday produced the Hormuz crisis's most significant military event: direct U.S.-Iran territorial exchange, a threshold 62 days of crisis had not crossed. The most important question for Tuesday was whether Monday's exchange represented a bounded warning round — a signal sent and received — or the beginning of sustained direct conflict. The Day 63 tanker attack answers part of that question. Strait commerce targeting did not pause after Monday's Larak Island exchange and Iranian retaliation. The tanker struck near Oman by three projectiles Tuesday was completing outbound transit — the same type of operation that has been targeted throughout the 63-day crisis. The disruption campaign is continuing on its own momentum even as the direct military exchange track opened Monday. That means the Hormuz crisis now has two simultaneous escalating tracks rather than one. The June memorandum of understanding, which was supposed to provide a framework for managed Strait passage, has been violated by both sides according to reporting from the crisis period. The Pakistan-mediated negotiating channel that produced the June MOU — and that offered the best diplomatic path to a durable de-escalation framework — is under its most severe stress since the crisis resumed in July. The tanker attack Tuesday is not just a supply disruption event; it is a signal that Iran has not agreed to a de-escalation framework as a consequence of Monday's Larak Island exchange. What that means for the next 48-72 hours depends on U.S. and Iranian decision-making that is not publicly visible. The most important observable indicator is whether U.S. forces conduct follow-on military action in Iran, and whether Iran conducts further strikes on U.S. infrastructure beyond Jordan and the UAE. If both sides pause and the diplomatic channel re-engages, Monday was a bounded exchange. If either side acts again, the direct confrontation track has entered a new sustained phase. Brent at $91.28 prices the uncertainty of that question, not its resolution.

The ICC arrest warrants issued Monday for both Vladimir Putin and Benjamin Netanyahu — the first simultaneous warrants for a sitting major-power leader and a sitting Western-allied head of government — continued generating political fallout Tuesday. Western governments that are members of the ICC face a legal obligation to arrest either leader if they enter member-state territory; the political consequences of that obligation are irreconcilable with the alliance relationships those governments maintain with both Russia (where they seek accountability via the ICC) and Israel (where they support a leader who now faces a warrant). The result is an institutional crisis for the ICC itself: if the warrants cannot be executed because of political protection from member states, the court's enforcement credibility is permanently damaged.

The practical near-term consequence is geographic: Putin and Netanyahu are both effectively limited in their international travel to non-ICC member states — roughly 40 countries, predominantly in Asia, the Gulf, and Africa — for as long as the warrants remain active. For Putin, already subject to a 2023 ICC warrant over Ukraine, the additional Netanyahu-era warrant is relatively incremental; for Netanyahu, this is a new and significant constraint that arrives at a moment of acute domestic political pressure over the West Bank settler violence that Netanyahu simultaneously condemned on Monday. The warrants place the ICC at the center of geopolitical fault lines it was not designed to navigate.

Russian aerial strikes on Ukrainian territory continued Tuesday, maintaining the sustained infrastructure-targeting campaign that has run through the latter part of August. Russian diplomatic signals — including a recent statement from Deputy Foreign Minister Ryabkov indicating conceptual flexibility on potential negotiations — continue to run on a separate track from the military campaign, creating the same parallel-tracks dynamic that characterizes both the Hormuz crisis's commerce disruption and military confrontation tracks. Military pressure and diplomatic signaling are not incompatible in the current moment; they are simultaneous instruments with different audiences and timeframes. Iran's cyber operations against U.S. water sector infrastructure, reported over recent weeks, also continued in the background.

The West Bank settler violence from Monday's Qusra incident continued to draw international criticism Tuesday, arriving in a context newly complicated by the ICC arrest warrant for Netanyahu. The convergence is specific: Netanyahu publicly condemned the settler attack on Monday and called the perpetrators lawbreakers; on the same day, the ICC issued an arrest warrant for him in connection with the Gaza conflict. The combination places Israeli internal security failures and international legal accountability in the same news cycle, amplifying the pressure on a government that faces mounting criticism from both its settlement-policy critics internationally and its settler-community supporters domestically. The political pressure from both directions simultaneously is historically unusual for an Israeli government.
