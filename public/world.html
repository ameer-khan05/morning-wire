The Anchor · The Winter Grid War — What It Means That Zelenskyy Is Warning of an Imminent Power Grid Attack While Repair Crews Are Already in Six Regions

Russia’s strategy for Ukraine’s third winter of the war is not new — infrastructure attacks designed to maximize civilian suffering — but the intelligence warning and the concurrent repair operations give Friday’s grid story a specific urgency. Zelenskyy’s warning that Ukrainian intelligence has identified an imminent major attack is not a general caution; it reflects specific signals about Russian targeting and timing. That warning arrives while repair crews are already working in six regions to restore power from the most recent 113-drone overnight strike. The pattern is relentless: Russia attacks, Ukraine repairs, Russia attacks again. Each cycle costs Ukraine engineering capacity, materials, and civilian resilience. The EU’s €45 billion loan is the financial instrument designed to sustain Ukraine through this cycle — but it is not approved, and in Brussels it is now tied to the EU’s 2028–2034 budget negotiations, meaning the disbursement timeline is linked to a multi-year political negotiation that has nothing inherently to do with Ukraine’s immediate energy needs. Russia’s sabotage of European targets — infrastructure explosions and arson that have roughly doubled — is the parallel operation: not to destroy European infrastructure, but to raise the political and economic cost of European support for Kyiv until European governments recalculate whether it is worth it. The grid attacks, the EU loan delay, and the European sabotage campaign are not three separate stories. They are three components of the same strategy.

A 113-drone overnight strike while repair crews are already in six regions is the operational rhythm Russia has sustained for three winters: attack capacity exceeds Ukraine’s ability to simultaneously defend and repair. Each cycle costs Ukraine engineering materials, labor, and civilian resilience. EU leaders continued Friday discussions in Brussels over a €45 billion loan package for Ukraine — a financing vehicle intended to fund Ukraine’s military and infrastructure repair needs through the winter and into 2027 — with the talks complicated by their linkage to negotiations over the EU’s 2028–2034 multi-year budget framework, a seven-year budget package that involves trade-offs between EU member states that are largely unrelated to Ukraine’s immediate security situation; the budget linkage creates a disbursement risk: Ukraine’s power grid is under attack now, its repair needs are immediate, and the loan’s availability is conditional on a political negotiation with a multi-month resolution timeline; and Ukraine has separately used domestically produced long-range weapons to strike targets in Russia, and struck a Russian naval vessel docked at a port in Libya using sea drones, in an expansion of the operational theater that has alarmed some European leaders by suggesting the conflict is extending beyond Ukraine’s borders.

Tying the Ukraine loan to EU budget negotiations is politically understandable — member states use budget negotiations to extract concessions — but it creates a structural lag between Ukraine’s security needs and the EU’s financial response. The alternative — a standalone emergency loan outside the budget framework — requires unanimous agreement and is typically slower, not faster. The linkage is an attempt to accelerate disbursement by embedding it in a negotiation that must conclude; the risk is that budget disagreements delay Ukraine funding along with everything else.

Alleged Russia-directed sabotage operations in Europe have roughly doubled over the past year — with the use of explosives and arson against infrastructure, logistics nodes, and critical services accelerating as Moscow engages in conflict via proxies intended to weaken European governments’ political will to support Kyiv — with Friday reporting from Geopolitical Briefings and Politics Explained citing the sustained trend across European countries as a campaign distinct from conventional military operations; the operations use recruited proxies in European countries rather than Russian nationals, complicating attribution and prosecution; and the campaign’s dual function — imposing economic disruption and raising the political cost of Ukraine support — is consistent with the pattern Russia has used in hybrid warfare contexts where it cannot or will not engage militarily directly with NATO member states.

Sabotage that has doubled over a year is not an ad hoc response to specific events — it is a systematically scaled campaign. The doubling implies organizational infrastructure: recruitment networks, logistics, communication, and payment systems to run proxy operations across multiple European countries simultaneously. European counterintelligence agencies have been aware of this campaign for most of its duration; the Friday reporting formalizes what has been a persistent but under-covered security threat alongside the kinetic Ukraine conflict.

The COP31 United Nations climate conference collapsed on November 22 after two days of overtime negotiations failed to produce a final agreement — a failure that received minimal coverage as the Thanksgiving news cycle dominated global media through the week; the collapse continues a recent pattern of UN climate conferences ending without binding outcomes, with the COP31 breakdown attributed to the same fault lines — developed versus developing country financing commitments, fossil fuel phase-out language, and loss-and-damage fund adequacy — that have blocked progress at previous conferences; and Australia and the Solomon Islands signed a formal bilateral security treaty in Canberra on Friday, with Prime Minister Wale and Australian Premier Albanese formalizing Australia as the archipelago’s primary security partner — a significant Pacific security arrangement that follows years of competition between Australia, the United States, and China for influence in the Solomon Islands after Honiara’s 2022 security agreement with Beijing raised alarm in Western capitals.

The Australia-Solomon Islands treaty is a material shift in Pacific security architecture. The Solomon Islands’ 2022 China security deal was the event that catalyzed Western concern about Beijing’s Pacific access strategy; the new Australian treaty formalizing primary partnership represents a successful counter-move. Whether the Solomon Islands formally terminates or allows the China agreement to lapse — or maintains both simultaneously — will determine whether this week’s treaty represents exclusive alignment or a hedging posture.
