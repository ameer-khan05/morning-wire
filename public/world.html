The Anchor · The Kerch Bridge Collapse — Why Ukraine Chose Tuesday and What It Changes Before Thursday’s Shenzhen Summit

Ukraine’s drone-and-missile strike that collapsed the Kerch Bridge’s main road arch on Tuesday is the most significant single military operation Ukraine has conducted against Russian territory since the war began — and its timing, two days before the Shenzhen summit, is not accidental. The Kerch Bridge connects Russia to occupied Crimea. It is not primarily a military logistics route at this stage of the war — its strategic importance is symbolic and political: it is Vladimir Putin’s most visible infrastructure achievement in Crimea and a daily demonstration that Russia controls the peninsula. Ukraine has previously damaged the rail line on the bridge; Tuesday’s operation — which Zelenskyy described as requiring weeks of planning by Ukraine’s defense forces, security services, and weapons manufacturers — brought down one metal arch and the road span beneath it, suspending vehicle traffic on the nearly 12-mile crossing. The Shenzhen context is inseparable from the operation’s timing. The Trump-Xi-Putin summit on Thursday is the first meeting where Ukraine’s future could conceivably be discussed by all three leaders in one room, without Ukraine in the room. Zelenskyy warned Saturday that the summit could become a platform for decisions about Ukraine without Kyiv’s participation. The Kerch Bridge collapse is Ukraine’s way of participating in a week from which it has been diplomatically excluded: the most dramatic single military operation of the war, executed two days before the summit, ensures that any discussion of Ukraine at Shenzhen happens in the context of an adversary that just collapsed a bridge on Russian-annexed territory. The operation also forces Russia to respond militarily or absorb the loss publicly — either response complicates Putin’s posture at Shenzhen, where he is meeting as a party seeking international legitimacy.

Iran’s IRGC launched drones at Bahrain’s Sheikh Isa Air Base on Tuesday — its third strike on the base in five days after a roughly two-month lull in Gulf strikes that began when the third US carrier group arrived in CENTCOM — and CENTCOM intercepted all incoming drones without damage to US equipment or injury to American personnel; NPR reported the IRGC said its drones carried out a “precision operation” targeting a US radar system at the base in response to “repeated violations of Iranian airspace by US military drones,” a framing that presents each attack as reactive rather than initiatory; Washington Times confirmed the attack using KSAT’s reporting that it “ends a 2-month lull with Iran’s third strike on US targets in Bahrain,” with the five-day frequency following the lull suggesting Iran’s drone operations restarted in a compressed window rather than gradually.

Three consecutive intercepted strikes with no damage tells the market one thing: CENTCOM’s interception rate against Iran’s current drone capabilities is effectively 100% over five days. That operational reality is reflected in Brent’s 4.2% decline on the same day Iran attacked again — the market is pricing the interception rate, not the attack rate. Iran’s continued attacks into a 100% interception environment suggests its objective is not to cause damage but to maintain a signaling posture ahead of Shenzhen, where the conflict’s diplomatic resolution is on Thursday’s agenda.

Ukraine’s drone-and-missile strike brought down the main road arch of the Kerch Bridge on Tuesday — collapsing one metal arch and the road span beneath it and suspending traffic across the nearly 12-mile Crimea crossing — in what President Zelenskyy described as an “unprecedented operation” requiring weeks of planning by Ukraine’s defense forces, security services, and weapons manufacturers; Military.com characterized the operation as a strike “two days before Putin meets Trump and Xi,” and CNBC confirmed the bridge’s road vehicle span was destroyed by a combination of Ukrainian drones and missiles in the second consecutive day of Kerch attacks; Ukraine’s Security Service said one metal arch and the road span beneath it collapsed on the crossing — a more significant structural damage than Ukraine’s previous attacks on the bridge’s rail section, which remained operational while the road span was under repair.

The road arch collapse is structurally different from Ukraine’s previous Kerch attacks. The earlier rail attack disrupted logistics but left vehicle transit intact. A road arch collapse suspends vehicle traffic entirely — a bridge that served as Putin’s most visible Crimea infrastructure achievement is now a bridge with no functional road crossing. The two-days-before-Shenzhen timing ensures that every image from Thursday’s summit — every handshake between Xi, Trump, and Putin — is printed against the backdrop of a bridge whose road span collapsed 48 hours earlier.
