The Anchor · The US Airstrikes on Iran — The First Direct Strike on Iranian Territory and What Three Carriers in CENTCOM Signal

The US launched airstrikes on Iranian missile launchers in southern Iran on Monday — the most direct US military action on Iranian territory since the conflict began in March 2026. This is a qualitative escalation, not a continuation. The US has struck Iranian proxy forces, Iranian-backed installations, and Iranian military assets in third countries throughout the conflict. Monday’s airstrikes on missile launchers inside southern Iran itself are categorically different — they are the first strikes on Iranian sovereign territory and represent a decision by the administration to respond to the Bahrain attack with a signal that is unmistakable: Iran’s launch infrastructure inside Iran is now within the range of US operational response. The trigger was Iran’s second attack on Bahrain’s Riffa Air Base in three days. Riffa hosts US and coalition forces — it is a US military partner base, not a Bahraini-only installation. The IRGC’s decision to attack it twice in three days, after the first attack was intercepted, represents Iran’s own escalation decision: the first attack tested whether Bahrain’s defenses would hold; the second tested whether the US would respond with proportional force. The US answer — airstrikes inside southern Iran — is a response that changes the conflict’s geographic scope. For the three carrier groups now in CENTCOM: they are not in theater to watch the exchange. They are there because the administration anticipated the exchange and pre-positioned the response capability. The gap between Sunday’s supertanker sinking and Monday’s direct US airstrike on Iran is 24 hours. The pace of escalation is accelerating.

Estonian Foreign Minister Margus Tsahkna announced Monday that Estonia will close all border crossings with Russia from December 1, citing a “failed sabotage attempt targeting an Estonian defense industry company” as the trigger — making Estonia the latest NATO member to implement a full border closure with Russia following similar decisions by Finland and Norway earlier in 2026; the closure comes in the same week that Ukraine launched its biggest overnight drone attack on Russia since the war began and the first-ever Putin-Xi-Trump trilateral summit is scheduled for Shenzhen Thursday — a week in which the Russia-Europe security boundary is being drawn more sharply at the same time Putin meets Trump and Xi; Tsahkna did not provide specifics about the sabotage attempt, but the defense industry targeting follows a pattern of Russian sabotage operations against NATO member defense infrastructure that European intelligence services have been tracking since late 2025.

Estonia’s full border closure with Russia removes one of the last open land crossings between the EU and Russia outside of exceptional circumstances. For travelers and cargo, it formalizes a separation that has been de facto for months. For NATO’s security posture, it removes a vulnerability — the same border that hosted the failed sabotage attempt — and signals that the Baltic states are treating the Russian threat as a persistent operational reality, not a contingency.
