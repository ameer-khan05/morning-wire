Day 37 (Thu Aug 6): Iran-Oman Hormuz framework status: Bloomberg reported Thursday morning that Iran and Oman have agreed coordinates for new commercial shipping routes through the strait — the "agreed in principle" characterization that represents the clearest formal progress yet in the 37-day conflict. Iranian Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmail Baghaei confirmed the agreement's existence and said the joint statement from Tehran and Muscat is "in final drafting." The framework's structure: Iran and Oman have negotiated a plan for maritime traffic management through the strait — specifying designated commercial shipping lanes, transit protocols, and the administrative framework under which Iran would allow commercial vessels to transit. This resolves the technical dimension of the deal. What remains unresolved: Iran's two explicit conditions for opening the strait — first, the US must remove its naval blockade of the Persian Gulf; second, the US must halt all strikes on Iranian infrastructure. Baghaei told reporters that the deal "would not end the crisis in the region" — meaning the broader nuclear dispute between the US and Iran is not included in this shipping-framework agreement and will continue regardless of the strait's reopening status. Oil market interpretation: WTI at $74.79 continued declining despite the unmet conditions, telling you that commodity traders read Iran's conditions as negotiating posture rather than a hard veto — a reading consistent with the pattern of Iranian-US negotiations in which stated conditions are routinely adjusted in the final stages when deal completion is the dominant objective of all parties. Ukraine developments: President Zelenskyy signed 10-year defense partnership agreements with three Gulf states during a regional diplomatic tour — a meaningful expansion of Ukraine's security architecture that moves beyond the NATO-anchored Coalition of the Willing to include Gulf states whose strategic interests in countering Russian influence in the region provide a basis for sustained defense cooperation. EU High Representative Kaja Kallas publicly characterized the Ukraine and Iran conflicts as "very much interlinked" through Russia — specifically, that Russia is using the Iran crisis as a strategic instrument to divert Western diplomatic and military attention from Ukraine's front lines. Kallas's framing confirms what the Ukrainian government has argued throughout the Iran conflict's 37 days: every day that the US diplomatic bandwidth is focused on Witkoff's Iran-channel negotiations is a day that Ukraine's intensifying air defense needs are receiving subordinate attention from the same officials who could be accelerating coalition support. The "agreed in principle" characterization is doing significant diplomatic work in today's reporting. In diplomatic language, "agreed in principle" means the substantive framework is in place — the what and how of the deal — but the formal announcement awaits the resolution of outstanding conditions. Iran's conditions (end blockade, halt strikes) are the outstanding items; whether the US accepts them as stated, negotiates modified versions, or allows them to lapse as the deal moves forward is the question the next 24-48 hours will answer. The oil market's continued $74.79 pricing tells you that sophisticated traders have effectively already resolved that question: they are pricing the conditions as resolved in the deal's final form, not as obstacles that could unravel the agreement. The UNSC August 17 deadline — 11 days away — is the remaining forcing function that incentivizes both governments to finalize before the Security Council session creates additional international pressure on both parties' nuclear positions.

The UN's World Economic Situation and Prospects 2026 estimate of 2.7% global growth — below 2025's 2.8% and the pre-pandemic 3.2% average — was built around the assumption that Hormuz's closure would persist through at least mid-2026. A formal reopening this week would begin the process of revising those estimates upward, as the three primary growth drags from the conflict (trade disruption, commodity elevation, geopolitical risk premium) all begin unwinding simultaneously upon the strait's reopening.

Iran's Foreign Ministry immediately stressed that the deal's maritime scope does not address the broader nuclear dispute, which Tehran insists is a separate negotiating track — meaning the Hormuz reopening, when it occurs, would close the maritime chapter of the conflict while leaving the nuclear dimension for the UNSC August 17 session and whatever diplomatic framework follows.

The oil market's interpretation — WTI continuing its decline to $74.79 despite the unmet conditions — is the most reliable real-time read on whether professional commodity traders believe Iran's conditions are a final obstacle or a negotiating position. At $74.79, WTI is pricing the conditions as negotiating position: the market has decided that the conditions, while real, are the type of face-saving requirement that Iran consistently raises in final deal stages and that consistently gets resolved through the Oman intermediation channel without collapsing the agreement. The alternative — that Iran's conditions are a genuine veto that will block the deal — is what $80+ WTI would be pricing; $74.79 tells you the market has settled the question in the conditions-as-margin direction.

President Zelenskyy signed 10-year defense partnership agreements with three Gulf states during a regional diplomatic tour Thursday — an initiative that reflects Ukraine's deliberate strategy of expanding its security architecture beyond the NATO-anchored Coalition of the Willing framework to include Gulf partners whose strategic interests in countering Russian influence in the region create a basis for sustained defense cooperation; the Gulf states' motivations for partnering with Ukraine are distinct from NATO members' — not collective defense obligations but shared strategic interest in preventing Russia from establishing a dominant position in a regional security environment that Gulf governments view as directly threatening to their own stability. EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas publicly connected the Ukraine and Iran conflicts in terms that the Ukrainian government has long argued but that Western officials have been reluctant to state directly: Russia, she said, has strategically used the Iran crisis to divert Western diplomatic and military attention from Ukraine's front lines, reducing the bandwidth available for coalition coordination, weapons delivery coordination, and diplomatic pressure on Moscow.

The connection Kallas drew between the two conflicts is structural rather than conspiratorial: Russia benefits from any geopolitical crisis that consumes the US diplomatic, military, and political capital that would otherwise be available to support Ukraine. The Iran-Hormuz conflict's 37 days have been 37 days during which Witkoff's diplomatic attention, Bessent's market-communication bandwidth, and the US intelligence community's focus have been partially redirected from Ukraine-adjacent priorities — NATO solidarity signaling, weapons delivery coordination, diplomatic engagement with Russia's potential mediators — to the Iran-Oman-US three-way negotiation. The Hormuz deal's completion, whenever it arrives, would begin reversing that bandwidth allocation.

Al Jazeera's Thursday analysis of each side's current positions provides the clearest publicly available map of what remains unresolved in the Hormuz framework: the US wants full commercial shipping restoration through the strait — including for vessels transiting to and from Israel and US-allied Gulf states — and has signaled through UNSC agenda-setting that it expects some Iranian nuclear transparency concession before the August 17 Security Council session; Iran wants the US to end its naval blockade of the Persian Gulf (which US forces have maintained since the conflict's outbreak), halt all US strikes on Iranian infrastructure (including the air campaign that has targeted Iranian missile and drone production facilities), and receive meaningful sanctions relief that would begin restoring Iranian oil export revenues; Oman is mediating both tracks through the same back-channel architecture that produced the June MOU framework and has been the operational hub of every substantive negotiating progress in 37 days.

The nuclear component's deferral from the maritime deal to the UNSC framework is the structural compromise that makes the Hormuz maritime agreement possible: by explicitly excluding the nuclear dispute from this deal's scope, Iran can accept the maritime arrangement without conceding any nuclear-related ground, while the US can accept the maritime deal as a first step toward a broader normalization that includes nuclear transparency discussions at the UNSC. The UNSC's August 17 session becomes the next pressure point — both governments have an incentive to arrive at that session with a maritime deal already in place, because a session held with Hormuz still formally contested creates more pressure and less flexibility for both parties than a session held after the maritime dispute is resolved.

Quick hits

  • The phrase "agreed in principle" carries specific diplomatic weight that distinguishes it from "close to agreement" or "making progress." In diplomatic practice, "agreed in principle" signals that the substantive content — the what and how — is settled, leaving only the formal adoption process and any explicitly stated conditions. When Iranian and Omani officials confirm this characterization to Bloomberg, they are not predicting a future outcome; they are describing the current state of the negotiation as one in which the deal's content is no longer being negotiated and the remaining steps are formal. The Iranian FM's statement that a joint statement is "in final drafting" is consistent with this characterization — final drafting is the stage before a joint announcement, not the stage of substantive negotiation. The remaining variable is Iran's conditions, which are attached to the deal's implementation timeline rather than to its content.
  • Zelenskyy's Gulf defense partnerships deserve reading in the context of Ukraine's evolving security architecture strategy. After 4+ years of war, Ukraine has learned that security guarantees from NATO members — even the strongest bilateral commitments — do not substitute for direct security partnerships with states that have their own defense industrial bases, strategic interests in countering Russia, and the financial capacity to fund sustained defense cooperation. Gulf states — particularly those with advanced weapons procurement programs and exposure to Russian-backed regional actors (Iran, Houthi, Assad remnants) — have strategic reasons to want Ukraine to succeed that are independent of NATO solidarity. A 10-year defense partnership is a multi-administration commitment that hedges against political changes in any single NATO capital; having three Gulf states anchoring Ukraine's defense architecture over a 10-year horizon reduces the single-point-of-failure risk that US or European political transitions represent.
