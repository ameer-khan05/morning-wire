The Anchor · Abu Dhabi Nov 10–11 — The Fourth Round and the Most Consequential

The November 10–11 Abu Dhabi negotiations are the fourth round of formal US-brokered Ukraine-Russia talks since January — but they are the most consequential because of the conditions around them. The first three rounds occurred while the Iran conflict was frozen and geopolitical risk was more compartmentalized. This round occurs after the Iran war erupted, after the US midterms produced a Democratic congressional majority, and after a full week of market re-pricing has established that the world is watching. The structure of the Abu Dhabi talks reflects how complex the peace process has become: the US, Russia, and Ukraine are in trilateral negotiations, while European allies — Britain, France, Germany, Italy, NATO, and the European Commission — are in Abu Dhabi for parallel bilateral meetings with Ukraine that the European side insisted on after earlier rounds left European security guarantors out of the room. Trump saying the war will “end soon” and suggesting Ukraine’s borders “could change” before the talks begin is a pressure tactic or a preview of the US negotiating position — either interpretation alarmed European governments, which have consistently maintained that any peace framework must respect Ukraine’s territorial integrity under the UN Charter. The gap between Trump’s “borders could change” and Europe’s territorial integrity position is one of several structural tensions that will determine whether Abu Dhabi produces a ceasefire framework or a continuation of negotiations into a fifth round.

The United States confirmed November 10–11 as the date for the fourth round of formal Ukraine-Russia peace negotiations in Abu Dhabi, with US special envoy Steve Witkoff and senior adviser Jared Kushner representing Washington, Russia sending deputy foreign minister Vladimir Medinsky as its chief negotiator, and Ukrainian officials meeting separately with European allies — Britain, France, Germany, Italy, NATO, and the European Commission — in parallel meetings that the European side negotiated to maintain its seat in the security guarantee framework; President Trump stated Monday that he believed the war would end soon and suggested Ukraine’s borders “could change,” a statement that alarmed European governments which have maintained that any peace framework must respect Ukrainian territorial integrity under the UN Charter; the fourth round follows three prior sessions that established the basic negotiating framework but produced no ceasefire; the gap between the US position (“borders could change”) and the European position (territorial integrity) is the most significant structural tension in the talks going into Abu Dhabi.

Trump’s pre-talk “borders could change” statement is either a negotiating preview or a pressure tactic on Ukraine. If it is a preview, it represents a material US concession to Russia’s position before the talks begin. If it is a pressure tactic, it is designed to signal urgency to Ukraine. European governments, who cannot afford to be seen acquiescing to territorial changes, are alarmed by either interpretation.

Russian forces launched coordinated strikes on infrastructure in four Ukrainian regions — Kyiv, Dnipropetrovsk, Cherkasy, and Vinnytsia — on Monday, targeting power and energy systems in what Ukrainian officials described as a widening of the infrastructure attack pattern beyond the Kyiv-centric strikes of the previous days; Ukraine responded by striking the Kiroshi oil refinery in Russia’s Leningrad region, killing one person — a targeted industrial strike that follows Ukraine’s established pattern of retaliating against Russian energy infrastructure rather than population centers; the dual strike-and-retaliation pattern occurred on the same day that Abu Dhabi peace talks were confirmed for November 10–11, illustrating the fundamental asymmetry of the current phase: diplomatic negotiations and military operations are proceeding simultaneously, with neither side treating the confirmation of talks as a reason to pause kinetic activity; Russia imposes gasoline sales restrictions amid the Ukrainian refinery attacks, Russian media report, suggesting the cumulative pressure on Russian energy infrastructure from Ukrainian strikes is beginning to generate domestic supply-side effects.

Both sides striking each other’s energy infrastructure on the day peace talks are confirmed is not evidence that the talks are insincere — it is evidence that the talks and the fighting are on separate tracks. The logic of infrastructure strikes heading into winter is seasonal and strategic; the logic of peace talks is political and economic. The two tracks run in parallel until one produces a framework that changes the conditions for the other.

COP31 opened in Antalya, Turkey on Monday with 190+ nations gathering to negotiate the next phase of international fossil fuel production commitments — a conference that opened under the distinctive shadow of the Iran war, which has disrupted oil shipments through the Strait of Hormuz, raised global energy prices, and prompted some countries to increase coal use to manage the supply disruption; the Iran-driven energy price elevation complicates the conference’s central task of building political momentum for fossil fuel production phase-downs, since the near-term cost of reducing fossil fuel supply is higher when geopolitical disruptions are already constraining supply; the UAE, Saudi Arabia, and other major fossil fuel producers are in Antalya with domestic incentives to protect production capacity; the conference’s geopolitical context — Iran war, Ukraine conflict, and Abu Dhabi peace talks running in parallel — makes this the most energy-market-consequential COP since the 2021 Glasgow sessions.

COP31 trying to negotiate fossil fuel production phase-downs while the Iran war is disrupting Hormuz is the week’s most structurally ironic geopolitical juxtaposition. The conference is designed to reduce long-term fossil fuel supply; the Iran war is reducing near-term fossil fuel supply in a way that makes the conference’s goal politically harder, not easier, because high energy prices increase the political cost of voluntary production cuts.

The Iran war has disrupted oil shipments through the Strait of Hormuz — the world’s most critical oil transit chokepoint, handling approximately 20% of global oil trade — raising energy prices and prompting countries that depend on Hormuz-routed supply to increase coal use and seek alternative LNG supply routes as the Iran-related disruption has lasted longer than energy markets initially anticipated; the disruption is part of the reason Brent has held above $90 since late February and why Monday’s 5% decline — on de-escalation signals — brought it to its lowest level since the conflict began; Trump’s Iran standoff decision remains the geopolitical x-factor that has not been resolved: a US decision to de-escalate with Iran would likely reduce Hormuz disruptions and push Brent below $85 within days, but no such decision has been announced despite weeks of market speculation that it was imminent; the Abu Dhabi talks focus on Ukraine rather than Iran, but the two conflicts’ oil price implications are running in parallel.

Iran is the missing piece in Monday’s de-escalation story. The oil price moved on the Ukraine peace talks and COP31, but the larger Hormuz disruption — which is the Iran war’s most direct economic consequence — has not been addressed. If the Iran standoff resolves, Brent could fall another $5-$10 from Monday’s $88.35. That is the second oil trade the market is waiting for.
