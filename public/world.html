The Anchor · New Delhi Declaration — How India Got Iran and the UAE to Sign the Same Document

The unanimous adoption of the BRICS New Delhi Declaration on Saturday — despite the Iran-UAE split that threatened to fracture the bloc's consensus framework — is the single most significant Indian diplomatic achievement of the 2026 BRICS chairship. The two outcomes that appeared structurally impossible entering the summit both materialized: a joint declaration was adopted, and Iran and the UAE co-signed it. The declaration's language was the mechanism: it called for restraint in the Middle East without condemning the US-Israeli strikes that Iran suffered or the Iranian missile attacks that prompted the UAE to suspend trade with Tehran. Both sides could claim the language reflected their position, and both signed rather than fracturing the bloc. India's strategy throughout the summit was to make the cost of non-signing higher than the cost of signing an imperfect document. The precedent from May's foreign ministers' meeting — where India issued a chair's statement rather than a joint communique after consensus failed — had established that India would not allow a failure to produce a communique to be interpreted as a bloc-wide endorsement of any party's position. That precedent gave both Iran and the UAE a reason to sign a declaration that acknowledged differences rather than allowing India's chair's statement to represent the consensus. The declaration's language on the Middle East conflict — calling for restraint, not assigning blame — is diplomatic boilerplate that neither Iran nor the UAE can credibly object to while still signaling disagreement to their respective audiences through other channels. Former Foreign Secretary Nirupama Menon Rao described it as a "significant diplomatic success" because getting two active adversaries to sign the same multilateral document while the conflict between them is ongoing is, in fact, significantly more than the May precedent suggested was achievable. The China-US Taiwan development running simultaneously is a counterpoint: even as India demonstrated that BRICS can maintain coherence across an active member-on-member conflict, the other major multilateral relationship of the week — the Trump-Xi summit — is under direct pressure from an arms sale that Beijing has characterized as a red line. The $14 billion Taiwan arms package, if approved by the US before September 24, would give China a stated pretext to cancel the meeting. Whether the Trump administration approves the sale before the summit is both a policy decision and a diplomatic calculation about which bilateral relationship it prioritizes in the near term.

The BRICS New Delhi Declaration was adopted unanimously on Saturday — Day 1 of the two-day summit — with all 11 members including both Iran and the UAE signing the joint document, which called for restraint in the Middle East while avoiding direct condemnation of either the US-Israeli strikes or Iranian missile attacks. Former Indian Foreign Secretary Nirupama Menon Rao described it as a "significant diplomatic success" for India's chairship: getting two adversarial members to co-sign the same multilateral declaration while their conflict is active is, as Al Jazeera noted, more than the May foreign ministers' meeting had suggested was achievable. The declaration also criticized the global trade war without naming the United States. Day 2 today sees Prime Minister Modi holding bilateral meetings with leaders of seven countries, including Ethiopia's Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed.

The New Delhi Declaration's diplomatic significance lies in what it demonstrated about BRICS's structural resilience: that a bloc including active adversaries can maintain formal consensus through sufficiently general language. The May foreign ministers' meeting had set a pessimistic precedent — India issued a chair's statement rather than a joint communique after consensus failed. The heads-of-state summit's unanimous adoption shows that at the principals level, the political cost of fracturing the bloc outweighed the benefit of holding out for stronger language. For Iran, the declaration provides multilateral legitimacy at a moment of deepening diplomatic isolation. For the UAE, it provides a signal that BRICS membership offers economic and diplomatic benefits worth preserving even when bloc members disagree. For India, it establishes the chairship as a diplomatic achievement. The Brent crude price — still $107.40 — says the oil market is not treating the BRICS declaration as a ceasefire signal. That is the correct reading: a diplomatic communique calling for restraint is not a ceasefire mechanism.

Sweden's general election — covering the 349-seat Riksdag plus 21 regional and 290 municipal councils simultaneously — is underway today, September 13, with the Red-Green bloc polling at 50.8% against the Tidö coalition at 47.6% in the final pre-election survey by Indikator for Swedish Radio. The 3.2 percentage point gap is within polling uncertainty for a simultaneous multi-level election, making this the tightest major European vote of the autumn 2026 season. First preliminary results from the vote count are expected at approximately 21:00 Central European Time tonight. The current Tidö government has governed since 2022 in a right-wing arrangement supported by the Sweden Democrats; a Red-Green victory would end that arrangement and produce Sweden's first left-leaning government since 2022.

Sweden's election carries European strategic significance beyond its domestic governance. Sweden joined NATO in March 2024 and is a full Alliance member whose government's posture on Russia, the Middle East conflict, and EU sanctions policy will affect NATO's consensus calculations directly. A Red-Green government governed differently from the current coalition on immigration policy and the humanitarian dimensions of Middle East engagement; those differences would become operationally relevant in the context of a NATO that is actively managing its response to the Iran war and the ongoing Ukraine conflict. The election results tonight will give European capitals their first governance data point after the BRICS summit concluded earlier today — a simultaneous read on how the Global South is managing the Iran war (BRICS) and how Western Europe is governing itself (Sweden) within 24 hours of each other.

China has threatened to cancel the September 24 summit between President Trump and President Xi Jinping if the United States approves new arms deals for Taiwan before then — warning that the Taiwan issue is a "red line" and that Beijing will take "countermeasures" if the proposed $14 billion arms package proceeds. Taiwan has indicated that its next US arms purchase is on track after receiving a guarantee, putting the arms sale on a collision course with the summit date. The Taipei Times reports China's position Sunday: the bilateral meeting, the most significant US-China diplomatic engagement of 2026, would be canceled if the administration approves the sale in the eleven days before its planned date.

The Trump administration's use of the Taiwan arms sale as a diplomatic bargaining chip is consistent with its negotiating approach to major bilateral relationships: the arms sale creates leverage before the summit that would be lost after it. The calculation for Washington is whether the summit's diplomatic value — covering trade, AI, the Iran war's regional implications, and potential agreements on fentanyl precursors — outweighs the benefit of proceeding with the arms sale on the Taiwan policy's stated timeline. The calculation for Beijing is whether threatening to cancel the summit actually prevents the sale, or whether it simply signals that the threat is the limit of what China will do before the summit itself. Taiwan is watching both: a sale proceeding on schedule signals US commitment to Taiwan's security regardless of China's diplomatic pressure; a delay signals that diplomatic considerations affected the timeline. The 11-day window before the September 24 summit is the resolution period for all three parties' calculations.
