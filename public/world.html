The Anchor · Mokha Seized — How the Houthis' Bab el-Mandab Foothold Changes the Oil Supply Math

The Houthi seizure of Mokha on September 11 is the single most consequential territorial development of the Iran war for global oil supply, not because it controls oil infrastructure directly, but because of where it is. Since the conflict began and Houthis began interdicting Red Sea shipping, the Bab el-Mandab threat has been a naval harassment capability: missiles and drones launched from inland Yemeni positions, capable of attacking but not controlling shipping near the strait. Mokha, located less than 50 miles from the Bab el-Mandab, changes the threat geometry fundamentally. From Mokha, Houthis can deploy shore-based anti-ship missiles with direct line of sight to vessels transiting the strait. They can establish observation posts that monitor shipping in real time without the satellite and intelligence limitations of inland targeting. They can operate a logistics and resupply hub for naval assets threatening the strait. And they can do all of this without conducting the kind of long-range attack that the US Navy can intercept before impact — shore-based batteries at 50 miles are a fundamentally different targeting problem than inland launch positions at 300 miles. The oil market's $5 single-session repricing — $102.30 to $107.40 — reflects the beginning of this strategic reassessment, not its completion. The prior $100-103 range was pricing one confirmed chokepoint disruption: Hormuz. The question the oil market is now answering is how to price two chokepoints — one where Iran holds the exclusion zone and the Houthi now holds a territorial foothold near the other. The US military response on Friday — permanently disabling three Iranian oil tankers in the Persian Gulf — and the IRGC's 20-ballistic-missile strike on a Jordan airbase indicate that both sides have interpreted Friday as an escalation day and are operating on escalation postures. The BRICS Summit opening Saturday in New Delhi has its own logic: Iran's Pezeshkian arrives at a gathering where India, China, Russia, and the UAE — a BRICS member that has suspended trade with Iran — must navigate internal divisions over a war in which they are on opposite sides. Bloomberg's framing is accurate: Iran is turning to BRICS as its diplomatic lifeline in a conflict that has severed its ties to Western-aligned economies. What BRICS can deliver for Iran — beyond diplomatic optics — is the question the New Delhi summit opens.

Iran-backed Houthi forces seized Yemen's port city of Mokha on Friday — the largest territorial gain since the 2022 ceasefire and the closest major city to the Bab el-Mandab Strait, located less than 50 miles from the waterway that connects the Red Sea to the Gulf of Aden — pushing Brent crude above $107 per barrel as markets began repricing the supply threat to a new strategic level. CNBC headlines the development as "Houthis seize Yemen port, raising threat to oil and shipping"; PBS describes it as "putting more pressure on oil markets." The advance gives Houthis shore-based positions with direct line of sight to Bab el-Mandab shipping lanes, a qualitatively different capability than the inland missile positions they have used to attack shipping since the Iran war began.

The strategic significance of Mokha is geographic, not economic: the city itself is not an oil export terminal. What it provides is proximity. Anti-ship missiles launched from Mokha can reach vessels transiting the Bab el-Mandab from ranges that eliminate the reaction time available to naval escorts when missiles come from inland positions 300 miles away. The CNBC report notes the Bab el-Mandab connects the Red Sea to global markets — a formulation that understates it: the strait is one of the world's five most critical oil trade chokepoints, carrying roughly 4-5 million barrels per day of oil in normal conditions. A Houthi territorial foothold within 50 miles is the most direct threat that chokepoint has faced since the current conflict began. $107 Brent is the first number of a repricing that has not yet resolved.

US Central Command announced Friday it had permanently disabled three Iranian oil tankers in the Persian Gulf — including the M/T Downy off Kharg Island and the M/T Stark 1 near Jask — in response to IRGC ballistic missile attacks on a US aircraft carrier and guided-missile destroyer; CBS News reports US forces "permanently disabled" the vessels. Separately, the IRGC fired 20 ballistic missiles at a US airbase in Jordan on Friday, the first IRGC ballistic missile strike on Jordanian territory in the current conflict. Iran threatened "more painful" retaliation. The conflict has now extended simultaneously to Jordan (missiles), the Red Sea (Mokha), and the Persian Gulf (tanker strikes).

The geographic expansion is Friday's most strategically significant development beyond the Mokha seizure. The conflict began in February 2026 as a US-Israeli operation targeting Iranian nuclear and military infrastructure; it has since expanded to encompass the Strait of Hormuz exclusion zone, Houthi operations in the Bab el-Mandab, Iranian missile strikes on the Arabian Peninsula, and now IRGC ballistic missiles on Jordanian soil. Jordan is a US treaty partner and critical transit country; an IRGC ballistic missile strike on a US airbase there is a direct attack on US-aligned infrastructure outside the original Iran-Israel-US conflict geography. Iran's "more painful" retaliation threat, following CENTCOM's tanker strikes, establishes the same compounding escalation dynamic that has characterized every previous exchange in the conflict: US action produces Iranian escalation vow produces further US action. The cycle has not broken. Friday added two new geographies to the theater.

The BRICS summit opens Saturday in New Delhi for a September 12-13 gathering that Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian arrived to attend Friday, with Bloomberg reporting "Iran turns to BRICS summit as US war deepens its isolation" — the gathering is Iran's highest-profile diplomatic outreach to a multilateral forum since the US-Israeli strikes in February. Al Jazeera's opinion piece frames the summit as potentially "the only place another Iran ceasefire can be built," given that the US and Western allies are not parties to BRICS. The summit faces internal contradictions: the UAE — a BRICS member since 2024 — suspended trade and financial transactions with Iran in August after Iranian missile attacks, making two BRICS members active adversaries of each other within the bloc.

What BRICS can realistically offer Iran is limited but not trivial: diplomatic recognition that Iran is not diplomatically isolated from the world's largest emerging-economy bloc, potential energy and financial arrangements that circumvent Western sanctions pressure, and a forum where India and China — both pursuing Iran relationships independent of US policy — can signal their positions on a diplomatic resolution. What BRICS cannot offer is a ceasefire mechanism: any ceasefire requires the US and Israel to stop military operations, and neither is a BRICS member. Al Jazeera's framing is accurate in one specific sense: BRICS may be the only forum where the political will for a ceasefire is present among relevant parties who have back-channel influence over Iran and the surrounding actors. Whether that will translates to a negotiated outcome is a different question. Sweden's election on September 13 — the same day the BRICS summit concludes — is a Western democratic data point running on a parallel track to the India-hosted geopolitical gathering.
