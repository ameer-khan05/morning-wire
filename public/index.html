Scientists confirmed that the Silverpit Crater, buried 700 meters beneath the North Sea, was formed when a 160-meter asteroid struck Earth 43 to 46 million years ago and unleashed a tsunami more than 330 feet high — solving a decades-long geological mystery using newly available seismic images and microscopic mineral crystals recovered from an oil well near the crater; separately, Texas A&M University researchers identified a blood biomarker for Alzheimer’s disease that is elevated in adults as young as 45 who express memory concerns, before structural brain changes appear on imaging — the earliest biological detection window the disease has ever had — and GSK won FDA approval for a hepatitis B drug that extended functional cures to more patients than any previous treatment.

The Silverpit confirmation is a rare instance of settled science resolving a mystery that has been active for decades: the crater was discovered in 2002 but remained controversial because the evidence for an asteroid origin versus internal geological processes was ambiguous. The newly available seismic data combined with microscopic mineral crystal evidence creates a decisive case. The Alzheimer’s biomarker finding is the more immediately impactful for the broadest readership: moving the detection window from late-stage symptom appearance to midlife blood testing transforms the disease from something you learn you have when it is already advanced into something with an intervention window measurable in years, potentially decades.
