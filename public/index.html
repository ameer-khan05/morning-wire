NYU Langone scientists published a study in Nature Genetics showing that pancreatic cancer cells continuously rewrite their own DNA through MYC gene amplification to evade treatment and transition from a less aggressive “classical” state to a more dangerous “basal” state associated with invasion, spread, and treatment resistance — analyzing nearly half a million individual cells from dozens of patient tumors — and found in laboratory experiments that blocking MYC activity reverses the shift, returning basal cells toward the classical state and exposing them to immune attack; the finding identifies a target for which MYC-inhibiting drugs are already in clinical trials.

Pancreatic cancer’s 5-year survival rate has remained below 12% for decades because it is typically diagnosed late and rapidly develops resistance to standard treatment. The NYU Langone finding explains the mechanism of that resistance at the single-cell level: it is not a static genetic state but a continuously evolving one, with cancer cells actively rewriting their DNA in the direction of maximum treatment evasion. MYC inhibition blocking that rewriting — and exposing the cells to immune attack — is the kind of mechanistic result that advances drugs already in trials rather than opening a decades-long research program from scratch.
