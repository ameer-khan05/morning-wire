Duke Injectable Scaffold Grows New Blood Vessels in Stroke-Damaged Brains — Recruits Neutrophils as Repair Agents, Restores Motor Function in Mice · MIT Injectable Mini Livers for Failing Livers · Sea Squirt Compound Reverses Multiple Aging Signs in Older Mice Inject Duke injectable scaffold (Sep 2026): stroke-damaged mouse brains → injectable material → new blood vessel growth + nerve regrowth + motor function recovery → immune mechanism: recruited neutrophils → switched damaging to repairing → novel post-stroke repair pathway · MIT injectable mini livers (Sep 2026): liver failure patients → engineered hepatic organoids → injectable → supports essential metabolic functions → bridge to transplant · Sea squirt compound (Sep 2026): edible sea squirt (tunicate) → compound given to older mice → reversed multiple aging signs → tissue repair + muscle + organ function markers → longevity research advance

Duke researchers developed an injectable scaffold that prompted stroke-damaged mouse brains to grow new blood vessels, support nerve regrowth, and recover motor function — the treatment appears to work by recruiting the body's own neutrophils, which switched from a tissue-damaging role to a tissue-repairing one under the scaffold's influence, introducing immune-cell recruitment as a new post-stroke repair mechanism. MIT engineers separately developed injectable "mini livers" — engineered hepatic organoids designed to be injected into patients whose failing livers can no longer perform essential metabolic functions, providing a potential bridge to transplant. A compound extracted from edible sea squirts reversed multiple signs of aging in older mice, including tissue repair, muscle function, and organ performance markers — one of the more comprehensive aging-reversal results from a natural compound in 2026.

All three results share a design philosophy: inject something, and leverage biology to do the repair work. Duke's scaffold doesn't build vessels — it recruits immune cells to do it. MIT's organoid provides hepatic function without transplant surgery. The sea squirt compound reverses aging signs through mechanisms the cells already have. The gap from mice and preclinical models to human clinical trials is real and historically inconsistent; but the mechanistic logic in each case is cleaner than single-marker longevity or single-tissue repair results that preceded them. The week's science output — taken together — marks a productive period in the injectable regenerative biology field.
