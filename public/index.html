CRISPR researchers used gene editing to remove the CD33 surface protein from donor stem cells before transplant — allowing edited donor cells to survive a cancer-targeting therapy that otherwise destroys both cancer and healthy stem cells — opening a new pathway for treating aggressive blood cancers with currently limited curative options. A study tracking more than 95,000 people found that those spending more time in REM sleep had significantly lower risks across 83 conditions including dementia and heart failure; the NIH announced a $170 million biomedical reproducibility initiative; and climate scientists warned the world is bracing for the worst effects of a Super El Niño.

The CD33 CRISPR approach solves a specific, tractable obstacle in blood cancer cell therapy: by editing the target protein out of donor stem cells before transplant, researchers create a therapeutic cell population that can survive the CD33-targeting drug, enabling more aggressive treatment against cancer cells without sacrificing healthy donor cells. The mechanism is clean, the target is established, and the approach applies to an aggressive blood cancer class with high unmet clinical need.
