A magnet-producing bacterium extended C. elegans average lifespan by 43% while protecting neurological and intestinal health simultaneously — a result consistent with the field’s canonical longevity interventions but through a mechanism not previously connected to aging biology; researchers confirmed that mammalian cells can synthesize cysteine through a backup pathway when primary routes are disabled, complicating cancer therapy strategies targeting cysteine metabolism; and Phelan-McDermid syndrome was found to affect 1 in 7,300 people, higher than prior estimates, with a direct autism link.

The cysteine backup finding is the week’s most immediately actionable result for cancer research: if tumor cells can use the backup pathway when primary cysteine synthesis routes are blocked, deprivation-based therapies in development may be failing to restrict cysteine availability. The bacterium’s 43% lifespan extension, if the mechanism proves conserved in more complex organisms, opens a new avenue in longevity biology through bacterial magnetism.
