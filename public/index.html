McGill University researchers published in Nature Immunology that tissue-resident memory T cells detect the mechanical stiffness of surrounding tissue and use that physical signal to determine whether they survive long-term as protective memory cells — with T cells in stiffer environments more likely to become durable defenders, with direct implications for cancer immunotherapy design, autoimmune disease treatment, and transplant rejection protocols; three concurrent studies published in Nature by Australian researchers separately identified nearly 1,500 genes linked to 16 major diseases through immune-cell-specific regulatory networks, substantially expanding the genetic map of immune-mediated disease.

The McGill stiffness finding changes a foundational assumption in immunotherapy: tumors’ dense physical microenvironment does not only block immune cell infiltration — it also shapes whether infiltrating T cells become durable memory cells. A dense tumor sends a stiffness signal that affects the long-term fate of any T cell that penetrates it, which explains why some tumors escape immunity despite initial T-cell infiltration and points toward engineering the mechanical microenvironment as a therapeutic lever alongside checkpoint inhibitors.
