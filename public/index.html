Psilocybin: Single 25 mg Dose → 30-Day Brain Structural Changes (UC San Francisco + Imperial College London, Nature Communications) · Fluoride-Free Gel Rebuilds Tooth Enamel · Hyperemesis Gravidarum: 9 New Genes, GDF15 Pathway 30 days Psilocybin: single 25mg dose → brain entropy ▲ (EEG, 60min) + denser neural tracts / higher integrity (DTI, 30 days) + higher well-being + cognitive flexibility · UCSF + Imperial College London · Nature Communications · fluoride-free enamel gel: biomimetic hydroxyapatite · hyperemesis: 9 new genes, GDF15 overlap

A placebo-controlled study by UC San Francisco and Imperial College London, published in Nature Communications, found that a single 25 mg dose of psilocybin in 28 psychedelic-naive healthy adults produced measurable increases in neural entropy within 60 minutes and physical brain structural changes — denser neural tracts with greater integrity on diffusion tensor imaging — still detectable at 30 days, with participants also reporting higher well-being and cognitive flexibility at follow-up; the finding advances therapeutic psychedelics from an "experience" model to a structural-change model. Separately, researchers developed a fluoride-free gel that rebuilds lost tooth enamel through biomimetic hydroxyapatite crystallization — addressing erosion damage that fluoride cannot reverse — and a global genetic study identified nine new hyperemesis gravidarum risk genes, several overlapping with the GDF15 pathway that also governs drug-induced nausea.

The psilocybin DTI finding is the study's structural advance: prior research documented psilocybin's acute connectivity effects during the trip; this is the first clear evidence that the brain does not simply return to baseline after the acute window closes. Denser, higher-integrity neural tracts at 30 days — the structure of a healthier brain — suggests psilocybin is triggering neuroplasticity changes that persist well beyond the subjective experience. Whether those structural changes produce lasting therapeutic benefit in clinical populations (depression, PTSD, addiction) is what the next generation of trials must answer. The GDF15 overlap in hyperemesis genetics is the other finding with cross-domain implications: the same hormonal pathway that drives pregnancy sickness may be the mechanism by which GLP-1 drugs produce drug-induced nausea — potentially opening a route to suppress both.
