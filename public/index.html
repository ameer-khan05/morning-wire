A clinical trial published Friday found the ketogenic diet reduced liver fat by 67% in just four months among patients with obesity, prediabetes, and fatty liver disease — significantly more than Mediterranean or plant-based diets achieved in the same period — reversing prediabetes in roughly half of participants and producing the largest glucagon increases of any diet tested; MIT researchers found that the brain’s natural waste-removal system — the glymphatic network that clears metabolic byproducts during sleep — becomes significantly less effective as people age, potentially explaining the link between age-related sleep changes and neurodegenerative disease progression; and researchers published new data toward a blood test for CTE, the neurodegenerative condition caused by repeated brain trauma, which currently can only be diagnosed at autopsy.

The keto liver trial, the MIT sleep finding, and the CTE blood test research all point toward the same underlying shift: the biology of aging and brain injury is becoming measurable at a resolution that clinical intervention can target. Liver fat at 67% reduction in four months is a metabolic result with direct implications for the millions of patients on GLP-1 drugs who also carry fatty liver disease. MIT’s glymphatic finding connects sleep science to dementia prevention in a way that is now mechanistically testable.
