The RESET for Remission trial, published Friday in The Lancet Regional Health — Americas and presented at the European Association for the Study of Diabetes annual meeting in Milan, found that 54% of younger adults with type 2 diabetes achieved full glycemic remission after 24 weeks of an 800–900 kcal/day diet combined with twice-weekly supervised exercise and once-weekly independent exercise, compared to just 4% in the usual-care control group, establishing the largest documented remission rate for type 2 diabetes through a lifestyle intervention and challenging the clinical assumption that the condition is a permanent and progressive disease in younger adults; separately, Johns Hopkins researchers publishing in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences overturned a 30-year theory of retinal development by establishing that a vitamin A–derived molecule and thyroid hormones work together to remodel the foveola — the retinal region responsible for sharpest vision — before birth, finding that blue cones do not migrate away from the fovea as previously believed but instead remain in place and change their molecular identity under the coordinated action of retinoic acid decline and thyroid hormone signaling, with immediate implications for treating macular degeneration and glaucoma; scientists also found that spiders experience REM-like sleep phases complete with twitching muscles and visibly moving eyes under their translucent exoskeletons, suggesting that REM sleep evolved far earlier in evolutionary history than mammal-centric research frameworks have assumed; and a new study identified a small cluster of brain cells that actively prevents dangerous blood pressure spikes even in individuals whose average blood pressure appears normal.

Saturday’s science beat is defined by two studies that overturn long-standing clinical and biological assumptions: early-onset type 2 diabetes is reversible through diet and exercise more often than medicine has assumed, and the fovea forms through a mechanism that textbooks have described incorrectly for 30 years. Both carry immediate implications for treatment funding and clinical protocol.

The Anchor · Diabetes Remission at 54% — Type 2 Is Not Irreversible If Caught and Treated Early

The RESET for Remission trial achieved what most endocrinology textbooks describe as unlikely in type 2 diabetes: full remission in more than half of a younger adult cohort through diet and exercise alone, sustained over six months. The clinical implication is large. The 54% versus 4% remission gap between the intervention and control groups is not close — it is the difference between a condition that is reversible and one that is managed for life. The critical modifier is “younger adults with early-onset type 2 diabetes”: the trial enrolled patients aged 18 to 45, mean age 38, diagnosed within the prior six years. That specificity matters. Type 2 diabetes in a 38-year-old with six years of disease duration is a meaningfully different clinical entity than the same condition in a 65-year-old with 20 years. The intervention was demanding — 800 to 900 kcal per day is a strict calorie restriction, and twice-weekly supervised exercise has real compliance barriers — but the trial showed that the biological reversibility is real when the structural support to execute the intervention is present. The policy implication: intensive remission programs targeting early-onset type 2 diabetes in younger adults are not experimental. They work in more than half of cases. The question is whether healthcare systems fund them at the scale the evidence now supports.

The RESET for Remission trial enrolled 96 adults aged 18 to 45 with type 2 diabetes diagnosed within the prior six years, randomizing 50 to an intensive intervention — 800–900 kcal/day diet, twice-weekly supervised aerobic and resistance training for 12 weeks, followed by a weight-maintenance phase through week 24 — and 46 to usual care; 27 of the 50 intervention participants (54%) achieved full glycemic remission at week 24 versus 2 of 46 (4%) receiving usual care, with the intervention group averaging 18.5 pounds of predominantly fat-specific weight loss; the results, published in The Lancet Regional Health — Americas and presented at the European Association for the Study of Diabetes annual meeting in Milan, represent the highest remission rate ever documented in a type 2 diabetes trial of this design.

The 54% number is large, but its scope condition is precise: younger adults, early disease, intensive structured support. The same intervention applied to older patients with longer disease duration produces different results. What the trial establishes is that remission is biologically achievable in the early-onset cohort at a rate that justifies building structured intensive programs specifically for that population — a funding and policy decision, not a research one.

Johns Hopkins University researchers published findings in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences overturning the prevailing 30-year theory of how human central vision develops, using miniature human retinas grown in the lab to discover that the foveola — the tiny central retinal region responsible for sharpest vision — forms through two coordinated molecular processes: first, levels of retinoic acid, a vitamin A–derived molecule, decline and limit the formation of new blue cones; then thyroid hormones convert the remaining blue cones into red and green cones, creating the specialized cone arrangement needed for sharp vision; this overturns the prior theory that blue cones simply migrate away from the foveola during development, establishing instead that they remain in place and undergo a molecular identity change, with the discovery potentially opening new approaches to treating macular degeneration, glaucoma, and other vision disorders affecting millions.

Thirty years of retinal development textbooks have described a process that does not happen as described. The implications are not merely academic: treatment strategies for macular degeneration and other foveal disorders have been built on models of cone migration. If the cones do not migrate — if they stay and change identity — the intervention points for treating developmental and degenerative foveal disease are different.

Scientists found that spiders experience a REM-like sleep phase complete with twitching muscles and visibly moving eyes — observable through their translucent exoskeletons — providing the first clear evidence that REM-like sleep stages exist in arthropods and suggesting that the neural mechanisms underlying this type of sleep evolved far earlier in evolutionary history than frameworks built primarily around mammalian sleep biology have assumed; the finding expands the taxonomic range over which REM-like sleep is documented from vertebrates and some invertebrates to include arachnids, with implications for understanding whether the consolidation and processing functions associated with REM sleep in mammals serve similar roles in phylogenetically distant species.

Spider REM sleep is not merely a curiosity — it challenges the assumption that complex sleep architecture is a feature of complex brains. If arthropods with significantly simpler nervous systems exhibit REM-like stages, the function of REM sleep must be explained at a more fundamental biological level than mammalian neuroscience has provided.

Researchers discovered a previously unknown small cluster of brain cells that actively prevents dangerous blood pressure spikes even in individuals whose average blood pressure readings appear normal, establishing that the neural regulation of blood pressure operates through a spike-suppression mechanism that is independent of the average pressure level — meaning that someone with a normal average blood pressure can still be at elevated cardiovascular risk if this cell cluster is compromised or dysfunctional; the finding provides a new mechanistic target for understanding sudden cardiovascular events in patients without hypertension diagnoses, and suggests that blood pressure risk assessment based solely on average readings may miss a clinically important dimension of cardiovascular vulnerability.

Normal average blood pressure has been the primary clinical threshold for cardiovascular risk stratification. A brain mechanism that prevents dangerous spikes independent of average pressure means that two individuals with identical average readings can have very different spike-suppression capacity — a gap that current clinical assessment tools do not measure. The cell cluster is a target; the next step is a biomarker.
