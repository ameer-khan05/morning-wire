The Anchor · 2026 ED138 — A Sixth Dwarf Planet on the Edge of the Solar System

The solar system may be getting a new member. The Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile — which began full survey operations earlier in 2026 and is designed to detect exactly these kinds of faint, distant objects — identified 2026 ED138 at a distance of approximately 46 astronomical units from the sun, placing it well beyond Neptune and in the class of trans-Neptunian objects that includes other recognized dwarf planets. Its estimated diameter of 600 to 1,500 kilometers puts it in the range that the International Astronomical Union uses to evaluate dwarf planet status — large enough that its own gravity would have pulled it into a roughly spherical shape. If the IAU confirms its classification, 2026 ED138 would become the sixth named dwarf planet in the solar system, joining Pluto, Eris, Haumea, Makemake, and Ceres. The process is not fast: astronomers need approximately two years of additional observations to pin down 2026 ED138’s orbital parameters precisely enough for a formal classification vote. What is already established is the detection itself — and the reason it matters is as much about the instrument that found it as about the object found: Rubin’s Legacy Survey of Space and Time surveys the entire southern sky every few nights, and 2026 ED138 is the most prominent object in the survey’s early trans-Neptunian catalog. If Rubin can find a 1,000-kilometer object hiding at 46 AU, astronomers believe there are more waiting in the same catalog.

The Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile detected 2026 ED138 at a distance of approximately 46 astronomical units from the sun — well beyond Neptune’s orbit — with a preliminary diameter estimate of 600 to 1,500 kilometers that places it among the largest known trans-Neptunian objects and within the size range the International Astronomical Union uses to classify dwarf planets; if confirmed through two more years of observational refinement, 2026 ED138 would become the sixth member of the solar system’s dwarf planet category alongside Pluto (39.5 AU, 2,377 km), Eris (38-68 AU perihelion-to-aphelion, 2,326 km), Haumea (1,595 km), Makemake (1,430 km), and Ceres in the asteroid belt (945 km); the detection is a demonstration of the Rubin Observatory’s Legacy Survey of Space and Time, which surveys the entire southern sky every few nights and is specifically designed to find faint, distant objects that prior telescopes missed; astronomers described 2026 ED138 as the most significant new solar system object in roughly 20 years, and the first genuine candidate to challenge the existing dwarf planet census in a generation.

The two-year window before an IAU classification vote reflects the precision required for orbital mechanics. A trans-Neptunian object detected recently has a short observational arc, meaning its orbital period — thousands of years for an object at 46 AU — is estimated with wide error bars. The IAU requires a confident orbital solution before classifying. In the meantime, 2026 ED138 sits in observational limbo: probably a dwarf planet by size, definitively the most significant solar system discovery in decades, and awaiting the data to confirm it officially.

Researchers at UCLA identified a naturally occurring molecule that appears to counteract the persistent brain fog and exhaustion that many breast cancer survivors experience following the completion of treatment — a cluster of symptoms collectively described in the clinical literature as cancer-related cognitive impairment and cancer-related fatigue, which affects a substantial proportion of breast cancer survivors and significantly impairs quality of life after the cancer itself is in remission; the molecule, which exists in the body under normal conditions, appears to target the biological pathways through which cancer treatment produces the neuroinflammation and mitochondrial disruption associated with post-treatment cognitive symptoms; the UCLA team’s approach is focused on restoring the molecule to levels that cancer treatment depletes, rather than introducing a new pharmacological agent — which could reduce the regulatory and side-effect hurdles that have limited other approaches to treating cancer-related cognitive impairment.

Cancer-related cognitive impairment is one of the most underreported consequences of breast cancer treatment, partly because it is not life-threatening and partly because it is difficult to quantify. Patients describe it as a foggy version of their former mental clarity — slower recall, difficulty concentrating, persistent fatigue — that can persist for years after treatment ends. The UCLA finding is notable precisely because it uses a naturally occurring molecule rather than a novel drug: treatment depletes something the body already makes, and restoring it appears to help.

A New York City research team published a machine-learning model capable of predicting the likelihood that a patient will be diagnosed with PTSD one month before a clinical diagnosis would otherwise be made, using only patient-reported answers to standardized questionnaires and demographic data — with no biomarkers, imaging, or clinical observation required; the model’s one-month lead time represents a clinically meaningful intervention window, since early therapeutic engagement is associated with better outcomes in PTSD and the current diagnostic pathway often identifies the condition only after symptoms have become entrenched; the team emphasized that the model is a prediction tool rather than a diagnostic instrument, and that its purpose is to flag high-risk individuals for earlier clinical attention rather than to replace formal psychiatric evaluation.

The practical significance of a one-month lead time is not abstract: PTSD is substantially harder to treat the longer it goes unaddressed, and the current pathway — in which a patient experiences traumatic symptoms, seeks care, receives a diagnosis, and then begins treatment — typically takes months. A model that identifies high-risk individuals from intake questionnaires before they cross the diagnostic threshold gives clinicians a window to intervene with preventive therapeutic engagement, potentially reducing both the severity of PTSD when it emerges and the duration of treatment required.

Ebola Genomic Tracking Real Time Pathogen Genome Sequencing Enables Faster Outbreak Detection Transmission Chain Identification Precise Geographic Containment Friday November 13 2026 Ebola Genomic Tracking · Real-Time Sequencing · Faster Outbreak Response Ebola genomics (Nov 13 2026): real-time → pathogen → genome → sequencing → Ebola → outbreak → faster → detection → transmission → chain → identification → precise → geographic → containment

A new study described how real-time pathogen genome sequencing is transforming the response to Ebola outbreaks, enabling epidemiologists to identify transmission chains within days rather than weeks and to contain geographic spread with far greater precision than the contact-tracing methods used in previous Ebola responses; the technique — which sequences viral genomes from patient samples rapidly enough to inform active outbreak decisions — produces a phylogenetic map of exactly how the virus is moving through a population, allowing health officials to identify undetected transmission links, distinguish between independent spillover events and sustained community transmission, and allocate containment resources to the locations where chains are still active; the authors argued that the genomic approach represents a step-change in outbreak response capability compared to the 2014-2016 West Africa epidemic, in which the absence of real-time genomic data meant that transmission chains were routinely invisible until they had already seeded new clusters.

Real-time genomic sequencing during outbreaks was essentially unavailable during the 2014-2016 West Africa Ebola epidemic, when containment relied almost entirely on contact tracing — a method that depends on patients accurately recalling and naming contacts, which is inconsistent under crisis conditions. Genomic sequencing does not require patient memory: the virus’s own mutation rate produces a unique signature that tells epidemiologists where it has been regardless of what patients report. The 2026 study codifies the techniques developed across smaller recent Ebola outbreaks into a reproducible protocol for the next large one.
