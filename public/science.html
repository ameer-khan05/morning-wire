The Anchor · The Measles Count — What 5,400 Cases and 10 Deaths in a Country That Held Elimination Status for 25 Years Tells You About the Gap Between Knowing What To Do and Doing It

The US has 5,400 measles cases and 10 deaths this year. It held elimination status for 25 years. The MMR vaccine is 97% effective. The gap between those facts is 3 percentage points of vaccination coverage. The measles outbreak at 5,400 cases and 10 deaths is not a knowledge failure or a technology failure. The MMR vaccine works — 97% effective after two doses, available, free in most public health systems, and deployed successfully for more than 50 years. What it is is a deployment failure at a specific scale in specific communities. Vaccination rates fell from 95% of kindergartners in 2019 to 92% in 2026. That 3-percentage-point decline is not uniform across the country — it is concentrated in communities where vaccine hesitancy has increased since the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted routine childhood vaccination and reinforced doubts that pre-pandemic public health campaigns had not fully resolved. The consequence of that 3-percentage-point gap is a disease that eliminated itself from the United States in 2000, that the country went 25 years without meaningful domestic transmission of, returning at the highest case count since 1991 and killing 10 people. Seven of those deaths were in Pennsylvania. The seven Pennsylvania deaths are the specific, precise cost of the vaccination rate falling below the threshold in the specific communities where it fell. The tools to close the gap exist. They worked for 25 years. The question is not what to do. It is whether the public health system and the political will to deploy the tools that worked for 25 years at the scale and in the communities where they are now needed can be mobilized before the case count reaches 1991’s levels.

Measles cases nationwide have topped 5,400 with 10 confirmed deaths — the worst US outbreak in more than 30 years and the highest annual case count since 1991 — with states confirming 7 deaths in Pennsylvania, 1 in New York, and 2 in Wisconsin, and roughly 1 in 9 reported measles cases resulting in hospitalization, according to ABC News and CNBC; nearly all cases have been in people who had not received the MMR vaccine or whose vaccination status was unknown, with the CDC noting the MMR vaccine is 97% effective after two doses; the outbreak follows the United States losing its measles elimination status earlier this month — a designation held since 2000 — after a commission of the Pan American Health Organization confirmed MMR vaccination of kindergartners had fallen to 92%, below the 95% threshold; and PBS NewsHour reported on the question of whether the US can regain elimination status, noting that recovery requires sustained public health investment in the specific undervaccinated communities where coverage fell below the threshold.

The 5,400 case count and 10 deaths are the measles elimination story made concrete. The loss of elimination status was a statistical milestone — a vaccination rate falling 3 percentage points below a threshold. The 5,400 cases and 10 deaths are the medical consequence of that statistical milestone expressed in human terms. At 1991’s levels — the year the US last saw case counts comparable to this — the outbreak was driven by pockets of unvaccinated populations in urban communities. The 2026 outbreak has the same driver in different geography: post-pandemic vaccination gaps in communities where hesitancy and disrupted outreach programs created the same pockets of susceptibility.

New EFEMP1 Associated Late Onset Retinal Degeneration Genetic Eye Disease p.Arg140Trp Variant Night Blindness Peripheral Vision Loss Central Vision Intact Silent Damage JAMA Ophthalmology Penn Medicine Scheie Eye Institute Sunday November 22 2026 EFEMP1 Eye Disease · Silent Night Vision Loss · Peripheral Damage · JAMA Ophthalmology EFEMP1 (Sun Nov 22 2026): p.Arg140Trp → EFEMP1 → variant → night → blindness → peripheral → vision → loss → central → intact → JAMA → Ophthalmology → Penn → Medicine

An international team including researchers from the Scheie Eye Institute at the University of Pennsylvania’s Perelman School of Medicine published findings in JAMA Ophthalmology on a previously uncharacterized genetic eye disease caused by the p.Arg140Trp variant in the EFEMP1 gene — formally designated EFEMP1-associated late-onset retinal degeneration (L-ORD) — that silently destroys peripheral and night vision while leaving central sight largely unaffected for years, making it difficult for patients to notice the condition until significant retinal damage has already occurred; the disease mechanism involves abnormally thick material building up between the eye’s cells due to the specific EFEMP1 variant, and differs from a previously identified EFEMP1-related condition that affects central rather than peripheral vision — both caused by different variants in the same gene; and ScienceDaily noted that the new condition presents a diagnostic challenge because patients retain the central visual acuity that standard eye exams measure, while peripheral and night vision — which routine clinical exams do not comprehensively test — are progressively damaged.

The EFEMP1 L-ORD finding is an example of genomic medicine catching what clinical detection misses. The condition destroys the vision types — peripheral and night — that routine eye examinations are least equipped to detect early, in a population that retains the central vision acuity that routine exams measure as a primary indicator of eye health. The gene variant is the biomarker that makes early detection possible; without genomic screening, patients lose peripheral and night vision for years before the damage is clinically apparent. That is the diagnostic gap the JAMA publication closes — not by producing a treatment, but by producing the name and mechanism for a condition that was previously damaging vision without a framework for identifying or treating it.

Solar Sail Interstellar Travel Physics Light Drag Deceleration 75 Percent Speed Light Relativistic Aberration Diffuse Photons Redirect Forward Brake Counterintuitive Fundamental Problem Sunday November 22 2026 Solar Sail Speed Paradox · Light Drag At 75% · Propulsion Becomes Braking Solar sail (Sun Nov 22 2026): 75% → speed → light → relativistic → aberration → photons → redirect → forward → drag → brake → propulsion → paradox → interstellar

A 2026 physics study published in SciTechDaily and covered by multiple science outlets found that solar sails designed for interstellar travel encounter a fundamental and counterintuitive deceleration problem at approximately 75% of the speed of light: at relativistic velocities, light aberration causes diffusely scattered photons — the photons that have reflected off the sail surface — to redirect forward in the direction the sail is traveling rather than backward, creating a drag force from the same photons that provide propulsion at lower speeds; the transition from net propulsion to net drag begins near 75% of light speed and intensifies as the sail accelerates further, creating a physical ceiling below the target velocity of proposed interstellar missions; the study examines purely radiative dynamics and does not account for additional real-world complications including interstellar dust collisions, thermal stress on sail materials, and spacetime curvature — all of which add further constraints; and practical interstellar solar sail missions, including proposed Breakthrough Starshot-class designs, would need to solve the deceleration problem through additional mechanisms not yet demonstrated at scale.

The solar sail finding is a physics result that moves the goalposts on interstellar travel in the direction no one anticipated. The deceleration problem at journey’s end — how to slow a laser-propelled sail arriving at a star at a significant fraction of light speed — has been a known engineering challenge since solar sail interstellar concepts were first proposed. What this study adds is a propulsion-phase problem: the light that accelerates the sail becomes a drag force before the sail reaches the theoretical maximum. The constraint is not engineering; it is physics. It does not make interstellar travel impossible. It makes it slower than the most ambitious current proposals assume, by a margin that requires either a new propulsion mechanism or a revised mission profile.
