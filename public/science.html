The Anchor · The Glymphatic Clock — MIT’s Findings on Brain Waste-Removal Decline With Age and Why the Years Before Symptoms Are the Actionable Window

MIT’s finding that the brain’s glymphatic waste-removal system declines measurably during sleep as people age — with the decline detectable years before clinical cognitive symptoms emerge — is the most practically significant Alzheimer’s-related finding of the week because it defines when intervention is possible, not just when it is necessary. The glymphatic system is the brain’s waste-clearance mechanism. During sleep, cerebrospinal fluid pulses through channels surrounding blood vessels, flushing amyloid beta and tau proteins — the two proteins whose accumulation is associated with Alzheimer’s disease — out of brain tissue. This system is most active during slow-wave sleep and is the primary mechanism by which the sleeping brain clears the metabolic byproducts of a day’s neural activity. MIT’s research found that this clearance mechanism’s efficiency declines with age in a pattern that is measurable before clinical cognitive symptoms appear. The practical significance of that sequencing is profound: if glymphatic clearance declines before the clinical presentation of Alzheimer’s, the window between “measurable glymphatic decline” and “clinical Alzheimer’s diagnosis” is an intervention window where treatments targeting the clearance mechanism might slow or prevent the protein accumulation that leads to disease. Tuesday’s MIT pink noise research addressed how to enhance glymphatic function through acoustic stimulation during sleep. Wednesday’s MIT finding addresses when that enhancement would be most urgently needed: not at clinical diagnosis, but years earlier, when the decline is first measurable. Together, the two MIT findings define a new Alzheimer’s prevention architecture: measure glymphatic function decline with aging, intervene with acoustic and pharmaceutical tools before symptoms appear. The AR1001 result from Tuesday’s POLARIS-AD trial addressed the clinical endpoint — slowing cognitive decline. The MIT glymphatic clock addresses the preclinical window where preventing that decline from reaching clinical presentation becomes possible. These are complementary layers of the same disease intervention strategy.

A joint United Nations and World Health Organization report published Wednesday estimated that endocrine-disrupting chemicals’ global health costs exceed $1 trillion annually — a figure the Japan Times described as the most aggressive public health framing of chemical exposure costs published by the UN-WHO system to date — with the report covering reproductive health impacts including reduced fertility and developmental abnormalities, cancer attributable to hormonal disruption, and neurodevelopmental effects in children linked to prenatal and early-life EDC exposure; the report described EDCs as a “global and growing threat” and called for binding international regulatory frameworks to restrict EDC use in consumer products, food packaging, and agricultural applications, in a document that positions EDC regulation alongside climate and antimicrobial resistance as a tier-one global health governance priority; the $1 trillion cost estimate includes direct healthcare costs and productivity losses from EDC-linked health outcomes across all income levels, with the report noting that lower-income populations bear disproportionate exposure and health burdens due to less stringent chemical regulation in those markets.

The UN-WHO $1 trillion figure is a policy document, not a scientific measurement — it is designed to shift the regulatory conversation by translating diffuse epidemiological evidence into an economic figure that finance ministries and trade negotiators can process. The strategy is familiar from climate economics: establish a cost figure large enough to make inaction more expensive than regulation, then use that figure in multilateral forums to build momentum for binding standards. EDC regulation is several years behind climate in that framework development. This report is designed to accelerate the schedule.

Eli Lilly Stipple Bio Collaboration $5.9 Billion Cell-Surface Epitope Technology Cancer Targeting Distinguish Tumor Cells Healthy Tissue Precision Oncology Reduce Off-Target Toxicity Wednesday November 18 2026 Eli Lilly Stipple Bio $5.9B · Cell-Surface Epitope Cancer Targeting · Precision Oncology Lilly/Stipple (Wed Nov 18 2026): $5.9B → collaboration → cell-surface → epitope → technology → distinguish → tumor → cells → healthy → tissue → precision → oncology → reduce → toxicity

Eli Lilly signed a collaboration agreement with Stipple Bio worth up to $5.9 billion to develop cancer therapies that use cell-surface epitope technology to distinguish tumor cells from healthy tissue with greater precision than current oncology approaches — a deal that Endpoints News described as the week’s most significant oncology business development transaction — with the collaboration targeting a fundamental limitation of current cancer therapies: the difficulty of selectively killing cancer cells without damaging surrounding healthy tissue; Stipple Bio’s cell-surface epitope platform identifies unique protein markers on tumor cell surfaces that distinguish them from healthy cells expressing the same underlying proteins — a precision approach that could reduce the off-target toxicity that limits dosing in many existing cancer therapies and expand the therapeutic window for treatments that are currently constrained by healthy tissue damage; the $5.9 billion deal structure includes an upfront payment and milestone payments across a development program that spans multiple tumor types, reflecting Lilly’s strategy of building a precision oncology platform through external partnerships rather than internal discovery programs.

The Lilly-Stipple deal’s $5.9 billion value reflects the premium the pharmaceutical industry is placing on targeted delivery solutions that can unlock the full therapeutic potential of cancer drugs constrained by toxicity. The cell-surface epitope approach is distinct from ADC (antibody-drug conjugate) targeting, which uses antibody-antigen binding rather than cell-surface epitope profiling — Stipple’s platform claims to achieve finer discrimination between closely related cell types. If the platform performs in human trials as it has in preclinical work, it addresses the central challenge of solid tumor oncology more directly than any single drug mechanism.

First-patient dosing is the clinical trial milestone that converts a drug from a regulatory document into a data-generating program. TAX2’s first patient dosing means the program is now on the clinical calendar alongside established programs; in 18 months, it will have human pharmacokinetic data that determines whether Phase II enrollment proceeds. The day’s combination of TAX2’s Phase I start, the Lilly-Stipple $5.9 billion platform deal, and Tuesday’s AR1001 Phase III confirmatory result maps the full cancer development pipeline from first patient to commercialization pathway in three consecutive data points.
