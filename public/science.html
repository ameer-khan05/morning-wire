The NIH published what researchers described as its largest-ever study of per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) and breast cancer on Thursday, finding that women with the highest blood levels of PFOS had a 93% higher rate of estrogen receptor-negative (ER-negative) breast cancer — a more aggressive tumor type with fewer known risk factors — while those with the highest PFOA levels had a 67% higher rate, in research drawn from the NIH’s Sister Study cohort of women who had each enrolled with a sister or half-sister previously diagnosed with breast cancer; a randomized controlled trial published in the journal Neuromodulation found that non-invasive autonomic neuromodulation significantly improved Long COVID symptoms across seven domains — fatigue, breathing, cognition, sleep quality, mental health, circulation, and overall symptom impact on daily life — in the first randomized trial to show multi-domain benefits for Long COVID treatment; colorectal cancer rates are rising again after 20 years of declining rates, with researchers actively investigating the reversal in a trend that had been one of modern oncology’s public health successes; and new research found that GLP-1 receptor agonists — the drug class that includes Ozempic and Wegovy — may offer benefits for brain health beyond their metabolic effects, including potential preservation of memory function.

Thursday’s science beat is led by a finding that affects virtually every person in the industrialized world: PFAS compounds in drinking water and consumer products are present in nearly all human blood samples at detectable levels, and the NIH’s largest study yet links them to nearly double the rate of one of breast cancer’s most dangerous subtypes.

The Anchor · NIH PFAS Study — The Largest Evidence Yet That Forever Chemicals Raise Aggressive Breast Cancer Risk

PFAS compounds — per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances — are called “forever chemicals” because they do not break down in the environment or in the human body. They are in the drinking water of hundreds of millions of people. They are in nonstick cookware, food packaging, water-resistant clothing, and firefighting foam. Studies of their health effects have been accumulating for years. Thursday’s NIH study is the largest, and its findings are specific: not all breast cancer, but the most dangerous kind. The study drew on data from the Sister Study — a large prospective cohort of women each with a sister or half-sister who had been diagnosed with breast cancer, enrolled before they themselves developed the disease — making it the right study design for identifying risk factors for a disease that takes years to develop. The researchers measured PFAS levels in blood samples collected at enrollment and tracked breast cancer diagnoses over time. The findings for ER-negative breast cancer — the subtype that is not fueled by estrogen, is harder to treat, and has fewer targeted therapies — are the most significant: women in the highest PFOS quartile had a 93% higher rate than those in the lowest. Women in the highest PFOA quartile had a 67% higher rate. These are not small elevations. They are nearly double the baseline rate for a cancer subtype that already carries a substantially worse prognosis than the more common ER-positive form. The study found no significant association between the two chemicals and overall breast cancer risk — the effect is specific to the aggressive subtype. That specificity is both clinically important and scientifically meaningful: it suggests a mechanism that selectively affects estrogen-independent tumor biology, not breast tissue generally. The implications for PFAS remediation priorities, clinical screening protocols for exposed populations, and the ongoing legal accountability of PFAS manufacturers are substantial.

The NIH published its largest-ever study of PFAS exposure and breast cancer Thursday, finding that women with the highest blood levels of PFOS had a 93% higher rate of estrogen receptor-negative (ER-negative) breast cancer — the more aggressive, harder-to-treat subtype — compared to those in the lowest exposure group, while women with the highest PFOA levels had a 67% higher rate of the same tumor type; the study used data from the Sister Study, an NIH prospective cohort in which participants each had a sister previously diagnosed with breast cancer and enrolled before developing it themselves, allowing the researchers to measure PFAS levels in pre-disease blood samples and then track cancer diagnoses over time — a study design that provides stronger causal evidence than cross-sectional approaches; neither PFOA nor PFOS showed a significant association with overall breast cancer risk, suggesting the chemical mechanism acts specifically on estrogen-independent tumor biology; PFAS compounds are present in the blood of approximately 97% of Americans at detectable levels, primarily from drinking water contaminated near manufacturing or military facilities, and from food packaging and nonstick cookware.

The study’s specificity to ER-negative breast cancer — rather than breast cancer overall — is clinically and scientifically important. ER-negative breast cancer responds to fewer treatments, is typically diagnosed at later stages, and carries substantially worse outcomes than ER-positive forms. If PFAS exposure nearly doubles its rate in heavily exposed populations, it has implications for which patients should be screened more aggressively — particularly those in communities with known PFAS-contaminated water supplies — and for the legal liability of PFAS manufacturers, who have been fighting settlements for years on the grounds that health effects were not proven. This study is evidence of a specific, serious effect.

A randomized controlled trial published Thursday in the journal Neuromodulation found that non-invasive autonomic neuromodulation — a technique that uses electrical stimulation delivered to the skin to modulate the autonomic nervous system — significantly improved Long COVID symptoms across seven domains: fatigue, breathing, cognition, sleep quality, mental health, circulation, and overall symptom impact on daily life; the trial is the first randomized study to demonstrate multi-domain benefits for Long COVID treatment, addressing the core challenge of a condition characterized by symptoms spanning multiple body systems simultaneously that few single-mechanism interventions can address; Long COVID affects an estimated 65 million people globally, with the most debilitating cases involving the fatigue-cognition-breathing triad that the neuromodulation intervention specifically targeted; the autonomic nervous system’s dysregulation — in which the balance between fight-or-flight and rest-and-digest nervous system functions is disrupted — has been proposed as a central mechanism underlying many Long COVID symptoms, making autonomic neuromodulation a mechanistically plausible treatment target.

A randomized controlled trial showing multi-domain improvement in Long COVID is a significant advance in a field that has struggled to produce evidence-based treatment options. The standard criticism of Long COVID treatment research has been that most interventions show modest effects in single domains, making it hard to know if any treatment is addressing the underlying pathology or just managing individual symptoms. An RCT with seven domains improving simultaneously suggests the autonomic dysregulation hypothesis is mechanistically real — and that treating it at the nervous system level may be more effective than treating symptoms individually.

Colorectal Cancer Rising Again 20 Years Declining Rates Reversal Scientists Investigating Causes Thursday November 12 2026 Colorectal Cancer Rising · 20-Year Decline Reverses · Investigation Colorectal (Thu Nov 12 2026): cancer → rising → again → 20 → years → declining → rates → reversal → scientists → investigating → causes → younger → adults → diet → microbiome → screening → gaps

Colorectal cancer rates are rising again after 20 consecutive years of declining incidence — one of modern public health’s most celebrated long-run successes — with researchers now actively investigating the causes of the reversal in a trend that had been attributed to increased colonoscopy screening, reduced smoking rates, and improved dietary guidelines; the increase is most pronounced among adults under 50, a cohort that was not traditionally targeted for routine colorectal cancer screening and in which the reversal has been steepest; proposed explanations under investigation include dietary pattern changes — particularly ultra-processed food consumption — gut microbiome disruption from antibiotic use and diet, obesity rates in younger cohorts, and inadequate screening coverage in the under-50 population that most surveillance programs do not address; colorectal cancer is among the most preventable cancers when detected early through colonoscopy or stool-based testing, making the under-50 screening gap particularly consequential as incidence rises in that age group.

Twenty years of declining colorectal cancer rates was one of the clearest public health victories from systematic screening programs combined with lifestyle improvement — the kind of multi-decade trend that makes epidemiologists optimistic about what sustained prevention can accomplish. A reversal in that trend, particularly concentrated in younger adults, is a warning that the factors that created the original decline are being partially offset by new ones. Identifying which new factors are driving the reversal — diet, microbiome, obesity, or screening gaps — determines what intervention prevents the trend from accelerating.

New research published Thursday found that GLP-1 receptor agonists — the drug class that includes semaglutide (sold as Ozempic and Wegovy) and related compounds, now taken by tens of millions of people globally for weight management and type 2 diabetes — may offer significant benefits for brain health beyond their metabolic effects, with early-stage research showing that GLP-1 activity in the brain may help preserve memory function and potentially reduce risk of cognitive decline; the finding adds to a growing body of evidence that GLP-1 receptors play a role in neurological as well as metabolic regulation — GLP-1 receptors are expressed in multiple brain regions including the hippocampus and areas involved in learning and memory — with observational studies showing lower rates of Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s disease diagnoses in patients on GLP-1 drugs compared to matched controls; the brain-health finding, if confirmed in larger randomized trials, would significantly expand the therapeutic rationale for GLP-1 drugs beyond the obesity and diabetes indications that have driven their current adoption.

The GLP-1 drug class is already the most commercially successful pharmaceutical development of the decade on the basis of metabolic indications alone. If the brain-health signals hold up in randomized trials — memory preservation, reduced cognitive decline, lower Alzheimer’s risk — the potential market expands by an order of magnitude to include healthy aging populations who would take GLP-1 drugs not for weight loss but for neurological protection. That would be the most significant expansion of a drug class’s indication since statins moved from treating diagnosed cardiovascular disease to preventing it in healthy individuals.
