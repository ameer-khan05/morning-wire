The Anchor · The Heart Regenerates — What the First Human Evidence of Cardiomyocyte Renewal After a Heart Attack Changes

The dogma that the heart cannot repair itself after a heart attack has been one of medicine's most consequential negative beliefs: it implies that every myocardial infarction causes permanent, irreversible damage and that treatment can only prevent further loss, never restore what was lost. The University of Sydney's world-first study in Circulation Research overturns this not by identifying a robust self-repair mechanism but by demonstrating that a repair mechanism exists at all — cardiomyocytes do divide after a heart attack in humans, the same process confirmed in mice years earlier, and the rate is measurable and biologically characterizable. The practical significance is the gap between what is and what could be. The study team is explicit: the natural regeneration is "far too weak to replace all the cells that are lost." But they have now demonstrated that the process exists, identified some of the proteins driving it (several of which were previously shown to promote regeneration in mice), and developed a living tissue sample laboratory model that can be used to screen interventions that boost the process. The path from "the heart weakly tries to regenerate" to "the heart regenerates therapeutically" is not trivial, but it is now a defined scientific question rather than an assumption-blocked one. Every therapeutic program aimed at post-heart-attack regeneration was operating against the assumption that the cellular machinery for regeneration did not exist in humans. It does. The question is now how to amplify a signal that was already there — which is a very different problem from creating a signal from nothing. Heart disease is the leading cause of death globally, and heart attack survivors' cardiac function declines over time precisely because lost cardiomyocytes are replaced by scar tissue that cannot contract. If regenerative therapies can eventually maintain cardiac function after infarction rather than allowing it to decline, the impact on global health outcomes would be among the largest of any therapeutic advance in the 21st century. The longevity finding adds a different but complementary biological insight: living to 117 is not about avoiding the biological processes of aging. Maria Branyas experienced advanced aging — the cellular and molecular changes that accompany extreme age were present and active. What distinguished her biology was the simultaneous presence of powerful protective mechanisms that counteracted those aging processes. Longevity research has often framed the goal as "slow down aging"; this finding reframes it as "protect against aging" — a different pharmaceutical target with different biological levers.

A world-first study published in Circulation Research confirmed that human heart muscle cells regenerate after a heart attack — overturning the longstanding medical consensus that damaged cardiac tissue cannot repair itself — with University of Sydney researchers demonstrating for the first time in humans that cardiomyocytes undergo mitosis after myocardial infarction, the same process previously established in mice. The team identified several proteins associated with the regeneration process and developed a new technique for collecting living tissue samples that gave them a laboratory model for studying how to therapeutically enhance the heart's own repair capacity. ScienceDaily notes the finding "overturns the idea that damaged areas of the heart are completely incapable of regeneration."

The discovery's clinical significance depends on the gap between the natural regeneration rate and what is therapeutically sufficient. The natural post-infarction regeneration is far too weak to replace the cardiomyocytes lost in a significant heart attack, which means patients continue to lose cardiac function over time as lost muscle cells are replaced by non-contracting scar tissue. The study establishes that the biological machinery for regeneration is present in humans — which was not known before — and identifies the proteins driving it as starting points for interventions that could boost the rate. The living tissue sample model developed by the Sydney team is the practical contribution: it gives researchers a system for testing whether specific interventions accelerate the process to therapeutically meaningful rates. The distance from "the process exists" to "we can amplify it clinically" is substantial but measurable. Before this study, the distance was infinite, because the assumption was the process did not exist.

Extreme Longevity Decoded: Living Past 117 Requires Both Advanced Aging AND Powerful Biological Protection Simultaneously · Maria Branyas Study · Reframes Longevity Science: Target Is Protection, Not Aging Absence 117+ Years Maria Branyas (117+): detailed biology study (Sep 11 2026) → finding: extreme longevity ≠ absence of aging → instead: advanced aging (present, active) + powerful biological protection (counteracting) → simultaneously → two opposing processes required → advanced aging: cellular + molecular changes → powerful protection: counteracts those changes → longevity framing reframed: not "slow down aging" → instead "protect against aging" → different pharmaceutical target → longevity science: biological levers shift → ScienceDaily / SciTechDaily reporting Sep 11

A detailed study of Maria Branyas, who lived past 117 years of age, found that surviving to supercentenarian status requires two biological processes operating simultaneously in opposition: advanced aging — the cellular and molecular changes associated with extreme age were present and active in her biology — and powerful protective mechanisms that counteracted those aging processes. The finding challenges the dominant longevity research framing, which has focused on slowing or preventing the biological processes of aging; the Branyas study suggests that extreme longevity may instead require a different biological strategy — not the absence of aging, but the presence of robust protection against it while aging proceeds.

The practical research implication is a target shift. If extreme longevity requires a protective mechanism that operates alongside advanced aging rather than preventing it, then the pharmaceutical intervention that most directly extends human healthspan is not one that slows aging processes but one that boosts the protective mechanisms that counteract them. Those are different molecular targets: anti-aging drugs target the biological processes driving cellular senescence and damage; protective-mechanism drugs would target the pathways that maintain cellular function and organismal resilience despite those processes. The Branyas study identifies the latter as the operative variable in the longest-lived human biology. That is not a minor finding — it suggests that the centenarian or supercentenarian who reaches 120 in good health is not someone who aged slowly, but someone whose protection systems were unusually robust in the face of normal or even accelerated aging.

Two-Drug Combination Reverses Prostate Cancer Cellular Identity Changes — Slows Tumor Growth in Preclinical Experiments · Addresses Treatment Evasion via Lineage Plasticity · New Pathway for Treatment-Resistant Prostate Cancer Identity Prostate cancer (Sep 2026): treatment evasion mechanism → lineage plasticity: tumors switch from adenocarcinoma → neuroendocrine phenotype → escape androgen-receptor-targeted therapy → finding: two-drug combination → reversed many cellular identity changes → slowed tumor growth in preclinical experiments → mechanism: drug class 1 (likely epigenetic regulator) + drug class 2 (complementary) → combined: address identity-switch mechanism → new treatment pathway for treatment-resistant cases → preclinical: not yet human trials → implications: large unmet need (treatment-resistant prostate cancer)

Researchers demonstrated that combining two classes of drugs can reverse the cellular identity changes that allow prostate cancers to evade standard treatment, slowing tumor growth in preclinical experiments. Prostate tumors treated with androgen-receptor-targeted therapies can escape by switching cellular identity — from the adenocarcinoma type that the drugs target to a neuroendocrine phenotype that is invisible to those same drugs. The two-drug combination addresses this evasion mechanism by reversing many of the molecular changes associated with the identity switch, restoring the tumor to a form that existing therapies can recognize and attack.

The treatment-resistance problem the two-drug combination addresses is one of prostate cancer's most significant clinical obstacles. Androgen-deprivation and androgen-receptor-targeted therapies have transformed prostate cancer outcomes over the past two decades, but the cancers that relapse after these treatments often do so through lineage plasticity — the cellular identity switch that makes them invisible to the drugs that were working. The neuroendocrine prostate cancer that results from this switch is significantly harder to treat and is associated with worse outcomes than the adenocarcinoma it originated from. A pharmacological combination that can reverse the identity switch — restoring the tumor to a treatable phenotype — would address the critical juncture in prostate cancer treatment where current medicine most consistently fails. The preclinical results are the starting point for that clinical translation.
