The Anchor · The CRISPR Moment — What It Means That a Single In‑Vivo Gene Edit Has Remained Effective Four Years Later and Is Now Months Away From the First FDA Authorization of Its Kind

A single CRISPR gene edit inside a living patient’s liver, made four years ago, is still working. The FDA is expected to decide by March whether to authorize the therapy. If it does, it will be the first time the agency has approved a treatment that edits human DNA inside the living body. The distinction that matters in Intellia’s lonvo‑z data is not the efficacy figure — it is the duration. CRISPR therapies approved before lonvo‑z, including Casgevy for sickle cell disease, work by editing cells outside the body and reinfusing them — a process called ex-vivo editing. Lonvo‑z works by delivering the gene-editing machinery directly into the patient’s liver, where it disables the KLKB1 gene that produces prekallikrein, the protein that triggers hereditary angioedema attacks. That is in‑vivo editing: changing the genome of cells that remain inside the body. If the edit degrades, drifts, or triggers immune responses over time, that would appear in multi-year follow-up data. Four years of durable efficacy — the therapeutic effect maintained without additional doses — is evidence that the in-vivo edit is stable. That stability is the scientific prerequisite for the FDA to authorize in-vivo CRISPR therapy at all. What it means for medicine broadly: a stable, single-dose in-vivo gene edit opens the therapeutic possibility of treating any disease caused by a specific gene’s overproduction — a category that includes dozens of rare genetic disorders and potentially extends to common diseases where genetic variants have been identified as causal. Lonvo‑z treats one of them. The class of potential targets is much larger.

Intellia Therapeutics released data showing lonvo‑z — its investigational in‑vivo CRISPR therapy for hereditary angioedema — remains durably effective four years after a single treatment, with the therapy using the CRISPR‑Cas9 system to disable the KLKB1 gene in the liver, preventing production of prekallikrein, the protein whose accumulation triggers dangerous swelling in the throat and other tissues; the FDA has granted Priority Review status with a decision date of March 10, 2027 — and BioEthics described Tuesday’s data as the clinical milestone that makes the approval path clear — marking the first time an in‑vivo CRISPR therapy (one that edits genes inside the living body rather than in extracted cells) has met the multi‑year durability standard needed for regulatory authorization; and the Daily Briefing’s November 24 episode identified the pending approval as “CRISPR’s FDA moment,” noting that successful in‑vivo authorization would open the class of single-gene diseases to a treatment category that did not exist five years ago.

The in‑vivo/ex‑vivo distinction is worth explaining precisely. Ex‑vivo CRISPR therapy (like Casgevy) removes cells from the patient, edits them in a lab, and reinfuses them — a process that is effective but expensive, complex, and only applicable to cell types that can survive extraction and reinfusion. In‑vivo therapy delivers the edit directly to cells inside the body — in lonvo‑z’s case, to liver cells via lipid nanoparticle delivery. The in‑vivo route is simpler to administer and applicable to organs that cannot be extracted. If lonvo‑z is authorized, it validates the in‑vivo route for the full range of single-gene liver diseases where a genetic cause has been identified.

A major longitudinal study of 113,000 people published Tuesday found that cardiovascular disease warning signs — including elevated blood pressure and elevated concentrations of specific blood proteins associated with vascular stress — can appear measurably in the clinical record 30 years before a cardiac event, suggesting that the preventive intervention window for heart disease is far longer than the five-to-ten‑year window that current clinical screening protocols typically target; the 113,000-person sample size provides statistical power to identify low-level but persistent signal in patients who eventually experienced heart attacks or strokes, distinguishing their early biomarker profiles from those who did not, per StudyFinds and Medical News Today’s coverage of Tuesday’s health research; and the finding suggests that blood pressure management and anti-inflammatory interventions in patients in their 30s and 40s — rather than waiting for elevated risk indicators in their 50s and 60s — could substantially reduce the eventual cardiac event rate.

Thirty years of advance warning is a clinical opportunity if the healthcare system is designed to act on it and a clinical frustration if it is not. Most preventive cardiology protocols are triggered by risk factors that appear 5‑10 years before events — the timescale that primary care visits, insurance coverage, and patient motivation can support. A 30-year signal requires an entirely different kind of clinical infrastructure: longitudinal monitoring, early‑life biomarker tracking, and sustained intervention over decades. The science now says the window is there. Whether the clinical system is designed to use it is a different question entirely.

Researchers announced Tuesday the identification of a new subtype of depression that may affect nearly one in four people with severe, recurrent, or persistent depressive disorder — a subtype characterized by distinct neurobiological markers that may explain why standard antidepressant treatments fail to produce full remission in a substantial fraction of patients, per ScienceDaily and Medical News Today’s coverage of Tuesday’s mental health research; separately, a meta-analysis of 149 published studies found that people who use cannabis develop psychosis an average of 2.5 years earlier than non-users, with the difference growing to more than six years among adults aged 30 and older — a finding researchers described as a dose‑duration effect consistent with cannabis’s known interaction with dopamine pathways involved in psychosis onset; and the two findings together characterize the week’s mental health research as focused on subtypes and risk stratification: identifying which patients have treatment‑resistant depression for distinct biological reasons, and which cannabis users face elevated psychosis risk based on age and duration of use.

The depression subtype identification is clinically significant precisely because treatment-resistant depression is currently managed by trial-and-error escalation — trying successively stronger antidepressants or combination therapies until one works. If a subtype exists that is biologically distinct — not treatment-resistant for social or psychological reasons but for neurobiological ones — targeted treatment for that subtype would change outcomes for the one-in-four patients currently cycling through ineffective protocols. The research question now is whether the subtype has a specific treatment target, not just a diagnostic marker.

A new study published Tuesday found that women taking GLP‑1 receptor agonist weight-loss drugs — including semaglutide products marketed as Ozempic and Wegovy — may see higher breast density measurements and consequently require more follow-up mammograms, according to Medical News Today’s coverage of Tuesday’s health research; breast density is a known risk factor for breast cancer and a measure used to assess mammogram quality, with higher density associated with both increased cancer risk and reduced mammogram sensitivity; and the finding adds to the accumulating evidence on GLP‑1 drugs’ systemic effects beyond their intended weight‑loss mechanism — a list that now includes potential cardiac benefits, potential fertility effects, and, as of Tuesday, potential changes to breast tissue density that have screening implications for the tens of millions of women currently taking these medications.

GLP‑1 drugs’ systemic effects are being discovered faster than any clinical guidance framework can process them. The breast density finding is a screening implication, not a safety signal: it suggests women on GLP‑1 drugs may need more frequent mammogram follow‑ups or more sensitive imaging than the standard protocol provides. That is a resource and logistics question for mammography programs, not a reason to stop taking the medications. But it is the kind of secondary effect that the drugs’ rapid adoption — without long enough follow-up to have discovered it in clinical trials — means clinicians are now learning in real time.
