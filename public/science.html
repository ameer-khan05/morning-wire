The Anchor · PACMAN and the 20-Millisecond Window — How Princeton's Fusion AI Moved from Simulation to Real Reactor Control

PACMAN's significance in the fusion AI landscape is in its deployment setting: not a simulation, not a training environment, but five real tokamak experiments at the Princeton Plasma Physics Laboratory, with the AI making closed-loop plasma control decisions in real time and the results peer-reviewed and published in Nuclear Fusion. Most AI-for-fusion work to date has operated in simulation — training models on historical plasma data, testing on replay, projecting performance estimates onto live reactors. PACMAN is different because it has been run live: the AI is in the loop, reading sensor data, making a decision in 20 milliseconds, and issuing that decision to the plasma control system before the instability it predicted 200 milliseconds in advance has time to develop. The 200-millisecond prediction window is PACMAN's operational contribution. Plasma instabilities in tokamaks — primarily disruptions, which can terminate a plasma shot and damage the reactor wall — develop on timescales of tens to hundreds of milliseconds. A 200ms warning gives the control system enough time to either correct plasma parameters or initiate a controlled shutdown, vs the narrower window available to conventional sensor-threshold systems. The fact that PACMAN made correct control decisions in 5 real experiments — not 5 simulations, but 5 live reactor shots — is the validation that distinguishes this from a paper-based result. Five experiments is a small number in physical science terms, and PPPL's tokamak is a research machine, not a power-producing reactor. The gap between a PPPL demonstration and a commercial device is real. But the direction is correct: AI moving from fusion simulation to fusion experiment is the prerequisite for AI eventually moving from fusion experiment to fusion reactor operation. PACMAN is the first validated step in that transition, and Nuclear Fusion is the appropriate journal — peer-reviewed, domain-specific, the venue where plasma physicists will assess the methodology. UC San Diego's 8-letter DNA work is a different kind of result — not a control system improvement but an expansion of the information substrate itself. All known life uses four nucleotide bases: adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C), and guanine (G). Researchers have synthesized additional base pairs, creating expanded alphabets. The step that UCSD demonstrated is not the synthesis but the readout: a cellular polymerase that can accurately read all 8 letters in a living cell. That is the bottleneck synthetic biology has been working on. If a cell can read an 8-letter message, the information density of a synthetic genetic payload doubles. Implications follow for drug delivery precision, for creating organisms that cannot exchange genetic information with natural life (biosafety), and for information storage in biological media.

UC San Diego researchers demonstrated the first cellular enzyme capable of reading an 8-letter genetic alphabet in a living cell — doubling the four-letter code (A, T, C, G) used in all known life — the first time expanded genetic information has been successfully read out in vivo, clearing the bottleneck that has limited synthetic expanded-alphabet DNA to test-tube applications. The result means a cell can be given an 8-letter genetic message and its own enzymatic machinery can read it, which doubles the information density of any synthetic genetic payload and opens a path to genetic systems that are orthogonal to natural DNA.

Orthogonality is the biosafety implication: a genetic system built on 8 letters cannot exchange information with organisms built on 4, because their polymerases cannot read each other's code. That is relevant to both synthetic biology containment (engineered organisms that cannot share genes with natural ones) and drug delivery (genetic payloads that are addressed only to cells that express the 8-letter polymerase). The information storage implication is also significant: biological media store data at approximately 1 exabyte per cubic centimeter of DNA; an 8-letter alphabet increases the bits per position from log2(4)=2 to log2(8)=3, a 50% density gain per nucleotide. Peer review is the appropriate filter for these claims, and the UCSD result is published at a high-impact journal. Previous 8-letter DNA synthesis work from the Benner lab established the chemistry; UCSD's advance is the live-cell enzymatic readout that makes the chemistry biologically operational.

Researchers identified the SET protein — a chromatin regulator — as a novel target in glioblastoma (GBM), demonstrating in a preclinical study that blocking SET prevents tumor formation in GBM cell models, providing a new potential druggable target for the most aggressive primary brain tumor, which currently carries a median survival of 14-16 months with standard-of-care treatment of surgery, radiation, and temozolomide chemotherapy. The SET protein is an epigenetic regulator overexpressed in GBM and appears necessary for tumor maintenance; blocking it disrupted tumor formation in the preclinical setting. Clinical translation is the next step, and the gap from preclinical to clinical in GBM has historically been wide — the tumor is well-studied with many preclinical hits that failed in humans — but SET blockade represents a mechanism distinct from current GBM therapeutics.

Separately, the American College of Cardiology and American Society of Nephrology released updated joint guidelines on cardiovascular-kidney risk management, finding that early integrated screening and treatment substantially reduces joint mortality and morbidity versus treating cardiovascular disease and chronic kidney disease independently. The updated guidelines integrate CKD staging into cardiovascular risk scoring for the first time, providing clinicians a unified risk model. The practical implication is bidirectional: patients with early-stage CKD should be screened for cardiovascular risk at presentation, and patients with cardiovascular disease should be screened for kidney function decline — a change that alters standard-of-care referral pathways between internal medicine, cardiology, and nephrology.
