ISS National Lab announced three new biomedical research batches on Thursday: Redpoint Oncology, a recipient of the Boeing and ISS National Lab Technology in Space Prize, will evaluate a novel class of antibody-drug conjugate cancer therapies using patient-derived tumor organoids grown in microgravity — targeting cancers resistant to conventional treatment; Stanford University and the Palo Alto Veterans Institute for Research will leverage engineered human muscle tissue in zero gravity to identify new treatment options for sarcopenia, an age-related condition causing progressive muscle loss that affects an estimated third of adults over 65; and Wake Forest Institute for Regenerative Medicine will continue maturing bioprinted liver tissue containing blood vessels in the microgravity environment that enables three-dimensional tissue structures conventional laboratory conditions cannot replicate; a sweeping analysis of GLP-1 receptor agonist clinical trials found that semaglutide and tirzepatide produce broadly consistent weight loss outcomes across age, race, and ethnicity with one consistent exception: women lose more weight than men on average across all trials reviewed; and a Cleveland Clinic study published in eClinicalMedicine found that patients who switch from semaglutide to tirzepatide after plateauing achieve greater two-year weight loss than those who stay on semaglutide, and patients who proceed to bariatric surgery after GLP-1 therapy achieve the best two-year outcomes of all strategies studied.

Thursday’s science beat has two parallel threads. The first is microgravity as a platform: the ISS’s three research batches leverage the unique cellular behavior that zero gravity induces in living tissue — three-dimensional tumor organoid growth, muscle tissue mechanics under altered loading, vascularized tissue maturation — to study problems that conventional laboratory conditions cannot model with the same fidelity. The second is GLP-1 optimization: with more than a million Americans on semaglutide or tirzepatide, the defining clinical questions are no longer whether these drugs work but for whom they work best and what comes next when they plateau.

The Anchor · Microgravity as a Laboratory — Why the ISS Enables Cancer, Muscle, and Tissue Research That Ground-Based Science Cannot Replicate

The three ISS National Lab research batches announced Thursday share a common scientific rationale: microgravity allows living tissue to behave in ways that reveal biology that gravity conceals. In a standard Earth-based laboratory, cells grow in two-dimensional cultures on flat surfaces because gravity pulls them down. That flat growth produces an artificial cellular environment: cells interact with each other along a single plane, receive nutrients and oxygen through a medium that flows in one direction, and develop without the mechanical loading pressures that gravity imposes on tissue in the human body. In microgravity, cells grow in three dimensions — forming spheroids and organoids that more accurately replicate the structural properties of real tumors, real muscle tissue, and real organ architecture. For cancer research, that three-dimensional growth produces tumor organoids that respond to antibody-drug conjugate therapies in ways that more closely predict human outcomes than two-dimensional cell cultures. For sarcopenia research, removing the constant mechanical loading gravity imposes on muscle tissue allows researchers to study muscle fiber behavior under conditions that isolate the cellular mechanisms of age-related decline from the mechanical effects of daily weight-bearing activity. For bioprinted liver tissue, microgravity allows the three-dimensional vascular structure — the network of blood vessels that supplies the liver’s internal architecture — to mature without the gravitational deformation that collapses vascular channels in Earth-based bioprinting. Each of the three ISS research batches is using the same fundamental platform — the absence of gravity — to study a different biological problem. That is not coincidence. It is a deliberate expansion of the ISS National Lab’s commercial research mandate: use the unique conditions of a facility that costs hundreds of millions of dollars per year to maintain as a laboratory for the specific problems that only those conditions can address.

Redpoint Oncology, a recipient of the Boeing and ISS National Lab Technology in Space Prize, will evaluate a novel class of antibody-drug conjugate cancer therapies aboard the ISS using patient-derived tumor organoids grown in microgravity — three-dimensional tumor structures that more accurately replicate the structural and cellular properties of human tumors than conventional two-dimensional cell cultures — with the research specifically targeting cancers resistant to conventional therapies, a population where ADC technology’s precision targeting may offer an alternative when standard-of-care treatments have failed; ADC therapies combine a cancer-targeting antibody with a cytotoxic drug payload, delivering the therapeutic payload directly to cancer cells while sparing surrounding healthy tissue — a mechanism that connects to Wednesday’s Eli Lilly $5.9 billion Stipple Bio deal targeting cell-surface discrimination in oncology; the ISS’s microgravity environment allows patient-derived tumor organoids to develop three-dimensional growth patterns not achievable in Earth-based culture, producing experimental results that the ISS National Lab has previously argued are more predictive of human clinical outcomes.

The patient-derived organoid approach is the most clinically sophisticated element of Redpoint’s ISS research design. By using tumor organoids derived from actual patient tissue — rather than established cancer cell lines — Redpoint is studying ADC efficacy in a system that preserves the genetic heterogeneity and drug-resistance mechanisms of real tumors. Combined with microgravity’s three-dimensional growth conditions, the research design more closely approximates a clinical trial in tissue than any ground-based model available. The limitation is scale: organoid studies produce mechanistic data, not statistical power. The path from ISS organoid result to Phase I clinical trial still requires substantial additional development.

Stanford University and the Palo Alto Veterans Institute for Research will leverage engineered human muscle tissue in microgravity to identify new treatment options for sarcopenia — an age-related condition causing progressive muscle loss and weakness that affects an estimated third of adults over 65 and significantly increases fall, fracture, and hospitalization risk — with the microgravity environment allowing researchers to study muscle fiber maintenance and degradation under conditions that isolate cellular mechanisms from the mechanical loading effects of gravity that Earth-based studies cannot remove; the Wake Forest Institute for Regenerative Medicine will continue maturing bioprinted liver tissue containing blood vessel networks in the ISS environment, with microgravity enabling three-dimensional vascular structures that collapse under gravity’s weight in ground-based bioprinting and represent the primary technical barrier to creating transplantable bioprinted organ tissue; both programs are part of the Northrop Grumman CRS-25 mission payload announced by ISS National Lab Thursday.

The sarcopenia research addresses the most underestimated age-related medical burden in developed health systems. Sarcopenia’s progressive muscle loss leads directly to the falls, fractures, and functional decline that drive elderly patients into long-term care — a clinical cascade that costs health systems more than any single disease most people associate with aging. Stanford’s microgravity approach to studying muscle tissue mechanics could identify molecular targets for interventions that GLP-1 drugs — which cause muscle loss alongside fat loss — have made more urgent to develop. Wake Forest’s vascularized liver tissue work addresses the organ transplant shortage’s most technical barrier: keeping printed tissue alive by giving it blood supply.

A sweeping analysis of dozens of GLP-1 receptor agonist clinical trials found that semaglutide (Ozempic/Wegovy) and tirzepatide (Mounjaro/Zepbound) produce broadly similar weight loss outcomes across age, race, ethnicity, and baseline health measures — with one consistent exception across all trials reviewed: women lose more weight than men on average on both agents; a separate study from Neuroscience News reported that individual response variation to GLP-1 drugs may be linked to genetics, with the body’s hidden metabolic slowdown response — a compensatory reduction in energy expenditure that the brain triggers in response to caloric deficit — varying by individual genetic profile and potentially explaining why some patients achieve dramatic 20%+ body weight reductions while others on identical dosing regimens lose only 5-7%; and investigators are using the genetic variability finding to develop personalized GLP-1 dosing and combination strategies that could improve outcomes for the estimated 20-30% of patients who are considered non-responders or low-responders to standard GLP-1 therapy.

The GLP-1 gender finding has an immediate clinical implication: treatment protocols that apply the same dosing and outcome expectations to male and female patients are undercounting women’s drug efficacy and potentially over-medicating men relative to what their biological response warrants. The genetics-based variability finding is more complex — individual metabolic compensation is real and measurable, but the genetic markers that predict it are not yet validated to the standard required for clinical prescribing decisions. The gap between the finding and its clinical application is the near-term GLP-1 research agenda.

A Cleveland Clinic study published in eClinicalMedicine found that patients who initially took injectable GLP-1 drugs — semaglutide or tirzepatide — achieved the greatest two-year weight loss if they subsequently had bariatric surgery, with patients who switched from semaglutide to tirzepatide achieving greater weight loss over two years than those who stayed on semaglutide; the study followed patients who had plateaued on their initial GLP-1 regimen, examining four treatment escalation strategies — stay on current GLP-1, switch within class, switch to other anti-obesity medication, or proceed to bariatric surgery — with the tirzepatide switch producing the best pharmacological outcome and bariatric surgery the best overall outcome regardless of initial GLP-1 choice; the findings are clinically significant for the growing population of patients who have achieved initial weight loss on semaglutide but face the common plateau where the drug’s efficacy diminishes as the body adapts, leaving clinicians without a clear evidence-based next step — Cleveland Clinic’s two-year data now provides that evidence.

The Cleveland Clinic study is the most practically useful piece of GLP-1 research published this week because it addresses the specific clinical decision millions of patients and physicians are facing right now: what do you do when Ozempic stops working? The answer — switch to tirzepatide or proceed to bariatric surgery — is not novel to practitioners who have been making those decisions individually. What the study provides is the evidence base that justifies escalation to insurance payors and clinical guidelines. The tirzepatide switch producing better outcomes than staying on semaglutide will accelerate tirzepatide’s market share capture in the GLP-1 class as those guidelines update.
