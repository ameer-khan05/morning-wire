The Anchor · The Silverpit Mystery Solved — How Seismic Images and a Single Oil-Well Core Sample Confirmed a 43-Million-Year-Old Asteroid Impact Beneath the North Sea

The Silverpit Crater was discovered in 2002 from seismic surveys of the North Sea, but geologists could not agree on what created it: the concentric ring structure was consistent with an asteroid impact but also consistent with salt dissolution and internal geological collapse. Wednesday’s confirmation resolves the debate with two independent lines of physical evidence that salt dissolution cannot explain. The first evidence line is the newly available high-resolution seismic imaging of the crater’s subsurface structure, which shows the shock-wave pressure signatures characteristic of hypervelocity impact rather than the gradual deformation of geological subsidence. The second — and more decisive — is the microscopic mineral crystals recovered from rock cuttings in an oil well drilled near the crater. These crystals, called shocked quartz, form only under the extreme pressure of a hypervelocity impact — pressures that geological processes cannot produce. Shocked quartz is the definitive physical signature of an asteroid or comet impact, and its presence in the Silverpit area eliminates every non-impact explanation for the crater’s formation. The asteroid’s estimated size — 160 meters wide — places it at the upper end of medium-sized impactors: large enough to produce a 330-foot tsunami that would have inundated what are now the coastlines of Britain, Denmark, and Norway, but small enough to have had primarily regional rather than global consequences, which is why the impact left no clear global extinction signature in the geological record. The 43-to-46-million-year age, in the Eocene epoch, predates human presence by tens of millions of years. The practical legacy of the Silverpit confirmation is methodological: it demonstrates that shocked quartz recovery from existing oil-well core samples can be used to verify impact craters across the North Sea and other heavily drilled sedimentary basins — potentially revealing other ancient impact sites that have been hiding in plain sight inside the oil industry’s existing geological data.

Scientists confirmed Wednesday that the Silverpit Crater, buried 700 meters beneath the North Sea seabed and discovered in 2002, was formed when a 160-meter-wide asteroid struck Earth 43 to 46 million years ago, triggering a tsunami more than 330 feet high that inundated the coastlines of what are now Britain, Denmark, and Norway — with the breakthrough coming from two independent evidence lines: high-resolution seismic images showing hypervelocity pressure signatures, and microscopic shocked-quartz crystals recovered from rock cuttings in an oil well near the crater, which can only form under asteroid-impact pressures that geological processes cannot replicate.

Shocked quartz is the definitive physical signature of hypervelocity impact and eliminates every alternative explanation for Silverpit’s formation. The debate since 2002 centered on whether the concentric ring structure was impact-created or formed by salt dissolution and geological collapse; shocked quartz is incompatible with the latter mechanism. The methodological significance of the discovery extends beyond Silverpit: recovering shocked quartz from existing oil-well core samples — without new drilling — provides a template for verifying other potentially impact-created structures across the North Sea and other heavily drilled basins, using geological data already in the oil industry’s archives.

Texas A&M University researchers identified a blood biomarker for Alzheimer’s disease that is elevated in adults as young as 45 who express concerns about their memory or thinking — even when brain scans show no detectable structural changes — establishing that biological signals linked to Alzheimer’s can emerge in midlife, years before the disease reaches the stage where conventional imaging identifies abnormalities, and providing a potential window for intervention before the irreversible neurological damage that defines late-stage Alzheimer’s has occurred; related research found that earlier changes in executive function may provide additional early warning signals detectable in the same midlife window.

The practical significance of the age-45 detection threshold is that it transforms the disease’s clinical trajectory: Alzheimer’s has historically been identified when patients present with dementia symptoms, typically in their 60s or 70s, at which point substantial neurological damage has already occurred and the intervention window for disease-modifying treatments is limited. A blood biomarker detectable at 45 — before structural brain changes appear on any imaging — opens a 15 to 20-year window in which biological risk can be identified and potentially treated before the disease progresses to symptomatic stages. The non-invasive nature of a blood test, compared to the PET scans and lumbar punctures that current early Alzheimer’s detection requires, makes midlife screening practically feasible at population scale in a way that existing tools do not.

GSK won FDA approval Wednesday for a hepatitis B drug that achieves functional cures — defined as sustained loss of the viral surface antigen and control of infection without ongoing treatment — in substantially more patients than existing methods, which have historically suppressed hepatitis B virus replication without achieving the functional cure that eliminates long-term risk of cirrhosis and liver cancer; hepatitis B remains one of the world’s most prevalent chronic infections, with hundreds of millions of people living with chronic infection and existing standard-of-care antivirals controlling rather than curing the disease in the vast majority of patients.

The functional cure distinction is the medical significance of the approval: hepatitis B’s current standard of care with nucleoside analog antivirals suppresses viral replication effectively but must be maintained indefinitely, because the virus persists in the form of covalently closed circular DNA that existing drugs cannot eliminate. A drug that achieves functional cure at a meaningfully higher rate than previous methods — measured as sustained surface antigen clearance, which correlates with long-term viral control — changes the treatment goal from indefinite suppression to finite cure for a larger proportion of patients, with direct implications for both patient quality of life and the long-term risk of the liver disease complications that chronic hepatitis B drives.

Priovant Therapeutics, a subsidiary of Roivant Sciences, reported positive results across two Phase 3 studies of Lisraya — a JAK inhibitor — in non-infectious uveitis, an inflammatory eye disease of the uveal tract that is a leading cause of preventable blindness among working-age adults; both studies met their primary endpoints, clearing the way for a regulatory submission, and addressing a condition for which current treatment relies on corticosteroids and immunosuppressants that carry significant systemic side effects with limited long-term efficacy in many patients.

Non-infectious uveitis affects the middle layer of the eye across anterior, intermediate, posterior, and pan-uveitis subtypes; severe or recurrent cases cause progressive visual impairment and blindness in patients who are often in their 30s and 40s — working-age adults whose vision loss carries substantial individual and economic consequences. The absence of an approved, targeted treatment has meant clinicians rely on broad immunosuppressants that control inflammation but create systemic immunosuppression as a side effect. A JAK inhibitor targeting the specific inflammatory pathway driving uveitis offers a more targeted intervention with a more favorable side-effect profile if the Phase 3 data translate to regulatory approval, which the positive primary endpoint results from two studies substantially supports.
