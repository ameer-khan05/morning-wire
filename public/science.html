The Anchor · CRISPR CD33 — The Elegance of Editing the Target Out of Friendly Cells

The CRISPR CD33 approach to blood cancer therapy is elegant in the engineering sense: it solves a problem not by making the cancer-killing drug more precise, but by making the healthy cells invisible to it. The result is a cell therapy that can be applied more aggressively because the drug no longer has to spare the cells you are trying to protect. CD33 is a surface protein expressed on myeloid cells — including the blast cells that characterize aggressive blood cancers like acute myeloid leukemia. Drugs targeting CD33 kill CD33-expressing cells effectively, but the problem is that the healthy donor stem cells used in transplants also express CD33. A drug aggressive enough to kill the cancer also destroys the donor stem cells, which is the therapeutic version of burning down the neighborhood to catch the thief. CRISPR editing allows researchers to remove CD33 from donor stem cells before they are transplanted, creating a population of therapeutic cells that does not express the target protein. When the CD33-targeting drug is administered after transplant, it kills CD33-expressing cells — which now means cancer cells only, because the donor stem cells have been edited to stop expressing CD33. The edited donor cells survive and continue their therapeutic function; the cancer cells, which cannot be edited in advance, receive the full force of the CD33-targeting drug without any protection. The mechanism is not novel as a concept — editing cells to evade a drug has been explored before — but the CRISPR precision and the specific CD33-AML application are the advance: CD33-positive AML is an aggressive cancer with high unmet need and a validated target, and the obstacle to CD33-targeting cell therapy has specifically been the healthy-cell collateral damage problem that this approach addresses. The REM sleep study is a different kind of scientific advance — not a mechanism-level therapeutic finding but a population-scale behavioral observation. 95,000 people, 83 conditions, one modifiable behavior: the finding is that spending more time in REM sleep is associated with lower risk across 83 conditions including dementia and heart failure. The statistical power of 95,000 is large enough to make small effects statistically detectable, which is why the count of associated conditions is so high. This is not “REM sleep cures dementia” — it is “in a population of 95,000 people, those who spent more time in REM sleep had measurably lower rates of 83 conditions, and the association is statistically significant.” The implication is that REM sleep duration is among the most broadly impactful modifiable health behaviors currently measurable — more broadly impactful than most specific dietary or exercise interventions, because the effect signature spans 83 conditions rather than being concentrated in a single disease area. The NIH $170 million reproducibility initiative and the US biomedical funding cut stories are the institutional context for both results: findings like CRISPR CD33 and the REM sleep study require replication and validation to become clinical practice, and the infrastructure that does that replication — independent laboratories, funded research teams, peer-reviewed replication studies — is the thing being threatened by funding cuts and the thing being defended by the NIH reproducibility investment.

Researchers used CRISPR gene editing to remove the CD33 surface protein from donor stem cells before transplant, creating a therapeutic cell population that survives a CD33-targeting cancer drug while cancer cells — which cannot be pre-edited — are destroyed by the same drug, opening a targeted cell therapy pathway for CD33-positive acute myeloid leukemia and related aggressive blood cancers that currently lack effective curative options at advanced stages. The approach specifically solves the collateral damage problem that has limited CD33-targeting therapy: the drug no longer destroys healthy donor cells because those cells no longer express the target protein.

The mechanism’s elegance is its specificity: instead of making the cancer-killing drug more precise — a genuinely hard engineering problem — researchers made the healthy cells invisible to it, which is a more tractable problem that CRISPR is well-suited to solve. The donor stem cell editing is done ex vivo — outside the patient’s body, before transplant — which means the editing occurs under controlled conditions rather than requiring in-vivo delivery of CRISPR machinery. That ex vivo editing model has already been validated for other blood disorders; applying it to the CD33 AML context has a cleaner path to clinical trial design than approaches requiring in-vivo gene editing at tumor sites.

A large observational study tracking more than 95,000 people found that those who spent more time in REM sleep had significantly lower risks across 83 medical conditions — including dementia, heart failure, and a range of metabolic and inflammatory conditions — providing the most statistically powerful evidence to date that REM sleep duration is among the most broadly impactful modifiable health behaviors measurable, with an effect signature spanning more disease categories than most specific dietary or exercise interventions. The study’s 95,000-person sample size gives it statistical power to detect associations too small to emerge from smaller clinical studies.

The 83-condition breadth is the study’s most striking feature: most behavioral health studies show strong associations in a narrow disease domain — diet and cardiovascular disease, exercise and metabolic outcomes, sleep and cognitive function. Finding statistically significant associations across 83 conditions in a single study suggests REM sleep duration has a systemic rather than domain-specific effect on health, likely through mechanisms related to inflammation regulation, hormonal cycling, memory consolidation, and immune system function that REM sleep enables and insufficient REM sleep impairs. The modifiable behavior implication is direct: if REM duration is causally connected to even a fraction of the 83 associations, the health return on improving sleep quality is higher than almost any other single behavioral intervention currently being tracked at population scale.

NIH $170M Biomedical Reproducibility Initiative · Research Integrity · Replication Science · Independent Validation · Reproducibility Crisis Context · Clinical Translation Infrastructure NIH $170M NIH (Sep 24-25 2026): $170M → reproducibility initiative → biomedical → replication studies → independent validation → clinical translation → reproducibility crisis: context → many published results → do not replicate → drug development: failures → failed replications → earlier-stage validation: solution → NIH: investing in the infrastructure

The National Institutes of Health announced a $170 million initiative to address biomedical research reproducibility — funding independent replication of high-priority published results, investing in statistical methodology training, and supporting the development of reporting standards that make findings easier to replicate — in response to a sustained concern in the scientific community that a meaningful fraction of published biomedical results do not survive independent replication, creating a pipeline of failed drug development efforts and eroding confidence in the evidence base for clinical practice. The initiative represents one of the largest single federal investments in research methodology infrastructure.

The reproducibility problem in biomedical research is not marginal: multiple large-scale replication projects across psychology, oncology, and preclinical pharmacology have found that a substantial fraction of published results — sometimes a majority — do not replicate when tested by independent teams with appropriate statistical power. The consequences are downstream: drug development programs built on non-replicable preclinical results fail at clinical trial stages, wasting hundreds of millions of dollars and years of development time per failed program. The NIH’s $170 million investment in the infrastructure that makes findings replicable earlier in the pipeline is, in expected-value terms, one of the highest-return investments available in biomedical research because it filters out non-replicable results before they consume clinical trial resources.

Climate scientists warned that the world is bracing for the worst effects of a Super El Niño event — a strengthening El Niño cycle with anomalously warm Pacific sea surface temperatures that historically precede record-breaking global temperature spikes, agricultural disruption across South America and Southeast Asia, and intensified storm seasons — as a separate reporting wave documented the US biomedical research disruption caused by federal funding cuts under the Trump administration, with scientists describing lab closures, job losses, and a shrinking research pipeline that may take years to rebuild.

The Super El Niño warning arrives at a moment when global average temperatures are already tracking at record levels, which means the additive warming effect of a strong El Niño event may push multiple climate indicators past thresholds that were previously approached but not breached. The US biomedical funding story is the institutional-context story for the week’s science beat: the CRISPR CD33 result and the REM sleep study represent the kind of research output that requires sustained federal funding to generate, validate, and translate — and the infrastructure that produces those results is what the funding cuts are reducing. The NIH’s $170 million reproducibility initiative announced on the same day as the funding cut stories is the most visible institutional response to the squeeze: defending the research quality standards at the same time the research quantity is being reduced.
