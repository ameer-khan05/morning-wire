The Anchor · The Pancreatic Breakthrough — What 60.7% Tumor Shrinkage Means in a Cancer Where the Historical Response Rate Was 23–42% and Most Patients Were Dead Within a Year

Pancreatic cancer is the benchmark against which oncology measures intractability. Daraxonrasib’s 60.7% response rate is a number that researchers in the field read against decades of near-failure. More than 90% of pancreatic cancers are driven by a mutated RAS gene, which keeps sending growth signals that cannot be stopped by conventional chemotherapy. For decades, oncologists described RAS as “undruggable” — the protein structure is smooth and featureless, with no obvious pocket for a drug molecule to bind. That consensus held until a series of drugs targeting specific RAS mutation subtypes (beginning with KRAS G12C inhibitors, then expanding) began reaching clinical trials in the late 2010s and early 2020s. Daraxonrasib is the most advanced pan-RAS inhibitor — it targets RAS regardless of which specific mutation is present, covering more than 90% of pancreatic cancer patients rather than a subset. The phase 1/2 trial enrolled 61 patients with metastatic pancreatic cancer who had not yet received treatment. The standard of care — gemcitabine and nab-paclitaxel chemotherapy — produces tumor shrinkage in 23–42% of patients in major trials. Daraxonrasib paired with those same chemotherapy drugs shrank tumors in 60.7% — a 50–160% improvement over the control. Halting growth in 90.2% (the disease control rate) means only one in ten patients saw tumor progression through the trial period. These are phase 1/2 numbers from a single-arm trial with no comparison group — the caveats are real. But in a disease where oncologists have learned to celebrate modest progress, 60.7% is not a marginal advance. It is a potential paradigm shift, pending the Phase 3 data that Revolution Medicines has not yet completed.

Revolution Medicines published phase 1/2 trial data Wednesday showing daraxonrasib — an oral daily pill targeting the RAS oncogene driving more than 90% of pancreatic cancers — shrank tumors in 60.7% of 61 patients with previously untreated metastatic pancreatic cancer when paired with standard gemcitabine and nab-paclitaxel chemotherapy, halting tumor growth in 90.2% of participants and producing complete tumor disappearance on scans in 8.2%, with Medical Daily reporting the results against a historical chemotherapy response rate of 23–42%; only 5% of patients discontinued treatment due to daraxonrasib-specific side effects; and LADbible and EMJ Reviews both characterized the results as “the biggest breakthrough in decades” for a cancer where the five-year survival rate below 5% has remained essentially unchanged since the 1970s — a disease where the mutated RAS gene was described as “undruggable” by the oncology community for most of the past 40 years until a series of increasingly broad RAS inhibitors demonstrated that the protein’s growth signaling can be blocked pharmacologically with a daily oral drug that most patients can tolerate.

The “undruggable” label on RAS was not pessimism — it was structural. The RAS protein has no obvious binding pocket for a small molecule. The drug design breakthrough that made daraxonrasib possible was finding a cryptic pocket that opens under specific conditions and designing a molecule that fits it. The pan-RAS approach — targeting the gene regardless of which of several dozen possible mutations it carries — is what makes daraxonrasib applicable to 90% of pancreatic cancer patients rather than a subset. These are Phase 1/2 numbers from a small, uncontrolled trial. Phase 3 data will be the real test. But 60.7% is a number that makes it into oncology textbooks if it holds.

Wisconsin health officials confirmed a third measles death Wednesday — an adult resident of Lafayette County — as the state’s outbreak reached 920 confirmed cases across 24 counties, making it the largest measles outbreak in the United States in 35 years, with WTMJ, WBAY, and CBS Minnesota reporting that 88 new cases were confirmed since Friday, November 20, and that 99.2% of all 920 cases — 913 patients — were in people who were unvaccinated or whose vaccination status was unknown; Wisconsin’s first measles-related deaths since 1990 have all occurred in this outbreak — two children in southwestern Wisconsin died earlier in November, and the third death on Wednesday extended the toll to three fatalities in one month; and 20 people have been hospitalized in connection with the outbreak, which the Wisconsin Department of Health Services said began in August and has expanded from its initial southwestern Wisconsin cluster through 24 counties via transmission in school, healthcare, and community settings.

A 920-case measles outbreak in a country where measles was declared eliminated in 2000 is a public health infrastructure failure, not a natural resurgence. The 99.2% unvaccinated-or-unknown figure is the entire story: measles vaccination rates below 95% allow transmission chains that can sustain an outbreak across an entire state’s geographic spread. Three deaths — two children, one adult — in a disease for which a 97%-effective vaccine has been available for 60 years is the clearest possible illustration of what vaccine-preventable disease looks like when vaccination rates drop below herd immunity thresholds.

Three cell and gene therapy advances converged this week: the first CAR T-cell therapy approved for a solid tumor — satricabtagene autoleucel (satri-cel), targeting claudin 18.2‑positive gastric cancer — treated its first international patient at the Jiahui International Cancer Center in Shanghai, extending CAR T therapy beyond blood cancers for the first time per AACR’s Cancer Discovery journal; REGENXBIO dosed the first patient in NAAVIGATE, a Phase 2b/3 trial of investigational surabgene lomparvovec (sura-vec), an AAV gene therapy for diabetic retinopathy delivered suprachoroidally — a novel delivery route that avoids vitreous injection — with the company targeting the trial as a potential path to FDA submission; and University of Michigan researchers published findings in Molecular Metabolism showing that intermittent fasting completely reversed type 2 diabetes in mice within five weeks — without insulin — by allowing pancreatic beta cells to rest and restore insulin production, with alternate-day fasting mice achieving normal blood sugar levels despite eating a high-fat diet on feeding days, while continuous calorie restriction mice maintained equivalent weight but did not reverse diabetes, per EurekaAlert and News Medical’s coverage of the mouse study.

CAR T therapy’s approval for a solid tumor is a structural milestone in cancer treatment: CAR T has been transformative in blood cancers (leukemia, lymphoma, myeloma) since the mid-2010s but consistently failed in solid tumors because tumors suppress T-cell function in the tumor microenvironment. Satri-cel’s gastric cancer approval is the first evidence that the solid tumor barrier can be crossed. The mechanism — targeting claudin 18.2, a protein highly expressed in gastric cancer — is specific to this cancer type. But it proves the class of approach is viable and opens the engineering question for other solid tumor types.

A large global study using data from nearly 2,000 perimenopausal women who tracked symptoms via the Flo Health app found that higher BMI is independently associated with greater overall symptom burden during perimenopause — particularly somatic symptoms including hot flashes and night sweats — with the results published Wednesday in Menopause, the journal of The Menopause Society, and covered by News-Medical and MedicalXpress; the analysis found that frequent physical activity — five or more times per week — was independently associated with lower total symptom burden, while mental health, smoking, and socioeconomic factors were also associated with symptom severity, suggesting that perimenopause symptom burden is modifiable through lifestyle factors rather than purely hormonal; and the study is among the first to use app-collected symptom data at scale to examine how BMI specifically correlates with perimenopause experience across a global rather than a single-country sample, providing population-level signal about which perimenopausal women are likely to experience more severe symptoms and which modifiable factors are associated with better outcomes.

The Flo Health data source is both the study’s strength and its limitation. The strength: 2,000 women tracking symptoms longitudinally via app across multiple countries is a sample and geographic diversity that clinical trial recruitment cannot easily match. The limitation: app users are self-selected — they are women engaged enough in their health to download and consistently use a symptom-tracking app. Whether the BMI-symptom association generalizes to the broader perimenopausal population is a question the app data cannot answer. The finding is real and consistent; the magnitude may differ in a more representative sample.
