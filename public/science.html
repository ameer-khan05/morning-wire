A new clinical trial published Friday found the ketogenic diet reduced liver fat by an average of 67% in just four to five months among 42 patients with obesity, prediabetes, and fatty liver disease — a significantly larger reduction than both the Mediterranean diet (45%) and a low-fat plant-forward diet (45%) achieved in the same period, with the keto group also showing the largest glucagon increases and reversing prediabetes in approximately half of participants, while producing no increase in blood fat or cholesterol; MIT researchers found that the brain’s natural waste-removal system — the glymphatic network that clears metabolic byproducts during sleep — becomes significantly less effective as people age, with the decline concentrated in the sleep stages where glymphatic clearance is most active, providing a mechanistic link between age-related sleep changes and the buildup of waste proteins associated with neurodegenerative disease; researchers published new data toward a CTE blood test that could one day enable diagnosis of chronic traumatic encephalopathy in living patients, addressing a condition that currently can only be confirmed at autopsy; and scientists discovered a rare inherited mutation in the CDK12 gene linked to aggressive prostate cancer, with all five identified carriers developing metastatic disease between ages 44 and 62.

Friday’s science beat has two parallel advances in metabolic and neurological health research. The keto liver trial quantifies a dietary intervention’s power to reverse liver fat at a scale that rivals pharmaceutical approaches. MIT’s glymphatic finding provides the mechanistic explanation for why aging sleep changes translate into neurodegenerative risk — and suggests the intervention target is sleep quality, not just sleep quantity.

The Anchor · Liver Fat at 67% — What the Keto Clinical Trial Tells You About Dietary Interventions’ Power in the GLP-1 Era and What Comes Next

The ketogenic diet cut liver fat by 67% in four months. The Mediterranean diet cut it by 45%. The plant-forward diet cut it by 45%. All three groups lost about 10% of their body weight. The difference in liver fat reduction was not about weight loss. It was about the specific metabolic mechanism that the ketogenic diet activates. Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is the most common chronic liver condition in developed countries, affecting an estimated 25% of the global population and present in a large proportion of the obesity and prediabetes population that GLP-1 drugs are now targeting. The question the keto trial answers is whether diet composition — independent of weight loss magnitude — can specifically target liver fat. The answer is yes, and the mechanism is glucagon. The keto group showed the largest increase in glucagon levels of any diet tested. Glucagon promotes fat mobilization in the liver, reducing hepatic steatosis through a hormonal pathway that is distinct from the caloric-deficit pathway that all three diets share. This matters for the GLP-1 era for a specific reason: GLP-1 drugs produce significant weight loss, and they activate glucagon-related pathways, but they also cause muscle loss alongside fat loss. A ketogenic diet that maximizes the glucagon pathway while preserving or improving metabolic markers offers a potential complement — or alternative for patients who cannot access or tolerate GLP-1 drugs — for the liver-fat component of metabolic disease. The 50% prediabetes reversal rate in the keto group adds the second clinical dimension: not only did keto reduce liver fat more than the comparators, it reversed the glycemic progression that makes fatty liver disease a pathway to type 2 diabetes for a large portion of patients who carry it.

A new clinical trial published Friday found the ketogenic diet reduced liver fat by an average of 67% over four to five months among 42 people with obesity, prediabetes, and fatty liver disease — a randomized comparison against a Mediterranean diet and a low-fat plant-forward diet that both achieved only 45% liver fat reductions despite all three groups losing approximately 10% of their body weight; the keto group produced the largest increase in glucagon — the hormone that promotes fat mobilization in the liver — and notably did not show an increase in blood fat or cholesterol, addressing the most common clinical concern about high-fat diets in patients with metabolic liver disease; approximately half of keto participants reversed their prediabetes diagnosis within the trial period, and Scientific American noted the keto approach “has greater health benefits than other popular weight-loss diets,” while EurekAlert’s coverage emphasized the glucagon-pathway mechanism as the key differentiator between keto’s liver outcomes and the other diets’ results.

The keto trial’s most clinically actionable finding is not the weight loss — all three diets achieved roughly equivalent weight loss — but the difference in liver fat reduction at the same amount of weight lost. A 67% versus 45% difference at equal weight loss is a composition effect, not a caloric effect. That means clinicians treating patients with fatty liver disease have a dietary tool that specifically targets hepatic steatosis beyond what general weight loss achieves. In an era where GLP-1 drugs are the primary weight-loss intervention, knowing that diet composition matters for liver outcomes — independent of weight change — is clinically actionable immediately.

MIT researchers found that the brain’s natural waste-removal system — the glymphatic network, which clears metabolic byproducts including amyloid-beta and tau proteins during sleep — becomes significantly less effective as people age, with the decline concentrated in the sleep stages where glymphatic clearance is most active; the finding provides a mechanistic explanation for the long-observed but poorly understood link between age-related sleep deterioration and the development of neurodegenerative diseases, suggesting that the progressive decline in glymphatic efficiency during sleep may allow waste proteins to accumulate in the brain at rates that overwhelm the brain’s other clearance mechanisms; the research identifies sleep quality — specifically the deep sleep stages where glymphatic pumping is strongest — as a potentially targetable intervention point for reducing neurodegenerative risk in aging populations, independent of the quantity of sleep that most public health guidance has focused on.

MIT’s glymphatic finding reframes the sleep-neurodegeneration relationship from a correlation into a mechanism. Prior research established that poor sleep was associated with higher dementia risk. This finding says the mechanism is glymphatic clearance failure during the specific sleep stages where clearance is most active. That mechanistic specificity matters: it means interventions that improve deep sleep quality — not just sleep duration — have a specific biological target to optimize against. It also suggests that the age-related decline in deep sleep that most older adults experience is not merely a quality-of-life issue but a brain health issue with measurable neurological consequences.

Researchers published new data toward a blood test for chronic traumatic encephalopathy — the progressive neurodegenerative disease caused by repeated brain trauma that is currently only diagnosable at autopsy — with STAT News reporting Friday that the data could one day lead to a clinical blood test that enables diagnosis in living patients; the development would transform how contact sports medicine and military medicine approach traumatic brain injury, allowing clinicians to identify CTE-associated neurological changes in active athletes and veterans rather than confirming the diagnosis post-mortem; and separately, scientists described a rare inherited mutation in the CDK12 gene linked to aggressive prostate cancer, with all five identified carriers in the study cohort developing metastatic prostate cancer between ages 44 and 62, a 100% penetrance rate that makes CDK12 mutation status potentially the strongest single genetic risk factor for metastatic prostate cancer currently identified in the literature.

The CTE blood test research is at the data-publication stage, not the validated clinical test stage — the gap between publishing promising biomarker data and having a test that meets clinical diagnostic standards can be a decade of further validation. But the direction of research is clear and the clinical need is acute: an estimated 3 million Americans play contact sports at levels where CTE risk is established, and hundreds of thousands more carry the traumatic brain injury burden from military service. The CDK12 prostate cancer finding is a more immediate clinical input: a mutation with 100% penetrance for metastatic disease in known carriers is exactly the genetic risk information that oncologists need for early surveillance and intervention decisions.

Friday’s science beat has three findings that share a common structure: biology is more modifiable than the standard of care has assumed, and the evidence is now specific enough to act on. The keto trial doesn’t just say dietary intervention affects liver fat — it says the composition of the diet, independent of weight loss, activates a specific glucagon pathway that conventional weight-loss guidance has not distinguished. MIT’s glymphatic finding doesn’t just say sleep matters for brain health — it identifies the specific mechanism and the specific sleep stage where intervention is most valuable. The CTE blood test research doesn’t just document a brain injury — it moves toward the diagnostic capability that would let medicine intervene in living patients rather than confirm damage in autopsies. Each of these findings moves the clinical frontier from correlation to mechanism, and from mechanism to intervention target. That is the arc of translational medicine at its most useful.
