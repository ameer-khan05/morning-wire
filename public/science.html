A University of Colorado Anschutz School of Medicine clinical trial of oral semaglutide — the GLP-1 receptor agonist best known as Ozempic and Rybelsus — found that adults with alcohol use disorder who took the drug showed significantly fewer heavy drinking days, consumed less alcohol per occasion, and reported weaker urges to drink than placebo-treated participants; the study also found reduced marijuana use in GLP-1-treated participants as a secondary finding, suggesting the mechanism operates across substance-reward circuitry rather than being specific to alcohol. The finding adds to a rapidly accumulating body of evidence that GLP-1 receptor agonists — approved by the FDA for type 2 diabetes and obesity — affect the dopamine-mediated reward and addiction circuitry in the brain in ways that reduce cravings for multiple substances simultaneously. No FDA-approved pharmacotherapy for alcohol use disorder (AUD) has come to market since 2006, and GLP-1 receptor agonists as a class are now the most-watched candidate class for expanding the treatment toolkit for the 29 million Americans the NIH estimates have AUD. In a separate development, NASA's WB-57 high-altitude research aircraft completed a 7-hour science flight from Torrejon, Spain on Wednesday (August 12) — the day after the ground-phase eclipse observation — capturing the first coronal dataset at Solar Cycle 25's near-maximum in nine wavelength bands from 80,000 feet, above the distorting effects of the lower atmosphere. The data is now in initial processing; peer-reviewed results are expected in 6 to 12 months.

The third science story: researchers published findings identifying the specific brain network and electrical rhythm that mediate the benefits of deep brain stimulation (DBS) for Parkinson's disease, a finding that resolves a 30-year-old puzzle about why the same DBS electrode placement reliably improves symptoms across patients despite individual variation in the precise electrode location. The network and rhythm finding suggests that DBS works by engaging a consistent functional brain circuit rather than by disrupting a specific anatomical target — a conceptual shift that opens a path toward closed-loop DBS systems that can confirm therapeutic engagement in real time rather than relying on post-operative symptom assessment.

The CU Anschutz oral semaglutide trial finding arrives at a moment when the clinical and commercial momentum of GLP-1 receptor agonists has made them the most closely watched drug class in medicine — not because their weight-loss and glycemic effects are underappreciated (they are very well understood) but because the accumulating evidence of effects on addiction, mental health, and cognition is producing a scientific picture of GLP-1 pharmacology that extends well beyond what the drugs were originally developed to do. GLP-1 receptors are not located only in the gut and pancreas — they are expressed throughout the central nervous system, including in the ventral tegmental area, nucleus accumbens, and prefrontal cortex: the core nodes of the dopamine reward circuit that mediates addiction and craving for all substances, not just food. The implication is that GLP-1 receptor agonists do not produce a substance-specific anti-craving effect — they appear to dampen the reward signal that drives craving across substances simultaneously. The CU Anschutz finding of reduced marijuana use alongside reduced alcohol use is consistent with this mechanism: both drugs engage the same dopaminergic reward circuitry, and GLP-1 receptor activation at the central level reduces the incentive salience of reward-predictive cues across the board. This has two clinical implications: first, GLP-1 receptor agonists may be useful for patients with co-occurring substance use disorders (AUD + cannabis use disorder being common comorbidities) in a way that drug-specific therapies (naltrexone for AUD, for example) cannot address simultaneously; second, the mechanism's generality means that GLP-1 therapies may produce unintended reductions in reward-seeking behavior that extend beyond the target substance — a pharmacological feature that may be therapeutic for some patients and problematic for others depending on the full profile of their reward-seeking behaviors. The FDA approval path for GLP-1 receptor agonists for AUD is currently under active discussion by the pharmacology and addiction medicine communities. The drugs are already approved for diabetes and obesity and are widely prescribed off-label for a growing list of conditions; the regulatory question for AUD is whether a new indication requires a formal new drug application (NDA) for the AUD indication specifically or whether a supplemental NDA for the existing drugs (semaglutide, tirzepatide, liraglutide) can expand the labeling. The CU Anschutz trial adds to the evidence base that the FDA requires for an indication expansion, but the trial is presumably not the final pivotal trial that the FDA requires — larger phase 3 trials adequately powered for an AUD primary endpoint are the likely requirement before an approval decision. The clinical urgency is real: 29 million Americans with AUD, a treatment-resistant condition with high morbidity and mortality, and a drug class already proven safe in diabetes and obesity with a mechanism that now appears active in addiction circuitry. The path from CU Anschutz trial to FDA approval is likely 3-5 years for a formal indication — but the off-label prescribing conversation in addiction medicine is already active, and the trial findings will accelerate that conversation considerably.

The NASA WB-57 eclipse flight from Spain on August 12 adds a different dimension to Thursday's science: it is a dataset that will not produce peer-reviewed results for 6 to 12 months, but whose scientific value is determined by timing — the first solar-maximum corona observation dataset at 80,000 feet altitude, captured during Solar Cycle 25's near-maximum activity period that no previous aircraft observation campaign has accessed (the 2017 and 2024 eclipse campaigns occurred at solar minimum and ascending phase respectively). The 9-wavelength-band coronal dataset — ultraviolet through infrared — captures coronal structure at a period when solar activity generates the most dynamic and scientifically interesting coronal features: streamers, helmet streamers, polar plumes, and coronal mass ejection source regions. The processing phase that began Thursday will extract calibrated spectra and imaging from the raw WB-57 data across all 9 bands, co-registered with 14 simultaneously operating ground and space campaigns that observed the same eclipse. The scientific value is in the simultaneous multi-altitude, multi-instrument dataset — the corona observed from 80,000 feet by the WB-57 is the same corona observed simultaneously by ground teams (with atmospheric effects), by SOHO, SDO, and PROBA-3 (without atmospheric effects but without 7-hour ground-track duration), giving solar physicists a dataset that cross-validates the coronal structure observed by all platforms simultaneously.

A University of Colorado Anschutz School of Medicine clinical trial found that adults with alcohol use disorder who took oral semaglutide — the GLP-1 receptor agonist marketed as Rybelsus for diabetes — showed significantly fewer heavy drinking days, consumed less alcohol per occasion, and reported weaker urges to drink compared to placebo-treated participants. A secondary finding showed that GLP-1-treated participants also reduced marijuana use over the trial period — a cross-substance effect consistent with GLP-1 receptor agonists operating in the brain's dopamine reward circuitry rather than in a substance-specific pathway. The finding is the latest in a rapidly building body of evidence that GLP-1 receptor agonists affect addiction and craving across multiple substances, with observational studies and small trials having previously suggested reduced opioid, alcohol, and tobacco use in patients taking GLP-1 drugs for diabetes and obesity. The CU Anschutz trial is notable because it used alcohol use disorder as a primary endpoint and enrolled participants specifically for AUD rather than studying AUD as a secondary outcome in a metabolic trial, giving it greater evidentiary weight for FDA purposes than prior observational reports.

GLP-1 receptors are expressed not only in the gut and pancreas — where they mediate insulin secretion and gastric emptying — but throughout the central nervous system, including in the ventral tegmental area, nucleus accumbens, and prefrontal cortex: the three core nodes of the dopamine mesolimbic reward circuit that underlies addiction and craving for all substances. GLP-1 receptor activation in these brain regions appears to reduce the dopamine signal that confers incentive salience on reward-predictive cues — the mechanism by which addictive substances produce craving when their associated cues (smell of alcohol, seeing other people drink, stress situations associated with drinking) are encountered. A GLP-1 receptor agonist that dampens this dopamine signal in reward circuitry would be expected to reduce craving not just for the primary substance but for all reward-seeking behaviors that engage the same circuitry — which is consistent with the CU Anschutz finding of reduced marijuana use as a secondary effect. No FDA-approved pharmacotherapy for AUD has come to market since naltrexone extensions in 2006. GLP-1 receptor agonists are now widely regarded in addiction medicine as the most promising candidate class for expanding the pharmacotherapy toolkit for AUD and potentially other substance use disorders.

NASA WB-57 Eclipse Data Processing Begins — First Solar-Maximum Corona Dataset · Spain Flight Aug 12 (7 Hours, 80,000 ft) · 9 Wavelength Bands · 14 Simultaneous Campaigns · Peer Review 6–12 Months · Solar Cycle 25 Near-Max vs. 2024 Min Comparison Now Possible WB-57 Corona WB-57 eclipse flight Aug 12 (Torrejon, Spain) · 80,000 ft: above lower-atmosphere distortion · 7 hours ground-track · 9 bands UV-IR · status: initial processing began Aug 13 · scientific value: first solar-max corona observation at altitude → coronal streamers, helmet streamers, polar plumes, CME source regions at peak Solar Cycle 25 activity · 14 simultaneous coordinated campaigns (SOHO, SDO, PROBA-3, ground teams) · peer review: 6-12 months

NASA's WB-57 high-altitude research aircraft completed a 7-hour science flight from Torrejon Air Base, Spain on Wednesday, August 12 — following the ground-phase eclipse observations — capturing the first coronal dataset at Solar Cycle 25's near-maximum activity level in nine wavelength bands from 80,000 feet altitude. The WB-57's altitude places its instruments above the bulk of the Earth's lower atmosphere, eliminating the seeing distortion, atmospheric scattering, and water vapor absorption that limit ground-based coronal observations even with sophisticated adaptive optics. The 9-band dataset spans from ultraviolet through near-infrared wavelengths, capturing coronal emission lines and broadband continuum that together provide plasma temperature maps, velocity structure, and electron density profiles of the corona at solar maximum. The data entered initial processing Thursday; preliminary calibration will produce the first look images within weeks, with fully calibrated, peer-reviewed data products expected in 6 to 12 months. The scientific value of the solar-maximum timing cannot be overstated for solar physicists: the 2017 total solar eclipse occurred near solar minimum (Solar Cycle 23-24 minimum), and the 2024 eclipse occurred during Solar Cycle 25's ascending phase — both producing historically important datasets, but neither capturing the corona at Solar Cycle 25's maximum activity level. The August 2026 campaign is the first high-altitude aircraft-based observation of a total solar eclipse at a Solar Cycle 25 activity maximum, providing the direct comparison between minimum and maximum corona that solar physicists have waited years to obtain.

The 14 simultaneously operating ground and space campaigns that observed the August 2026 eclipse alongside the WB-57 include ESA's PROBA-3 coronagraph (space-based, no atmosphere, continuous), NASA's Solar Dynamics Observatory (space-based, EUV), ESA's SOHO (space-based, optical and EUV), and ground teams at sites across the eclipse path with varying atmospheric conditions and instruments. The simultaneous multi-altitude, multi-instrument dataset allows solar physicists to cross-validate coronal structures across all platforms — confirming that features seen by the WB-57 at 80,000 feet are not atmospheric artifacts, confirming that features seen by PROBA-3 continuously match what the eclipse-ground track captured during totality, and confirming that EUV and optical structures correspond spatially. The 2026 solar-maximum dataset will be the reference observation set against which future eclipse campaigns are compared, and it arrives at a moment when solar corona modeling — critical for space weather forecasting — remains a major unsolved problem in astrophysics.

Brain Network Identified for Parkinson's DBS Benefit — Specific Network + Electrical Rhythm That Drives DBS Benefits Found · Resolves 30-Year Puzzle: Why Same Electrode Placement Works Across Patients Despite Anatomical Variation · Path to Closed-Loop DBS Systems Opens DBS Network ScienceDaily/Science News Aug 12: researchers identified specific brain network + electrical rhythm that mediates DBS benefit in Parkinson's · prior puzzle: same electrode placement → consistent benefit across patients despite individual anatomical variation → network (not anatomy) drives it · network identified → confirms functional circuit is the therapeutic target → closed-loop DBS possible → confirm engagement in real time → optimize without post-op symptom assessment

Researchers published findings on Wednesday identifying the specific brain network and electrical rhythm that mediate the therapeutic benefits of deep brain stimulation for Parkinson's disease — resolving a puzzle that has persisted since DBS was first approved by the FDA in 1997: why does the same electrode placement in the subthalamic nucleus or globus pallidus interna produce consistent symptom improvements across patients who have different brain anatomies and different disease progressions? The answer, the new research finds, is that DBS benefits are not mediated by disruption of a specific anatomical target in each individual patient — they are mediated by engagement of a consistent brain-wide functional network that includes the target nucleus but extends through multiple connected regions, and by the entrainment of a specific low-frequency electrical oscillation pattern in that network that corresponds to therapeutic benefit. The network and rhythm finding means that the therapeutic mechanism of DBS is network-level rather than local: the electrode stimulates local tissue, but the therapeutic effect propagates through a network of connected regions, and it is the network-level electrical state (the entrained rhythm) that produces clinical improvement.

The practical implication for neurotechnology is significant: if the therapeutic benefit of DBS can be identified by a specific brain network rhythm rather than inferred from post-operative symptom assessment, then a closed-loop DBS system — one that monitors the brain's electrical state in real time and adjusts stimulation to maintain the therapeutic rhythm — becomes technically feasible. Current DBS systems deliver continuous stimulation at fixed parameters set by the neurology team at programming visits, without direct feedback from the brain's functional state. A closed-loop system that detects whether the therapeutic network is in the target rhythm and adjusts stimulation accordingly could improve efficacy, reduce side effects from over-stimulation, and extend battery life by stimulating only when the brain's state requires it. The network-and-rhythm finding provides the biological signature that a closed-loop system needs to monitor: rather than measuring a proxy marker (patient-reported symptoms, accelerometer tremor data), it can measure the brain state directly and detect therapeutic engagement in real time. This represents a potential step-change in the capability of DBS as a treatment for Parkinson's and potentially other conditions where DBS is used or under investigation, including obsessive-compulsive disorder, treatment-resistant depression, and Tourette syndrome.

The GLP-1 story is the most immediately consequential for the largest number of people: 29 million Americans with AUD and a drug class that already has a proven safety record in hundreds of millions of patients worldwide for diabetes and obesity. The mechanism is not mysterious — GLP-1 receptors in the brain's reward circuitry, dopamine dampening, reduced incentive salience — and the CU Anschutz finding of cross-substance effects (reduced marijuana use alongside reduced alcohol use) is actually a feature rather than a bug if you understand the mechanism. The dopamine reward circuit is not alcohol-specific or marijuana-specific — it is substrate-general, and a drug that dampens its responsivity reduces craving for whatever reward the circuit would otherwise drive. For patients with co-occurring substance use disorders (common, not rare), a single drug that reduces craving for two substances simultaneously is a pharmacological efficiency that single-substance therapies cannot match. The WB-57 eclipse data story will matter more to science than it does to the daily news cycle: 6-12 months from now, when the first peer-reviewed results appear, they will add to the picture of the sun's corona at a moment of historical solar activity that no prior aircraft campaign has captured. The Parkinson's DBS network finding is the most immediately actionable for neurotechnology companies: it provides the biological target (a specific brain network rhythm) that closed-loop DBS systems need to monitor, and the technology to measure that rhythm intraoperatively already exists in research DBS systems. The path from finding to closed-loop regulatory approval is 5-10 years, but the finding is the foundational science that makes the path exist.

Quick hits

  • The GLP-1 mechanism's breadth across substance-reward circuitry raises an interesting long-term pharmacology question: if these drugs reduce the reward signal for substances across the board, do they also affect the reward signal for other motivated behaviors that engage the same circuitry — exercise, social interaction, creative work? The early data from patients on GLP-1 drugs for obesity and diabetes contains anecdotal reports of both reduced food reward (the intended effect) and reduced enjoyment of activities that patients previously found rewarding — a possible indication that the dopamine-dampening mechanism does not discriminate by type of reward. This is likely dose-dependent and receptor-location-specific, and it is an active area of research. For AUD treatment, where reducing the reward value of alcohol is the goal, this broadening of the reward-dampening effect is likely acceptable. For patients who are not dealing with addiction, the same mechanism might represent an unintended reduction in the reward value of activities that support wellbeing. The CU Anschutz trial's measure of marijuana use as a secondary endpoint is a step toward quantifying this — follow-on trials that measure the full profile of reward-seeking behavior changes across domains will be important for characterizing the clinical risk-benefit profile comprehensively.
  • The WB-57's role in eclipse science represents an unusual niche: a jet that is old enough that its basic airframe design dates to the 1950s (as a high-altitude reconnaissance platform, repurposed for NASA science missions) but remains scientifically competitive because it provides something that no other platform provides — long-duration, high-altitude aircraft observation with flexible payload configuration. Satellites like SOHO and SDO observe the sun continuously but cannot replicate the eclipse's natural coronagraph (the moon blocking the solar disk), and the WB-57 can follow the eclipse ground track at altitude for 7 hours in a way that ground observatories fixed to one location cannot. The August 12 flight is likely one of the final major eclipse observation campaigns for the WB-57 before the next US total solar eclipse in 2033; the scientific community will be watching the data products closely, not just for solar corona results but for what they demonstrate about aircraft-based eclipse observation as a complement to the now-mature space-based solar observatory fleet. If the 2026 solar-maximum data are as scientifically valuable as eclipse solar physicists expect, they will anchor the case for continued aircraft-based eclipse campaigns alongside next-generation space coronagraph missions planned for the 2030s.
