The Anchor · MAL Blood Group — How a 1972 Anomaly Waited 50 Years for Its Molecular Explanation

In 1972, a routine blood test on a pregnant woman revealed something unusual: her red blood cells lacked a specific surface molecule that more than 99.9 percent of people carry. For fifty years, the medical community managed patients with this AnWj-negative phenotype without understanding what genetic mechanism produced it. Blood banks developed workarounds, but the underlying biology remained unexplained — one of the last significant mysteries in a blood group classification system that underpins every blood transfusion performed globally. The research team at NHS Blood and Transplant and the University of Bristol that finally solved it identified a 6,646-base-pair deletion in the MAL gene as the cause: the deletion removes critical sections called exons, preventing cells from producing functional Mal protein, and the absence of Mal protein produces the AnWj-negative phenotype. The practical implications for transfusion medicine are direct. Patients with the AnWj-negative phenotype can react dangerously to blood products that contain the AnWj antigen — which means almost any donor blood — and managing these patients has required identifying the rare AnWj-negative blood donors who can provide compatible blood through phenotyping rather than genetic screening. Knowing the MAL gene deletion is the causal mechanism allows for genetic screening of AnWj-negative patients and potential blood donors: instead of phenotyping thousands of donors to find the rare compatible match, genetic testing can identify the MAL deletion carriers directly. The MAL blood group system is now the newest recognized blood group in transfusion medicine, adding to the forty-plus systems that the International Society of Blood Transfusion recognizes. Each recognized system represents a molecular mechanism understood well enough to be screened for systematically. MAL is now in that category. The reading and whole grains findings arrive on the same day as a useful reminder that not all meaningful health science requires a molecular mystery or a pharmaceutical intervention. Reading benefits memory and executive function in older adults — a finding that is accessible, free, and enjoyable. The whole grains review — 87 clinical trials, the most comprehensive nutritional cardiology meta-analysis of its type — quantifies a benefit that dietary guidelines have recommended for decades but that has lacked a specific serving-count evidence base. Four to six servings per day is the number that the trial data supports; that specificity matters for clinical guidance and for patient communication. In a week when aging medicine was discussing brain proteins and metabolic drugs, the data also supports two interventions with no side effects and no cost barriers.

Researchers at NHS Blood and Transplant and the University of Bristol identified the genetic cause behind the rare AnWj blood group antigen — a 6,646-base-pair deletion in the MAL gene that removes the exons needed to produce functional Mal protein, explaining why AnWj-negative individuals lack a surface molecule that more than 99.9 percent of the population carries — solving a mystery that began when a pregnant woman’s blood showed an unexplained anomaly in 1972 and establishing the new MAL blood group system. The discovery, published in the journal Blood, adds MAL to the list of recognized blood group systems and enables genetic screening for the AnWj-negative phenotype rather than the more laborious phenotyping previously required.

The practical transformation from this discovery is specific: blood banks have managed AnWj-negative patients for 50 years by phenotyping potential donors to find the rare individuals with compatible blood, a labor-intensive process that depends on having enough AnWj-negative donors identified in advance of need. Knowing the MAL gene deletion is the cause replaces phenotyping with genetic testing — faster, cheaper, and capable of being integrated into standard blood donor screening. The research also opens the question of why Mal protein absence produces the AnWj-negative phenotype: understanding the Mal protein’s normal function and its role in the red blood cell membrane could yield insights relevant beyond transfusion medicine, including in areas of cellular biology where MAL gene expression has been studied in other contexts.

A new study found that regular reading benefits memory and executive function in older adults and may slow the pace of brain aging — adding outcome data to one of the most accessible and cost-free cognitive health interventions available, with the finding that cognitive engagement through reading is associated with maintained memory performance and executive function in older adults beyond what would be expected from their demographic profile alone. The research adds to a growing body of evidence that cognitively stimulating activities — reading, puzzles, learning new skills — build cognitive reserve that slows age-related decline.

The practical significance of the reading finding is precisely its accessibility: in a period when aging medicine is focused on molecular targets, protein therapies, and pharmaceutical interventions, a study confirming that reading has measurable brain health benefits provides a zero-cost, zero-side-effect, universally available recommendation that clinicians can make immediately without waiting for drug approvals or therapeutic development timelines. The cognitive reserve hypothesis — that a more cognitively engaged life builds neural resilience that slows age-related decline — has been supported by decades of observational research; studies like this that find specific activity benefits in controlled or longitudinal designs add specificity to that hypothesis. Reading 30 minutes or more per day is associated with the measured benefits; that is a specific and actionable target for patients and public health messaging.

87-Trial Review: 4–6 Daily Whole-Grain Servings Cut Heart Disease Risk · Largest Nutritional Cardiology Meta-Analysis of Its Type · Quantifies Dietary Guidance Into Evidence-Based Serving Count Whole Grains Whole grains (Sep 19 2026): 4–6 servings/day → cardiovascular benefit: meaningful → 87 clinical trials → large meta-review → most comprehensive nutritional cardiology review: type → specific serving count: 4–6/day → heart disease risk: reduced → evidence base: quantified → dietary guidance: now specific → clinical: actionable → no cost barrier → accessible: widely available → mechanism: fiber + phytonutrients + glycemic index

A large review of 87 clinical trials found that eating 4 to 6 servings of whole grains daily delivers meaningful cardiovascular benefits — reducing heart disease risk in the most comprehensive nutritional cardiology meta-analysis of its type, and converting what has been broad dietary guidance into a specific, evidence-based serving count that can be communicated to patients and incorporated into clinical recommendations. The review is notable both for its scale — 87 trials representing thousands of participants — and for the specificity of its finding: not simply that whole grains are good for heart health, but that 4 to 6 servings per day is the range at which meaningful cardiovascular benefit is measured.

The specific serving-count finding is what makes this review clinically actionable in a way that previous whole-grain guidance has not been. Dietary guidelines have recommended whole grains for decades on the basis of epidemiological association; 87 clinical trials providing a specific dose-response relationship converts that recommendation from qualitative to quantitative guidance. Four to six servings per day — achievable through a combination of whole-grain bread, oatmeal, brown rice, and similar foods — is a target patients can track and clinicians can recommend with a specific evidence base behind it. The cardiovascular benefit pathway likely runs through multiple mechanisms: whole-grain fiber reduces LDL cholesterol, phytonutrients provide anti-inflammatory effects, and the lower glycemic index of whole versus refined grains reduces insulin demand and metabolic stress. The meta-review finds the combined effect meaningful at 4 to 6 daily servings.
