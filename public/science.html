The Anchor · Roman Is in Space — The Most Ambitious Cosmological Survey in History Has Begun

At 7:26 a.m. EDT Sunday, a SpaceX Falcon Heavy carrying NASA's Roman Space Telescope lifted off from Kennedy Space Center. At 7:57 a.m., Roman separated from the Falcon Heavy, solar panels began deploying, and the spacecraft began its six-month transit to the Sun-Earth L2 point 1 million miles from Earth. Roman is now in space. The 20-year development program that began with a proposal in 2004 is complete. The science mission that will attempt to answer whether dark energy is a static cosmological constant or a dynamical field has begun. Data from Roman's wide-field infrared surveys — measuring the shapes of billions of galaxies distorted by dark matter and dark energy between those galaxies and us — will begin arriving within months. The first precision measurement of dark energy's equation of state will follow within years. The scientific stakes are specific and immense. Since 1998, when supernova distance measurements established that the universe's expansion is accelerating, cosmologists have known that dark energy exists. They have not known what it is. General relativity predicts a static cosmological constant — a fixed repulsive energy density built into the fabric of spacetime — that is consistent with the expansion data collected so far. But the precision of that data has been insufficient to rule out a dynamical dark energy field, a form of energy that changes over time and would require physics beyond general relativity. Roman's weak gravitational lensing surveys will measure the dark energy effect with sufficient precision to distinguish between those two models. If Roman confirms the cosmological constant, it closes a 28-year-old question. If Roman finds deviation from the cosmological constant, it opens an era of new theoretical physics. Either answer is transformative. The Shingrix dementia story, running in parallel, is the week's second most scientifically significant development: multiple research groups have now formally called for randomized controlled trials to confirm the 24% dementia risk reduction from a vaccine already recommended for the relevant population. Formal advocacy for RCTs — not just scientific interest in them — represents the scientific community's judgment that the evidence is strong enough to justify the investment. The trials will happen. Their results will be the most watched public health finding of the late 2020s.

NASA's Roman Space Telescope launched at 7:26 a.m. EDT Sunday aboard a SpaceX Falcon Heavy from Launch Complex 39A at Kennedy Space Center. Roman separated from the Falcon Heavy at 7:57 a.m., 31 minutes after launch, with solar panels beginning to deploy on schedule. A post-launch press conference is underway. Roman is now in space and beginning a six-month journey to the Sun-Earth L2 point 1 million miles from Earth — where it will spend five years conducting the most ambitious cosmological survey in history. Its 2.96-billion-pixel Wide Field Instrument covers a sky area 100 times wider than Hubble, enabling surveys of billions of galaxies and providing the first data precise enough to measure dark energy's equation of state.

The dark energy measurement is Roman's defining mission. Since 1998, when supernova observations established the universe's accelerating expansion, cosmologists have known dark energy exists but not what it is. Roman's weak gravitational lensing surveys — measuring the distortion of billions of galaxy shapes by the combined effect of dark matter and dark energy — will be the first dataset precise enough to distinguish between a static cosmological constant (consistent with general relativity) and a dynamical dark energy field (which would require new physics). The Coronagraph Instrument will separately attempt the first space-based direct imaging of giant exoplanets orbiting nearby stars. Science data begins arriving within months of Sunday's launch.

Multiple research groups have now formally called for randomized controlled trials to confirm the Oxford-led finding that Shingrix shingles vaccine recipients were 24% less likely to be diagnosed with dementia over four years in a study of more than 500,000 people — an escalation from scientific interest to formal advocacy that signals the evidence has crossed the threshold the scientific community uses to justify RCT investment. The shift from "this finding warrants further investigation" to "randomized trials are a public health imperative" reflects the study's unusual combination of large sample size (500,000+ subjects), long follow-up (4 years), and large effect size (24% reduction). Roughly one dementia case prevented per 17 vaccinated is the estimated magnitude if RCTs confirm the finding.

Dementia has no approved pharmacological prevention. The amyloid-clearing drugs approved in recent years are treatments with modest conversion-prevention effects, not preventions. A 24% reduction from a vaccine already recommended for adults over 50 — with an established safety profile — would be immediately deployable without the decade of development new pharmaceuticals require. The biologically plausible mechanism: Shingrix prevents herpes zoster reactivation, reducing the neuroinflammatory insults that research links to amyloid and tau pathology. The formal RCT calls from multiple groups confirm that the randomized trials will happen; their results, expected earliest in 2027-2028, will be among the most watched public health findings of the decade.

Vanilla E-Cigarette Flavoring (Vanillin) Disrupts Earliest Steps of Human Development in Lab Experiments — Published August 28 · Vanilla: Most Popular E-Cig Flavor Category · Safety Evidence Extends From Lung Toxicology to Developmental Biology · FDA Restrictions Ongoing Vanillin Study (Aug 28): vanillin (vanilla flavoring) → common e-cigarette additive → lab cell experiments → finding: disrupts earliest steps of human development → significance: (1) extends safety evidence from lung disease to developmental biology; (2) vanilla = most popular e-cig flavor; (3) younger adults + adolescents most affected → prior flavoring evidence: diacetyl (popcorn lung), cinnamaldehyde (lung cell toxicity) → vanillin adds developmental biology concern → design: laboratory / cell (not clinical/epidemiological) → certainty: preliminary → FDA flavored e-cig restrictions: ongoing → vanillin: safe for oral consumption (food), never tested for inhalation at e-cig aerosol temperatures + concentrations

Laboratory experiments published Friday found that vanillin — the vanilla flavoring chemical in popular e-cigarette products — interferes with some of the earliest steps of human development in cell experiments, extending the e-cigarette safety evidence base from lung toxicology and nicotine harms to developmental biology. Vanilla is the most popular flavor category in the e-cigarette market and is disproportionately used by younger adults and adolescents — the population most developmentally vulnerable during the windows where vanillin may exert its effects. The FDA has been pursuing restrictions on flavored e-cigarette products; the vanillin finding adds to the regulatory evidence base for those restrictions specifically for vanilla-flavored products.

The e-cigarette flavoring safety literature has expanded from early diacetyl findings (buttered popcorn flavor leading to bronchiolitis obliterans) to a broader assessment of individual flavoring chemicals safe for food use but never evaluated for inhalation safety. Vanillin is safe to eat; it is present in natural vanilla and is a common food additive at oral concentrations. It was not designed to be inhaled at the temperatures and aerosol concentrations present in e-cigarettes. As a laboratory cell experiment, the developmental finding requires clinical and epidemiological follow-up before it independently drives regulatory action — but it adds to a pattern of flavoring-specific safety concerns that the FDA's enforcement trajectory already reflects.
