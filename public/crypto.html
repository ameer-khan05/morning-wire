Quick hits

  • The specific mechanism by which August CPI moves September rate-hike probability deserves a precise map for readers tracking crypto's macro dependence. September rate-hike probability is derived from fed funds futures prices — contracts that pay based on what the federal funds rate is at a given FOMC meeting. When traders price a higher probability of a rate hike, they sell September fed funds futures (pushing the price down and the implied rate up). When they price a lower probability, they buy those contracts. The probability number published in CNBC and Bloomberg headlines is an inference from those contract prices, not a survey. When CPI comes in below expectations, traders revise their probability estimate for a September hike downward (the Fed has less reason to hike), buy fed funds futures, push prices up, and the implied probability falls — which is the mechanism that would move 42% to something lower after a benign CPI print. The transmission to BTC: as hike probability falls, the dollar-weakening mechanism (lower future US rates = less demand for dollar-denominated assets = weaker dollar) runs again, the same channel that drove gold and BTC higher on Friday's jobs data. It is not a different mechanism — it is the same mechanism triggered again by a second dovish data point in the same week.
  • The CLARITY Act's three unresolved issues each have different resolution paths that are worth tracking as the recess negotiations proceed. Stablecoin yield rights: the banking lobby's objection is that interest-bearing stablecoins compete with bank deposits; resolution requires either removing yield from the bill (politically difficult — it's a key feature for crypto advocates) or convincing the banking lobby that the stablecoin market serves non-competing use cases (commercially unconvincing given the deposit-replacement potential at scale). The most likely resolution is a tiered yield framework — stablecoins held in regulated accounts can earn yield, stablecoins not held in regulated accounts cannot — that satisfies the banking lobby by keeping yield-bearing stablecoins in the regulated deposit ecosystem. Ethics rules: the Trump WLFI conflict requires language that applies ethics constraints broadly enough to appear non-partisan without specifically restricting Trump's holdings — a drafting challenge. Law enforcement data access: crypto-privacy advocates want a warrant requirement; law enforcement wants direct subpoena access; the most likely resolution is a tiered standard (warrant for content, subpoena for metadata) similar to existing phone record precedent. All three are politically resolvable; the question is whether the August recess produces the political will to close them.
