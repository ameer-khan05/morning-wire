The Anchor · Bitcoin’s $77K Recovery — Reading the Post-FOMC Crypto Momentum

Three consecutive days of post-FOMC gains for Bitcoin — from Wednesday’s intraday low near $75,972 to Friday’s $77,410 — tell a specific story about how crypto markets are interpreting the rate environment. The CLARITY Act’s Tuesday Senate block sent BTC down nearly 3%; the Fed’s Wednesday rate hike, which was 92% priced, barely moved it; and the recovery since then has been driven by post-FOMC macro relief and the DeFi market’s reaction to Ethereum’s Glamsterdam upgrade prospects. The 6.3% DeFi surge — with Starknet and Arbitrum up 17% each — is the market re-pricing the throughput and cost implications of the Glamsterdam Hard Fork before it activates. Glamsterdam introduces parallel transaction execution and enshrined proposer-builder separation, two changes that together could materially increase Ethereum’s practical transaction capacity and reduce the MEV-related inefficiencies that have made high-frequency DeFi expensive and extractable. L2 networks built on Ethereum — Starknet, Arbitrum, Optimism — benefit from increased base-layer throughput in ways that directly increase their own capacity and reduce their settlement costs. A 17% move in L2 tokens on Glamsterdam news is the market translating a technical roadmap item into a network value re-rating — one that will only convert to durable price if the upgrade delivers on its throughput targets and fee reduction. BitMEX’s September 23 shutdown is the end of a specific era in crypto history: the exchange’s perpetual swap product, launched in 2016, became the template for the derivatives market structure that now dominates crypto trading volume globally. BitMEX is shutting down; the market structure it pioneered is not. The SEC’s ETH-vs-SOL roundtable for 24/7 equity trading frames the next regulatory battleground: if equities can trade continuously on a blockchain, which chain wins that infrastructure competition determines a real-money institutional use case with durable demand for block space. Ethereum has settlement security and developer ecosystem depth; Solana has throughput and latency advantages. The SEC’s framing of this as a competitive roundtable rather than a standard rulemaking comment period is unusual — and suggests the commission is genuinely undecided.

BitMEX announced Friday it will shut down permanently on September 23, closing one of the most historically significant exchanges in crypto’s derivatives era — the platform that launched the perpetual swap contract in 2016, invented the funding rate mechanism, and established the leverage and liquidation architecture that became the template for virtually every crypto derivatives exchange that followed. Founded in 2014 by Arthur Hayes, Ben Delo, and Samuel Reed, BitMEX at its peak processed billions of dollars in daily volume and was the primary price-discovery venue for Bitcoin during the 2017 and 2020 cycles.

BitMEX’s closure is the end of the exchange, not the end of the market structure it built. Perpetual swaps, funding rates, and cross-margin liquidations are now standard products across Binance, Deribit, Bybit, and CME. The exchange’s decline began with the CFTC’s 2020 charges against its founders for operating an unregistered derivatives exchange — a case that eventually resulted in settlements and permanently damaged BitMEX’s regulatory standing in the US market. Its September 23 shutdown is the formal end of a chapter; the market structure it pioneered is the legacy that will outlast it.

The SEC held a roundtable Friday pitting Ethereum against Solana to determine which blockchain infrastructure should power 24/7 US equity trading — a competition with real regulatory and network-value stakes, as the chain that wins the infrastructure role would gain durable institutional transaction demand and significant block-space revenue. Separately, Ethereum’s upcoming Glamsterdam Hard Fork — targeting parallel transaction execution and enshrined proposer-builder separation — drove the DeFi market’s 6.3% surge as the market priced in the implied throughput gains and fee reductions that would benefit L2 networks built on Ethereum.

The SEC’s framing of this as a head-to-head roundtable — ETH vs SOL — is unusual in administrative procedure terms: standard rulemaking solicits public comment on technology options without formally staging them as competitors. The competitive framing suggests the commission sees genuine technical tradeoffs to evaluate rather than a settled technology choice. Ethereum’s case rests on settlement security, regulatory history, and developer ecosystem depth; Solana’s rests on throughput (65,000 transactions per second vs Ethereum’s current ~30 TPS) and sub-second finality. Glamsterdam’s parallel execution, if it delivers on its throughput targets, would narrow Solana’s throughput advantage substantially before the 24/7 equities decision is finalized.
