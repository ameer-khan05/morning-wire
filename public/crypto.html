The Anchor · The MSCI Strategy Removal — What $2.8 Billion in Forced Selling at November 30 Means for MSTR and Whether It’s a Bitcoin Event

MSCI’s removal of Strategy from its global equity indexes is the first formal institutional consequence for the Bitcoin treasury model — and what happens to Bitcoin at the November 30 rebalance will answer the question the crypto market has been unable to answer since MSCI announced the removal: is Strategy’s MSTR a Bitcoin proxy or a distinct equity instrument? MSCI confirmed the removal citing a new “non-operating company” rule — companies whose value derives primarily from holding assets rather than operating a business cannot remain in the index regardless of market capitalization. Strategy is the clearest case under that rule: its entire business model is to hold Bitcoin. The rule does not judge the model as imprudent; it judges it as categorically different from the operating companies an equity index is designed to track. The $2.8 billion estimate comes from JPMorgan’s analysis of MSCI index fund holdings in MSTR. Every fund tracking any MSCI index — from the MSCI USA to the MSCI World — must sell its MSTR position by the November 30 close. That is non-discretionary forced selling on a fixed date, regardless of MSTR’s price, Bitcoin’s price, or any other market condition. The question is what this $2.8 billion in MSTR selling does to Bitcoin. Strategy’s Bitcoin reserves are not being sold — MSCI is forcing the exit of equity positions, not the underlying asset. Bitcoin held $65,017 while MSTR fell. The market is currently treating the two as separate: MSTR is an equity wrapper being ejected from an index, and Bitcoin is the asset inside the wrapper, unaffected. Whether that separation holds through November 30’s forced sale — and whether a $2.8 billion MSTR equity exit creates any downstream pressure on Bitcoin through sentiment, leverage, or correlated risk-off flows — is the question that closes this month.

MSCI confirmed its removal of Strategy (MSTR) from its global equity indexes with changes taking effect after the November 30 rebalance — the first formal institutional consequence for the Bitcoin treasury model, with JPMorgan estimating approximately $2.8 billion in forced MSTR selling by funds tracking MSCI indexes — citing its new non-operating company rule that excludes from indexes any company whose value derives primarily from holding assets rather than operating a business; KuCoin noted the removal also puts Metaplanet in MSCI’s crosshairs under the same rule; MSTR shares fell sharply in after-hours trading following the confirmation while Bitcoin itself held $65,017, TradingKey noted MSTR faces the risk of sliding toward $105 from above $120 post-market in the forced-sale window; the Bitcoin reserves held by Strategy — more than 400,000 BTC as of the last disclosure — are unaffected by the index removal, as MSCI is forcing the exit of equity positions, not the underlying asset.

The MSCI removal is the most consequential structural event for the Bitcoin treasury model since it was invented. The question it poses is not whether Strategy’s Bitcoin holding was a good strategy — it poses the question of whether the equity wrapper structure for Bitcoin exposure is durable when institutional index inclusion criteria exclude non-operating companies by definition. If the wrapper is excluded from the indexes that institutional capital tracks, the premium over NAV that allowed Strategy to raise capital at above-Bitcoin prices to buy more Bitcoin is under structural pressure.

The Senate Agriculture Committee held hearings Tuesday on Trump’s CFTC nominees — Nathan Anonick and Matthew Rowland — alongside the SEC nominees Kristin Johnson and Steven Levine; CoinDesk noted the nominations are Trump’s first picks for either agency in approximately a year and his first Democratic nominees, filling empty commission seats at both regulators that have been driving crypto policy through the collapse of the Clarity Act in the absence of a quorum; Cult of Money noted the bipartisan slate — two Republicans, two Democrats — reflects an administration calculation that crypto regulatory clarity requires the kind of bipartisan commission majority that can produce rules with durable legal standing, rather than enforcement actions that the next administration can reverse.

The nominees’ confirmation timeline matters for the crypto market more than the individuals’ stated positions. The Clarity Act’s framework for distinguishing securities from commodities in the crypto context remains unresolved, and both the SEC and CFTC have been operating with reduced quorums that have limited their ability to issue binding rules. Full commissions at both agencies before year-end would be the first time since 2024 that both regulators have the institutional capacity to advance the regulatory certainty the industry has been seeking.
