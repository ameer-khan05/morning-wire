The Anchor · Glamsterdam Off the Calendar — Ethereum Faces Its First Indefinitely Paused Major Upgrade Since the Merge

Glamsterdam’s cancellation is structurally different from the delays the upgrade has already experienced. A delay moves a date; a cancellation means the protocol-level problem that caused it must be redesigned and re-validated before any new date can be proposed. Chainsecurity’s identification of 13 deployed contracts with vulnerable behavior is the technical reason for the cancellation, but the underlying issue is a governance and design problem: the proposed EIP gas changes had not been fully validated against the existing deployed contract state before the testnet activation was scheduled. The 13 contracts are on mainnet. Their behavior depends on the current gas semantics. A change that makes their currently-reverting errors permanent would create a class of permanently broken deployed contracts — an outcome that, once on mainnet, cannot be rolled back. The development team’s decision to cancel rather than delay reflects the correct risk calculus: an upgrade that breaks 13 deployed contracts is worse than no upgrade on any timeline. The open question is how long the redesign and re-validation process takes. Unlike a fixed delay, there is no announced date to work from. Glamsterdam entering an indefinite pause while ETH trades at $2,359 — down 9.3% this week — and the Fed meets Tuesday is the worst timing configuration for the upgrade since it was first scheduled. The Deribit expiry that was seven days out as of Friday is now six; the Fed is four days out. Neither calendar event is macro-immune. Glamsterdam was the only one that was.

Ethereum developers cancelled the Glamsterdam upgrade’s Hoodi testnet activation after security firm Chainsecurity identified 13 deployed mainnet contracts whose behavior would be adversely affected by the proposed gas changes — specifically, the risk that transactions that currently revert gracefully would instead fail permanently under the new gas semantics — forcing the development team to cancel rather than delay the testnet activation and leaving Glamsterdam without a scheduled date for the first time since the upgrade was announced.

The difference between a cancellation and a delay is structural: a delay preserves a timeline and moves its date; a cancellation removes the timeline entirely pending a fix whose scope and duration are not yet defined. The 13 identified contracts were deployed under current gas semantics; any upgrade that changes how their failed transactions are handled must demonstrate it does not break those contracts before a new testnet can be proposed. That validation process, not calendar preference, now determines when Glamsterdam has a date.

Bitcoin Core Merges Fast-Disclosure Policy Vulnerabilities Easily Rediscoverable AI Prompt Minimal Effort Oct 22 / Sat Oct 24 2026 Bitcoin Core Policy Bitcoin Core (Sat Oct 24 2026): fast-disclose → policy → merged Oct 22 → vulnerabilities → easily rediscoverable → minimal effort → AI prompt → fast route → prior policy: standard timeline → AI: changes disclosure calculus → node operators: update pressure

Bitcoin Core merged a revised security disclosure policy on October 22 that adds a fast-disclosure route specifically for vulnerabilities that can be rediscovered with minimal effort — including via a simple AI prompt — departing from its prior single-timeline policy after developers determined that some bugs are now discoverable fast enough that the standard responsible-disclosure window creates meaningful exposure before node operators can update; the revision was driven by the recognition that AI tools have lowered the technical bar for independent vulnerability rediscovery to the point where a standard disclosure timeline may no longer contain risk effectively.

The policy change reflects a structural shift in the threat model for open-source security protocols: when the disclosure timeline is set, the implicit assumption is that rediscovering the vulnerability independently requires substantial expertise and time. AI-assisted vulnerability research changes that assumption for a class of bugs — specifically, those where the logical flaw is describable in natural language and a capable model can infer the exploit from the description. Fast-disclosure for AI-rediscoverable bugs is the policy response to that changed threat model.

Vitalik Buterin published EIP-8288 proposing a mechanism for recursive aggregation of STARK proofs via off-chain computation — enabling complex cryptographic operations to be verified on-chain through compressed off-chain proofs rather than requiring full on-chain execution — which would make advanced applications relying on ZK-proof cryptography meaningfully cheaper and more secure; the proposal arrives on the same weekend that Glamsterdam’s cancellation removes the near-term upgrade catalyst, positioning EIP-8288 as a long-timeline technical improvement rather than an immediate protocol event.

STARK proof aggregation is significant for the ZK-rollup and Layer 2 ecosystem that has been the most active development layer of Ethereum’s scalability roadmap: reducing the cost of on-chain verification for complex off-chain proofs directly improves the economics of ZK-rollup transactions, which currently pay on-chain verification costs that recursive aggregation would substantially compress. The proposal’s arrival during a protocol-pause weekend positions it as foundational work for the post-Glamsterdam development phase.
