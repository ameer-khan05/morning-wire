EU AI Act GPAI enforcement activates in three days.

The Meta-Microsoft divergence answers the AI capex-FCF question with a precision the Alphabet comparison didn't quite deliver. Microsoft's Azure crossed $100 billion in annual revenue at a 41% growth rate: AI infrastructure investment generating commercial revenue at scale, today, not on a 2027 horizon. Meta's 91% FCF collapse at $31.1 billion in quarterly capex demonstrates the opposite condition: spending is scaling faster than the AI advertising yield that would justify it. The divergence is not a coincidence of timing — both companies are deploying AI into their core products. It is a function of business model: Microsoft's cloud product directly charges enterprise customers for AI compute; Meta's advertising product monetizes AI indirectly through user engagement lift, which takes longer to convert to cash flow.

The Meta-Microsoft split is the most important AI sector data point delivered this earnings cycle. It disproves the simplest version of the "all AI capex will crush FCF across the sector" thesis that Alphabet established — Microsoft's Azure contradicts it directly. But it confirms the more specific concern: companies whose AI monetization path is indirect (advertising yield, consumer engagement lift) face a longer FCF recovery period than companies whose AI product directly charges enterprise customers for compute. Meta's advertising-FCF engine is powerful when AI enhances advertising yield faster than capex scales. Right now, capex is scaling faster. Until that ratio inverts, $30B-per-quarter CapEx without commensurate FCF is the Meta story, and the market is pricing it accordingly.

Meta's operating margin compression from 43% to 31% in a single year reflects the pace at which capex is being recognized in depreciation and operating expenses. The $31.1 billion in Q2 capex includes data center construction, GPU procurement, and networking infrastructure — all of which begin depreciating immediately and add to operating expenses before AI advertising yield improvements are fully visible in revenue. The operating-margin compression is therefore the accounting manifestation of the capex-before-revenue sequence: spend first, depreciate immediately, recognize revenue later. Microsoft's Azure avoids this compression because enterprise cloud customers pay for compute on consumption models — revenue recognition and the underlying infrastructure deployment are closer in time, meaning Azure's $100 billion in annual revenue is approximately contemporaneous with its infrastructure investment rather than preceding it by several quarters.

The Anthropic investment gain — $3.2 billion recognized in Microsoft's Q4 — adds a specific wrinkle to the Microsoft AI capex story. Microsoft has invested in Anthropic alongside its OpenAI relationship, meaning its AI infrastructure bets extend across two frontier labs. The $3.2 billion gain is a mark-to-market investment return, not operating revenue, but it demonstrates that Microsoft's AI investment strategy is generating financial returns through equity appreciation even as Azure generates operating revenue. For the sector, the implication is that the companies most exposed to AI infrastructure risk (through the equity and credit risks of funding frontier labs) are also positioned for the largest financial upside if those labs appreciate — a portfolio-diversification structure that pure cloud infrastructure companies don't replicate.

Meta AI is clearly lifting advertising revenue — 28% revenue growth demonstrates that — but the FCF bill for building the infrastructure required to run Meta AI at scale is arriving faster than the advertising yield improvements can offset it. The market's 9.64% after-hours decline reflects not a judgment that AI advertising integration has failed, but a judgment that the timeline for FCF recovery is longer than the capex guide's upward trajectory implies.

Quick hits

  • Meta's fourth consecutive capex guide increase — from $115–$135 billion at the start of the year to $130–$145 billion today — is itself a signal that the AI infrastructure deployment is accelerating rather than plateauing. Each quarterly increase reflects a decision made in the prior quarter to commit additional capital: data center construction contracts signed, GPU procurement agreements executed, land and power infrastructure commitments made. These commitments have multi-year lead times and cannot be reversed once placed — which means the $130–$145 billion guide is not a forecast, it is a schedule of committed expenditure. The FCF impact of that committed schedule will be felt through depreciation for 5-10 years after the infrastructure is deployed. Meta's bet is that the AI advertising yield improvements the infrastructure enables will generate enough incremental FCF over that decade to justify the commitment. Wednesday's 9.64% decline is the market's assessment that this is a longer wait than the current share price previously implied.
  • The Anthropic-Microsoft investment gain is worth examining in the context of what it signals about the frontier AI lab equity market. Anthropic has raised at escalating valuations through its cloud partnerships with Microsoft, Google, and Amazon — each partnership includes equity stakes alongside compute commitments. Microsoft's $3.2 billion Q4 gain on its Anthropic investment implies a mark-to-market valuation for Anthropic that is substantially higher than its last public round. For investors tracking frontier lab valuations, the Microsoft earnings disclosure is one of the few public mark-to-market datapoints for Anthropic equity — alongside Amazon's AWS partnership stake and Google's Vertex AI partnership position. The fact that Microsoft recognized a $3.2 billion gain in a single quarter suggests Anthropic's enterprise valuation has risen materially since Microsoft's initial investment commitment.
