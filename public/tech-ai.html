The Anchor · The Spending Machine — What ChatGPT Wallet Means When AI Agents Can Complete Purchases on Visa, Mastercard, and American Express

OpenAI’s ChatGPT Wallet is not a payment product. It is an agency product. The distinction matters. ChatGPT Wallet connects OpenAI’s AI agents to the payment step that those agents have previously stopped short of. Prior to ChatGPT Wallet, an AI agent could research a product, find the best price, and confirm availability. The user then clicked through to complete the purchase. ChatGPT Wallet removes that last step: the agent completes the purchase directly, using a card the user has pre-authorized. The agent does not retain payment data between transactions — the user verifies identity biometrically before each purchase. Card rewards accrue to the user’s existing card program. The payment infrastructure is existing card network rails: Visa, Mastercard, American Express, and Chase. OpenAI has not built a new payment system; it has plugged its agents into the existing payment system at the checkout step. That integration is immediately scalable — it works wherever those cards work, without merchants or payment processors needing to adopt new standards. The economic implication is not primarily about ChatGPT’s paying subscriber base. It is about the precedent. An AI agent with spending authority is a qualitatively different kind of agent than one that only recommends. It has an incentive structure, a trust relationship with the user, and a commercial relationship with sellers that prior AI assistants did not have. How OpenAI governs that relationship — which products the agent recommends, which sellers it prefers, how it handles conflicts between recommendation quality and revenue — is the competitive and regulatory question that ChatGPT Wallet has just made urgent.

OpenAI researchers published nine mathematical preprints Wednesday covering a range of theoretical computer science and mathematics problems, with The Neuron’s daily AI digest reporting the most significant claimed result is a tight 9/4 bound on the matrix-multiplication exponent — the minimum value of omega, which determines the theoretical complexity limit of large-matrix multiplication and underlies algorithms across scientific computing, graphics, and machine learning; a claimed Smale linear-programming result was also among the nine manuscripts; and the preprints mark the most concentrated burst of theoretical mathematics output from an AI lab in a single week, with OpenAI’s mathematics research team releasing results that, if validated by the community, would rank among the most significant advances in theoretical computer science in years — though peer review and independent verification will be required before the claims are considered established.

Matrix multiplication’s exponent omega is one of theoretical computer science’s longest-standing open problems. The current best published bound is approximately 2.37; a tight 9/4 (= 2.25) result would be a significant advance. “Tight” means both an upper and a lower bound — not just proof that an algorithm achieves 2.25, but proof that no algorithm can do better. If the result holds under peer review, it closes one of the field’s most pursued questions. The community response over the coming weeks will determine whether this claim survives scrutiny.
