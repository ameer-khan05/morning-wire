The Anchor · Nvidia’s Supply-Chain Breach — From CEO Scandal to Confirmed Chip Smuggling

Jensen Huang’s resignation on Thursday was a corporate governance event. Friday’s Bloomberg and SemiAnalysis reports are an intelligence failure. Bloomberg reported Friday that banned Nvidia H800 AI chips — the specific GPU architecture that the Commerce Department restricted from export to China in October 2023 — are physically installed at a Shanghai data center with direct ties to Alice Huang, the person US intelligence considers an alleged Chinese spy and Jensen Huang’s former romantic partner. The H800 chips in Shanghai are not a theoretical risk. They are hardware, in a building, operated by an entity connected to the person at the center of the espionage investigation. SemiAnalysis, the semiconductor analysis firm, separately published analysis claiming to have tracked 1.5 gigawatts of US-restricted AI accelerators — a staggering quantity — flowing into China through Megaspeed International, a Singapore-based company that Alice Huang founded. At $7.5 billion in estimated chip value, the alleged smuggling operation is the largest single instance of US dual-use technology export control violation ever alleged. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, who is responsible for administering the export control regime, agreed Friday to testify before Congress on what the enforcement system knew and when. House Speaker Mike Johnson said the revelations would “strengthen and accelerate” pending chip export control legislation. The DOJ investigation, which opened Thursday, now has a physical target — the Shanghai data center — in addition to the intelligence question of what Alice Huang may have learned from Jensen Huang about Nvidia’s export strategies. The national security question has moved from “what might she have known” to “what hardware is there, and how did it get through?”

Google released Gemini 4 Hydrogen across all tiers of AI Studio, the Gemini API, the Gemini app, and Google Search on Friday — the company’s next-generation efficiency model — with a 1-million-token context window (1,048,576 tokens), $1.50 per million input tokens and $7.50 per million output tokens in standard pricing, 2.5 times faster throughput than the Gemini 3.8 Flash model it replaces, and cache read pricing of $0.075 per million tokens; Gemini 4 Hydrogen’s availability across free consumer tiers makes it the first 1-million-token context model available to users without a paid subscription, expanding access to long-context AI reasoning from enterprise and developer users to the general public; the model’s combination of 1-million-token context, 2.5x speed improvement, and aggressive $1.50/M input pricing positions it as a direct challenge to Anthropic’s Claude models and Meta’s Muse Ember — both of which also feature 1-million-token context windows — in the enterprise API market.

Gemini 4 Hydrogen’s $1.50/M input token pricing is the competitive move to watch. At that rate, it undercuts Claude and Meta Muse Ember at equivalent context length while running 2.5x faster — two of the three dimensions enterprise buyers evaluate for high-throughput AI applications. The question for developers is whether Google’s instruction-following quality and coding performance at Gemini 4 Hydrogen’s performance tier matches the speed and price advantage. Benchmark scores will determine adoption rate in the enterprise segment.
