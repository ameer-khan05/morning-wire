Tech & AI · Deep Dive · Nvidia ▼7% AI Regulation Fears · Meta Acquires Conductor · Foxconn +46% · Sentra $150M · Thursday, November 5, 2026

The AI chip selloff that dominated Thursday’s session was not an earnings story — it was a regulatory-expectations story: investors fled Nvidia (-7%), Qualcomm, Arm, and AMD on speculation that the incoming Democratic Congress will clamp down on the AI industry through data regulation, chip export controls, or cloud concentration legislation, dragging the Nasdaq 100 down as much as 5% in the sharpest post-election reversal in its recent history; Tesla and SpaceX each lost over 8%; South Korea was hit hardest, with SK Hynix losing 9% and Samsung Electronics 6.7%; separately, Meta confirmed the acquisition of AI coding startup Conductor, whose software allows developers to assign separate pieces of a coding project to multiple AI agents working in parallel, with Conductor’s cloud service set to shut down November 20; Foxconn reported October revenue of NT$1.31 trillion ($39.7 billion), up 46% year-over-year on AI-server demand; and cybersecurity startup Sentra raised $150 million to protect enterprise data from autonomous AI agents.

Thursday’s tech beat is the market re-reading Wednesday’s election result through a different lens: the Democratic sweep that reduced geopolitical risk on Wednesday is now raising regulatory risk on Thursday, specifically for the AI chip sector that the Nasdaq’s weighting is built around. The selloff is not driven by any concrete legislation or committee action — it is driven by uncertainty about what a Democratic majority will choose to do.

The Anchor · AI Chip Selloff — The Election That Helped on Wednesday Hurts on Thursday

Nvidia fell 7%. Not because of an earnings miss. Not because any Democratic lawmaker introduced a bill. Nvidia fell 7% because the market decided a Democratic Congress is a risk to the AI industry — and that decision, once made simultaneously by enough institutional investors, became self-fulfilling in a single session. No legislation exists, no committee has acted, and the new Congress does not convene until January. But markets price risk premiums on probability, not on actual bills — and the probability distribution of AI regulatory outcomes shifted when Democrats won the Senate. The selloff’s selectivity is the key data point: it hit AI-chip and AI-adjacent stocks like Tesla and SpaceX most severely while leaving non-AI tech, healthcare, and consumer names largely untouched. SK Hynix at -9% is the memory supply chain expressing the same concern, reaching from chip design through training and inference to the entire AI hardware stack.

Meta confirmed the acquisition of AI coding startup Conductor, whose software allows developers to assign separate pieces of a coding project to multiple AI agents working in parallel — enabling coordinated multi-agent development workflows that single-agent coding tools do not support; TechCrunch confirmed that Conductor’s cloud service will shut down on November 20, with users directed to migrate their workflows before that date; the acquisition adds multi-agent coordination infrastructure to Meta’s AI coding toolchain, complementing the company’s existing AI development investments and positioning Meta to compete more directly with OpenAI’s Codex and GitHub Copilot in the enterprise coding-agent market.

Conductor’s multi-agent coordination capability addresses the same enterprise gap OpenAI’s Pierre acquisition targeted: running multi-component coding projects across AI agents without losing context. Meta acquires the coordination layer; OpenAI acquires the persistent storage layer.

Foxconn reported October revenue of NT$1.31 trillion ($39.7 billion), up 46% year-over-year — its strongest October on record — driven by surging demand for AI servers as hyperscalers including Microsoft, Google, and Amazon continue accelerating their data-center buildouts; the result arrives on a session where AI chip stocks are selling off on Democratic regulation fears, providing a supply-chain data point that cuts against the bearish narrative: Foxconn’s assembly lines are running at record levels because AI server orders placed months ago are being fulfilled, regardless of what Congress may do in January 2027; the 46% growth rate is the hardware-layer confirmation that enterprise AI infrastructure spending — which Menlo Ventures quantified at $177 billion last week — has not peaked at the manufacturing level.

Foxconn’s October number and Thursday’s chip selloff point in opposite directions: the selloff is a forward-looking regulatory risk premium; Foxconn’s 46% revenue growth is a backward-looking demand confirmation. Both can be simultaneously true, and the gap between them is what Thursday’s market is pricing.
