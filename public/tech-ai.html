The Anchor · The Software Bet — What Amazon’s $7.5 Billion Acquisition of Decart Says About the AI Chip Race When Nvidia Falls 9% the Same Day

Amazon paid $7.5 billion for a chip-optimization startup on the same day Nvidia fell 9%. That is not coincidence — it is a thesis. Amazon’s acquisition of Decart is the clearest statement yet that the AI chip race will be decided partly at the software layer. Decart’s optimization stack improves AI inference performance on non-Nvidia hardware — specifically on Amazon’s own Trainium and Inferentia chips, which power AWS AI services. Amazon is paying $7.5 billion for the belief that software-defined performance on its own silicon can narrow the gap with Nvidia without waiting for the next hardware generation. The timing — the same day Nvidia fell 9% in a partial retracement of Monday’s governance-driven rally — makes the deal look prescient or contrarian depending on how Tuesday resolves. But the deal’s strategic logic is not about Tuesday’s stock price. It is about the structural position AWS wants to hold in AI infrastructure over the next five years. Nvidia’s dominance in AI chips is a function of CUDA, the software ecosystem that makes Nvidia GPUs the default for AI developers. Amazon cannot match CUDA by buying silicon. It can try to match it by buying optimization software that makes its own chips fast enough to compete. That is what Decart does. That is what $7.5 billion is paying for. Whether it works is a question for the next three years of AWS AI performance benchmarks, not for Tuesday’s stock move.

Amazon announced an agreement to acquire Israeli AI startup Decart for $7.5 billion, integrating Decart’s AI compute optimization software into Amazon’s Annapurna Labs chip division — the unit responsible for Trainium training and Inferentia inference processors used throughout AWS — with TechCrunch describing the deal as “a bet on using software to narrow the gap with Nvidia in the AI chip race” in its headline; Decart was founded in 2023 by Dean Leitersdorf, Orian Leitersdorf, and Moshe Shalev, who own approximately 64% of the company and have agreed to remain at Amazon for five years, with the deal tied to performance targets; JNS reported that Decart had previously been in acquisition talks with Anthropic — discussions that collapsed in September at a $6 billion valuation — before agreeing to the Amazon deal at $7.5 billion; and 247 Wall St. described the acquisition as Amazon paying for Decart’s thesis that AI inference performance on non-Nvidia hardware can be significantly improved through software-layer optimization, reducing AWS’s dependence on purchasing Nvidia GPUs at premium prices.

The Amazon–Decart deal’s strategic logic is about the CUDA moat. Nvidia’s dominance in AI chips is built on the CUDA software ecosystem that makes Nvidia GPUs the default for AI training and inference. Amazon cannot replicate CUDA by buying silicon. It can try to replicate the performance advantage by buying software that makes its own chips fast enough to compete on the workloads AWS needs to serve. Whether Decart’s stack can deliver on that thesis at scale — across the full range of Trainium and Inferentia deployments — is a three-year benchmark question, not a Tuesday earnings question.

Members of xAI unveiled a new AI startup called Epochal that lets AI agents browse an archived version of the internet as it existed on specific historical dates — a “time portal” designed to teach AI systems to simulate past events and thereby train models to predict future ones — according to Bloomberg’s Tuesday report; the architecture involves presenting AI agents with the information environment that existed at a specific historical moment, allowing the model to reason about what happens next without access to future-leaked context, a training method intended to improve forecasting performance on real-world events; and the startup’s founding by former xAI members signals that prediction‑focused AI — sometimes called “forecasting AI” or “proactive AI” — is emerging as a distinct product category alongside conversational and coding assistants.

Epochal’s archived‑internet approach addresses a fundamental problem in training AI for prediction: models trained on the internet already contain the outcomes. If a model learns from web text written after an event, it already “knows” what happened and cannot simulate the genuine uncertainty of the moment before. Epochal’s time portal removes future context, forcing the model to reason from incomplete information the way a human analyst would. Whether that produces materially better forecasts than current reasoning models is an empirical question. The startup’s founding pedigree suggests it has evidence that it does.

OpenAI released GPT‑6.2 Sol to ChatGPT’s main chat interface for all paying subscribers Tuesday, making the model accessible to the full base of ChatGPT Plus, Team, and Enterprise customers without additional configuration, according to The Neuron’s daily AI digest; Anthropic separately launched Claude Ship, a set of AI agents that handle the workflow that follows code generation — reviewing pull requests, approving changes, and managing deployment pipeline steps — moving Claude’s role in software development from writing code to owning the post-writing process; and both releases advance the same underlying trend: AI tools are expanding from discrete generation tasks into end-to-end process ownership, with ChatGPT upgrading its reasoning model and Claude extending its agency deeper into the software development lifecycle.

Claude Ship’s positioning is a meaningful strategic move for Anthropic: code generation is a crowded market with GitHub Copilot, Cursor, and multiple competitors. Code review and approval — the process that determines whether generated code makes it into production — is less crowded, more trust-intensive, and more valuable per workflow step. By launching Ship, Anthropic is betting that the merge decision is worth more than the write decision in enterprise software development. That bet has implications for how enterprises evaluate AI coding tools: not just on generation quality, but on whether the full post-generation workflow can be delegated.

Tidal announced it will delete more than 29 million fully AI-generated music tracks from its platform by December 9, removing tracks defined as content produced by generative AI without meaningful human creative input — a policy The Neuron described as the most decisive platform-level action against AI-generated music content taken by any major streaming service; the deletion affects tracks that Tidal identifies as fully automated generative output, as distinct from music that uses AI tools in production but retains human compositional authorship; and the scale of the removal — 29 million tracks — indicates how thoroughly AI-generated content has penetrated streaming catalogs since generative music tools became widely available in 2024, with Tidal’s action setting a precedent that other streaming platforms will face pressure to match or explicitly reject.

Tidal’s deletion of 29 million tracks is the first major streaming service to draw a hard line between human‑made and machine‑made music at catalog scale. Spotify, Apple Music, and Amazon Music have flagged the issue but not committed to mass removal. Tidal’s action forces the question: is the standard for inclusion in a commercial music catalog that the content was produced by a human, or only that it meets minimum quality thresholds? Tidal’s answer is the former. The platform’s market share will determine how much that answer matters to the industry.
