Intel fell 29% on Friday — its worst single-session decline since 1974 — after Q3 results that beat on revenue ($16.5 billion, +21% year-over-year) and showed data center and AI revenue surging 78% to $7.3 billion were immediately overshadowed by Q4 guidance of $15.0–$16.0 billion well below analyst expectations and a disclosure that a manufacturing accident at its Kiryat Gat fab in Israel had disrupted production; Amazon’s simultaneous Thursday-night earnings miss compounded the damage, with EPS of $1.89 missing the $1.97 estimate and the company disclosing a twelve-month cash burn exceeding $42 billion from AI infrastructure investment, even as AWS reported strong 39% revenue growth to $46 billion with operating income of $28.1 billion; Mistral released the open weights for Large 4, its 1.05 trillion-parameter multimodal mixture-of-experts model trained on 3,800 NVIDIA Grace Blackwell GPUs in European data centers, under a modified MIT license allowing self-hosting while requiring a commercial license for operating it as a service — making it the largest freely downloadable multimodal AI available; and OpenAI published an updated Model Spec reorganizing its public behavioral guidance around five core values — faithfulness to human intent, harm minimization, honesty and transparency, human agency, and care — establishing a new instruction hierarchy and retiring most detailed style guidance.

Friday’s tech beat closes October’s earnings season with Intel’s implosion completing a week in which Alphabet, Amazon, and Intel all disappointed on forward guidance or cash flow while Microsoft and Mistral moved in the opposite direction — AI’s winners and losers diverging at an accelerating pace.

The Anchor · Intel’s 29% Drop — When AI Data Center Revenue Grows 78% and the Stock Still Crashes

Intel’s data center revenue grew 78% in Q3. Its stock fell 29% anyway. The reason is that guidance — the number the market pays for — told a completely different story. Q3’s 78% data center growth was the product of a strong backlog. Q4’s $15–$16 billion guidance range tells you that backlog has been worked down, the new PC cycle is not arriving, and a manufacturing accident in Kiryat Gat removed one of Intel’s fabrication options for the critical quarter. The stock fell 29% not because Q3 was bad but because Q4’s forecast range implies that the data center momentum does not carry forward at the pace investors had priced. Intel’s 29% single-session fall is not about AI spending slowing — AWS grew 39%, and Microsoft’s Azure grew 48% last week. It is about Intel’s specific production situation: fab accident, PC inventory, and a guidance range that left no room for optimism. The Mistral Large 4 release on the same morning sharpens the point: the open-weight frontier is now at 1 trillion parameters, and getting there required European infrastructure, not Intel’s fabs.

Intel reported Q3 revenue of $16.5 billion (+21% year-over-year) with data center and AI revenue surging 78% to $7.3 billion, a genuine beat — then issued Q4 guidance of $15.0–$16.0 billion with non-GAAP EPS of just $0.25, well below analyst expectations, and disclosed that a manufacturing accident at its Kiryat Gat fab in Israel had disrupted production capacity at a critical moment; the stock fell 29% in Friday’s session, its worst single-day performance since 1974, sending Nasdaq futures lower overnight and compounding the AI-earnings reckoning begun by Alphabet’s Thursday miss, as investors confronted the gap between Intel’s data center momentum and its fab execution risk.

Data center growing 78% answers the AI demand question — enterprise spending on Intel’s datacenter products is real. The Q4 guide answers a different question: whether Intel’s production capacity can meet that demand. Kiryat Gat’s accident removes one answer. The PC segment’s 5% decline removes another offset.

Amazon reported Q3 EPS of $1.89, missing the $1.97 analyst estimate, with revenue of $201.9 billion (+12%) also falling short — despite AWS growing 39% to $46.0 billion with operating income of $28.1 billion, up sharply from $17.4 billion a year earlier; the more consequential figure was the disclosure of a twelve-month cash burn exceeding $42 billion from AI infrastructure investment, roughly double its previous record set during the 2022 expansion cycle, as Amazon accelerates data center and custom-chip buildout faster than its AWS operating margin expansion can fund; the stock fell approximately 1% in Friday’s session, a modest decline relative to Intel’s but contributing to the week’s narrative of AI infrastructure costs outpacing near-term revenue returns.

AWS operating income of $28.1B is real and growing rapidly — up 62% year-over-year. The $42B cash burn is also real, representing a bet that the AWS growth rate sustains long enough to service the investment. Both can be true; Friday’s market reaction said the cash burn number is the one that leads.

Mistral Large 4 Open Weights Released 1.05 Trillion Parameters Multimodal Mixture Experts 1 Million Context Window Modified MIT License 3800 NVIDIA Grace Blackwell European Data Centers Hugging Face / Fri Oct 30 2026 Mistral Large 4 · 1T Open Mistral (Fri Oct 30 2026): Large 4 → open weights → 1.05T params → 52B active per token → 1.6B vision encoder → MoE → 1M context → multimodal → modified MIT → self-host free → service needs license → 3,800 NVIDIA Blackwell → EU data centers → Hugging Face → NVFP4 quantized already live

Mistral released the open weights of Large 4, its 1.05 trillion-parameter multimodal mixture-of-experts flagship, trained from scratch on 3,800 NVIDIA Grace Blackwell GPUs across European data centers under a modified MIT license — meaning the weights are freely downloadable for self-hosting, but operating Large 4 as a commercial service requires a separate license from Mistral; the model features 52 billion active parameters per token, a 1.6 billion parameter vision encoder, a 1 million-token context window, and is available on Hugging Face with a quantized NVFP4 checkpoint ready for immediate deployment, making it the largest open-weight multimodal model available to any organization with sufficient compute.

A trillion-parameter open-weight model changes the cost curve for organizations that can self-host: they get frontier-class inference at the margin cost of their own compute rather than a per-token API fee. The modified MIT license carves out commercial API service — so cloud resale of Large 4 requires Mistral’s involvement, but internal enterprise deployment does not.

OpenAI Model Spec Rewrite Five Core Values Faithfulness Harm Minimization Honesty Transparency Human Agency Care New Instruction Hierarchy Root System Developer User Retires Style Guidance / Fri Oct 30 2026 OpenAI: Model Spec Rewrite OpenAI (Fri Oct 30 2026): Model Spec → updated Oct 29 → five values → faithfulness → harm minimization → honesty → human agency → care → instruction hierarchy → root → system → developer → user → style guidance → retired → context guidance → expanded → oversight → agency → current models → not yet fully reflective

OpenAI published an updated Model Spec on Thursday evening, reorganizing its public description of desired model behavior around five core values — faithfulness to human intent, harm minimization, honesty and transparency, human agency, and care — establishing a new instruction hierarchy in which root-level rules from the Spec itself cannot be overridden, system messages rank next, followed by developer and then user instructions; the revision retires most detailed style guidance from the prior Spec, adds new sections on context interpretation, oversight, and AI agency, and explicitly notes that current models do not yet fully reflect the behaviors described, framing the document as a target state rather than a description of present behavior.

Retiring detailed style guidance means developers relying on the Spec as a precise behavioral contract need to update their expectations — the new document sets values and principles rather than rules. The “not yet fully reflective” caveat is the most important sentence: OpenAI is publishing an aspirational specification, not a technical sheet.
