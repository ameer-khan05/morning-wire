The Anchor · The Valuation Race — Why OpenAI Is Rushing $30 Billion Before Anthropic’s IPO Sets the Public AI Benchmark

OpenAI’s urgency to close a $30 billion round before Anthropic’s mid-November IPO is not about needing the capital — it is about controlling the valuation narrative before a public market sets a reference price. The dynamic works as follows: Anthropic’s IPO will place audited revenue, margin, and growth numbers in front of public investors for the first time at a frontier AI lab. Those numbers — whatever they are — will become the basis against which every private AI valuation is measured for the next several quarters. If Anthropic lists at $1.5 trillion on $16.8 billion in Q3 revenue, the implied price-to-revenue multiple becomes a live market benchmark. OpenAI’s $30 billion raise at $1.4 trillion, if closed before that listing, lets OpenAI set its terms against a market that does not yet have an AI-specific public multiple to anchor to. Once Anthropic is public and the multiple exists, every $1.4 trillion private valuation for OpenAI faces the same scrutiny: does OpenAI’s revenue justify its multiple relative to Anthropic’s publicly verified financials? Reuters’ report that OpenAI is pitching $50 billion in annualized revenue and projecting $70 billion by year-end is the specific narrative OpenAI needs investors to accept before that scrutiny is possible. Whether those revenue numbers are real — or whether they reflect the same projection optimism that Anthropic’s slower-than-expected Q3 growth already calls into question — is what public markets will eventually determine. OpenAI’s strategy is to raise at the valuation it wants before those markets can say otherwise. The timing pressure is not artificial: mid-November is less than four weeks away. SoftBank and MGX are the named investors still negotiating; no term sheet has been signed. The window is closing.

OpenAI is racing to lock in roughly $30 billion from SoftBank Group, Abu Dhabi’s MGX, and existing investors at a valuation above $1.4 trillion — nearly double its March closing price of $852 billion — by pitching $50 billion in annualized revenue from GPT-6 Astra and projecting $70 billion by year-end, before Anthropic’s mid-November IPO creates a public market benchmark against which every private AI valuation gets measured; no term sheet had been finalized as of Tuesday, and negotiations remain active with the window to close before Anthropic lists narrowing to roughly four weeks.

The mathematical logic of OpenAI’s urgency is straightforward: private investors are currently pricing AI labs on self-reported revenue projections without public-market scrutiny. Anthropic’s IPO changes that framework by introducing audited financials into the equation. If Anthropic lists at a lower revenue multiple than OpenAI’s current private valuation implies — or if public investors apply a discount to AI revenue quality — the $1.4 trillion valuation OpenAI is seeking becomes harder to defend to its own investors. Closing before the listing removes that comparison from the table. Anthropic’s Q3 revenue growth slowing from stronger prior quarters is already a signal that the most optimistic AI revenue projections may face scrutiny — exactly what OpenAI is racing to price around.

Anthropic is moving toward a mid-November IPO that could value it near $1.5 trillion, having announced a Public Benefit Fund that will initially hold 5% of the company’s shares — structured as a wholly owned LLC to support nonprofit donations and direct distributions, with the board able to add another 2% for every 50% increase in Anthropic’s value above a baseline — as Q3 revenue rose to $16.8 billion from $11.6 billion, though slower monthly growth rates are already raising valuation-multiple questions that public market investors will be the first to answer with real capital at risk.

The Public Benefit Fund structure is Anthropic’s attempt to preserve its public-benefit corporation identity through a public listing — a structural mechanism that locks in its stated mission commitment before investors who may not prioritize mission over returns can dilute it through board influence. The 5% initial holding, growing with valuation increases, is designed to ensure the fund remains materially significant even as the company scales. Whether public equity investors will pay a $1.5 trillion multiple for a company whose closest rival is simultaneously pitching a higher revenue projection to private investors at a marginally lower valuation is the central uncertainty of Tuesday’s AI industry story.

Meta’s Muse Spark personal AI agent has reached 5 million downloads faster than OpenAI’s ChatGPT, Anthropic’s Claude, or SpaceX’s Grok, establishing it as the fastest-growing consumer AI agent on record — a consumer distribution signal that arrives the day before Meta reports Q3 earnings Wednesday, where investors will scrutinize Meta’s AI capital expenditure guidance as the most consequential forward signal for AI equity valuations this quarter; if Meta maintains or increases its data center and infrastructure commitments, the AI-repricing thesis that drove last week’s Nasdaq selloff weakens because the market’s largest-cap AI operator is doubling down.

Muse Spark’s 5 million download milestone is the consumer distribution story that corporate AI agents have been waiting for: prior consumer AI agents reached large user counts through organic discovery over months; Muse Spark’s accelerated timeline suggests either a significant distribution advantage from Meta’s social platform reach or a product-market fit that its rivals have not yet achieved at scale. For Wednesday’s earnings, the Muse Spark data provides both a direct revenue signal — consumer AI agents monetize differently from API-based enterprise products — and a platform leverage signal: Meta’s ability to distribute AI products through its existing user base faster than standalone AI companies may be the competitive moat the market has been underpricing.

Microsoft CEO of AI Mustafa Suleiman confirmed in Berlin that Microsoft is now free to independently pursue artificial superintelligence, a statement that marks a structural shift in Microsoft’s AI strategy from OpenAI-dependent to self-directed at the capability frontier; Tenstorrent raised $1.5 billion at a $6.2 billion valuation, continuing the trend of AI chip challengers attracting significant capital as alternatives to Nvidia; and Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang appeared with German Chancellor Friedrich Merz in Berlin to recast Germany’s industrial manufacturing base as physical-AI training data — positioning Germany’s factory infrastructure as a source of real-world training signal for robots and industrial AI systems.

The Suleiman statement is the week’s most quietly consequential AI governance signal: Microsoft’s original OpenAI partnership gave OpenAI exclusive access to Microsoft’s Azure infrastructure in exchange for constraints on Microsoft’s own AI development. Suleiman’s claim that Microsoft is “free” to build toward superintelligence independently means those constraints have either expired or been renegotiated — which changes the competitive landscape by adding a third labs-scale capability actor (alongside OpenAI and Anthropic) to the ASI race. The Huang-Merz Berlin summit frames the physical-AI thesis at the national infrastructure level: Germany’s challenge is that its industrial strength is in physical manufacturing, and Huang is arguing that physical manufacturing is exactly the training data that the next generation of industrial AI needs.
