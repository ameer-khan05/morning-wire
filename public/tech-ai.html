The Anchor · Anthropic’s IPO — 6x Covered and the Week’s AI Capital Story

The IPO book closed six times over. The question now is whether the week’s national security backdrop — banned Nvidia chips in a Shanghai data center, $7.5 billion in alleged smuggling, Lutnick preparing to testify — has been priced into what the market is calling a $1.4 trillion AI lab. Anthropic asked investors for up to $64.4 billion in equity at the $70–$78 IPO range. It received orders for approximately six times that amount — roughly $386 billion in institutional demand. The oversubscription is not abstract: Google committed $10 billion and Nvidia committed $10 billion, each buying Class N non-voting shares in the IPO at the same price as retail investors. AMD added $2.5 billion. Broadcom, Micron, and Microsoft added approximately $1 billion each. TSMC added $500 million. The seven-company anchor package totals $26 billion — more than 40% of Anthropic’s total IPO at the high end of the range. What it means: Google and Nvidia are not passive investors in this IPO. They are customers, cloud providers, chip suppliers, and competitors simultaneously — and they are each putting $10 billion into the company that trains Claude models on Nvidia GPUs via Google Cloud. The same week’s Nvidia national security crisis — banned H800 chips confirmed in a Shanghai data center, 1.5 gigawatts of restricted AI hardware tracked to China, Lutnick testifying before Congress — sits beneath Anthropic’s $1.4 trillion valuation in a way that neither the IPO roadshow nor the institutional investor base has fully addressed publicly. The question the IPO market has not yet priced is whether the chip export control regime that underpins US AI competitive advantage has been systematically compromised. The 6x oversubscription reflects confidence in AI’s commercial trajectory. It does not resolve the supply chain security question.

Anthropic’s IPO book closed at approximately 6x oversubscribed as of Saturday, with its November 10 S-1 amendment listing seven anchor investors buying $26 billion of Class N stock at the IPO price — Google and Nvidia each at $10 billion, AMD at $2.5 billion, Broadcom, Micron, and Microsoft at approximately $1 billion each, and TSMC at $500 million — in an offering that asks for $64.4 billion and received orders for roughly six times that amount; the valuation at $70–$78 per share implies $1.2 trillion to $1.4 trillion for Anthropic as a public company, making it the most valuable technology company to debut on public markets; final pricing is November 18 and Nasdaq trading as “ANT” begins November 19; the IPO’s 6x subscription level, the highest for a major US technology IPO in years, is a data point about institutional capital’s confidence in AI’s commercial trajectory — it does not resolve the hardware supply chain security questions raised by the same week’s Nvidia chip smuggling revelations.

The anchor investor composition is as strategically significant as the subscription level. Google is Anthropic’s cloud provider and a Claude competitor. Nvidia makes the GPUs that train Claude. Their combined $20 billion commitment is not passive investment — it is each company deepening a relationship with the lab that is simultaneously their partner, their customer, and their rival in the AI platform race.

OpenAI’s quarterly revenue crossed $10 billion for the first time in Q3 2026 — up nearly 50% from the second quarter’s $6.7 billion and representing the fastest quarterly revenue growth in the company’s history — driven by strong demand for its recent AI models and enterprise contract expansion; operating losses, however, widened to $10.9 billion in Q3, exceeding the revenue OpenAI generated in the same period as the company invested heavily in model training, compute infrastructure, and talent; OpenAI also raised $15 billion in the same period at a post-money valuation of $1.25 trillion, with its investors absorbing the widened losses as the cost of maintaining the revenue trajectory that crossed $10 billion; at $1.25 trillion, OpenAI trades at approximately 125x its current annual revenue run rate — a multiple that prices extraordinary growth continuation rather than current profitability.

The $10 billion quarterly revenue milestone matters as a benchmark, not just an absolute number: it confirms OpenAI’s model of relentless capability investment producing compounding commercial returns faster than the losses expand. The gap between $10B revenue and $10.9B losses is roughly the same size; the question for investors at $1.25T is whether the revenue curve continues to outrun the loss curve as the enterprise AI market matures.

Colette Kress, Nvidia’s CFO, was formally confirmed Saturday as interim CEO following Jensen Huang’s resignation on Thursday — she will serve simultaneously as EVP/CFO and interim CEO while the board conducts its CEO search — as Nvidia shares fell 12.75% on the week, its worst five-day performance since the chip scandal that preceded Huang’s departure; analysts and VCs are now repricing an AI ecosystem where Nvidia under Jensen Huang wrote the anchor check on neoclouds, inference platforms, and open-weight labs — the question for the post-Jensen era is whether the institutional relationships and strategic bets that made Nvidia the indispensable AI infrastructure layer persist without the CEO who built them; the transition arrives as the DOJ investigation into the Shanghai data center and Megaspeed International chip smuggling operation is active, adding an ongoing national security dimension to the standard transition uncertainty.

Colette Kress’s simultaneous CFO and interim CEO role is unusual and signals that the board’s priority is financial and operational continuity — not a transformative search. The public CEO transition process will run on a separate track from the DOJ investigation; for investors, the distinction matters: the former is manageable, the latter is not.

Tesla SpaceX Merger Boards Set Up Special Committees Bloomberg Deal Potentially December 2026 Musk Companies Consolidating Single Public Entity Saturday November 14 2026 Tesla-SpaceX Merger · Boards Set Committees · Deal Potentially December Tesla-SpaceX (Sat Nov 14 2026): merger → boards → special → committees → Bloomberg → December → deal → Musk → companies → consolidate → single → public

The boards of Tesla and SpaceX set up special committees last month to discuss merger terms and governance, Bloomberg reported, with a deal potentially coming as early as December 2026 — a consolidation that would combine Tesla’s public equity and electric vehicle platform with SpaceX’s rocket, Starlink satellite, and AI division assets under a single public entity; the merger talks represent the culmination of a consolidation logic that Musk and investors have discussed since at least 2024 — SpaceX’s Starlink revenue now exceeds its launch revenue, creating an enterprise technology asset that could be valued more accurately in a combined public company than in the private market; the special committee process is the formal corporate governance step that precedes a binding merger vote — the creation of committees does not guarantee a deal, but signals that both boards have moved from informal discussion to structured deliberation.

The Tesla-SpaceX merger, if completed, would create the first major corporate combination of an electric vehicle platform and a space-and-telecommunications company — not a conglomerate by sector but a conglomerate by capability: electric mobility, autonomous driving, low-earth-orbit broadband, and rocket launch. The challenge is governance: SpaceX is Musk’s most personally controlled asset, and the merger’s terms will determine whether the new entity functions as a public company or a lightly-listed private one.
