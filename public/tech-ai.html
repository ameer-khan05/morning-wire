The Anchor · DeepSeek Super-agent — The ARTEX Pattern, Immediately Repeated

ARTEX went open-source on October 1. It was weaponized nine days after its developer closed the source code on October 8. DeepSeek released dsh-superagent on October 31 — the day ARTEX’s full operational implications were still being absorbed. The question every security team faces this Sunday is whether the nine-day clock just restarted. DeepSeek’s Super-agent is an autonomous reasoning agent designed to execute multi-step tasks end-to-end without human intervention at each step. That description maps directly onto what ARTEX did — which is why the timing of dsh-superagent’s release matters more than its capabilities at the prototype stage. Whether dsh-superagent can execute authentication attacks at 2,900 attempts per minute, as ARTEX did inside ConnectWise’s infrastructure, is a technical question that takes days to answer. What is already true is that state actors demonstrated with ARTEX that they can absorb, adapt, and operationally deploy an open-source AI agent before the security community has finished reading the initial CrowdStrike report. DeepSeek’s release arrived before that report was even one week old. The nine-day window is not a theoretical benchmark. It is a documented operational fact. Security teams have until approximately November 9 to determine whether dsh-superagent repeats it.

DeepSeek released an open-source prototype of its Super-agent — dsh-superagent — on October 31, one day after CrowdStrike’s Black October report documenting how ARTEX was weaponized nine days after its developer closed its source code; dsh-superagent is described as an autonomous reasoning agent capable of executing multi-step tasks end-to-end, a capability profile that maps directly onto the attack-chain automation ARTEX provided — reconnaissance, credential validation, privilege escalation without human intervention at each step — raising an immediate question for enterprise security teams about whether this is the sector’s second test of the nine-day open-source weaponization window that ARTEX established as a documented operational benchmark in October.

The critical difference between ARTEX and dsh-superagent is that ARTEX was built explicitly as a security penetration testing tool, while dsh-superagent is a general reasoning agent. General reasoning agents may require adaptation to become attack tools — but ARTEX showed that state actors with an existing copy of an open-source agent can adapt faster than the security community can respond. The benchmark is nine days. The window opened October 31.

The Microsoft-AFT-UFT AI Safety Standard for U.S. Schools took effect November 1, banning student-data training and AI companions in educational AI products — a standard developed jointly by Microsoft, the American Federation of Teachers, and the United Federation of Teachers — marking the first major federal-adjacent AI policy trigger to go live since the October earnings season repriced the sector’s investment thesis; the standard applies to educational technology vendors deploying AI in school settings and establishes compliance requirements around data use, transparency, and the types of AI interaction patterns permitted with minors in educational contexts.

The AI schools standard is significant not for its immediate commercial impact — which is limited to educational technology vendors — but for its template value: it is the first jointly negotiated AI deployment standard between a major technology company and two of the largest U.S. teachers’ unions, and it establishes a model for sector-specific AI policy that does not require Congressional legislation to take effect.
