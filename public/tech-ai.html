The Anchor · Anthropic IPO — The AI Industry’s First Real Public Market Test

Private valuations are negotiated between companies and investors with shared incentives. A public market valuation is set by millions of strangers making independent bets — and it is updated every second while the market is open. Anthropic going public at $1.33 trillion is not just a fundraising event. It is the AI industry submitting to a form of continuous valuation that it has never faced before. Anthropic is selling 825 million shares on Nasdaq at $70–$78 per share under the ticker “ANT.” At the midpoint ($74), the IPO raises $61 billion and prices the company at $1.33 trillion fully diluted — a valuation that places it in the same range as the world’s ten most valuable companies. At $78, it reaches $1.5 trillion. Seven strategic investors — including Google, Nvidia, and five others not yet publicly named — have agreed to purchase $26 billion of non-voting stock at the IPO price, providing a floor under the offering and signaling strategic alignment from the companies whose infrastructure Anthropic depends on most. The valuation is built on two claims: that Anthropic’s Claude models are commercially competitive with OpenAI’s GPT-4o and Google’s Gemini, and that the AI market is large enough to justify a $1.33 trillion price tag at current and near-term revenue levels. The first claim is defensible. The second requires the AI market to be significantly larger and more durable than any prior technology category at a comparable stage. That is the bet. Trading is expected to begin next week.

Anthropic launched the roadshow for its initial public offering on Wednesday, targeting a price range of $70–$78 per share on Nasdaq under the ticker “ANT” — 825 million shares that would raise $61 billion at the midpoint and imply a $1.33 trillion fully diluted valuation, the highest of any company to debut on a US exchange; bankers are reportedly weighing plans to price the IPO above the stated range given heavy investor interest, with the top-end valuation rising to $1.5 trillion at $78 per share; seven strategic partners, including Google and Nvidia, have committed to purchasing $26 billion in non-voting stock at the IPO price, providing a demand floor while signaling that the most important AI infrastructure providers are aligned with Anthropic’s long-term prospects; the last disclosed private valuation was $61.5 billion in a May 2026 private round, implying the IPO is pricing at more than a 21x step-up in six months; trading is expected to begin next week, with no specific date yet announced.

The gap between Anthropic’s last private valuation ($61.5B) and its IPO target ($1.33T) in six months is not a measure of Anthropic’s growth — it is a measure of how far private AI valuations have been suppressed relative to what a public market, priced against the AI infrastructure buildout and enterprise adoption data, is willing to pay. That compression will itself be one of the most-watched data points in AI market history once trading begins.

Google rolled out Gemini 4 Carbon to paying API developers and AI Ultra subscribers on Wednesday at an introductory price of $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens — pricing that rises to $4 per million input and $20 per million output on December 1, with the introductory window lasting just 19 days from general availability; Google positioned Gemini 4 Carbon as its most cost-efficient frontier model in the 4 family, sitting below Gemini 4 Ultra in capability but above Gemini 4 Flash in task complexity and reasoning quality; DeepMind separately announced that its AI agents had formally verified the correctness of Signal’s end-to-end encryption protocol using mechanized mathematical proof — a process that traces every logical step from the cryptographic specification through the implementation to confirm that no gap exists between what the protocol promises and what the code delivers — the first formal cryptographic verification of a consumer messaging standard at this scale, and a demonstration that AI can play a role in security auditing that previously required dozens of human cryptographers over months.

Gemini 4 Carbon’s pricing is competitive with Anthropic’s Claude 3.5 Sonnet at comparable capability levels — meaning the pricing war between frontier AI providers is now live at the product tier most enterprise developers use. The DeepMind Signal verification is a different kind of signal: AI agents doing useful cryptographic work at scale is what “AI doing real security work” looks like, and it arrives the same week Anthropic prices itself at $1.33 trillion.

New York Governor Kathy Hochul announced a state-run independent AI evaluation program on Wednesday, establishing a framework under which AI systems deployed in New York — in areas including healthcare, education, criminal justice, and financial services — must pass independent technical evaluation before public deployment, with assessments covering accuracy, bias, transparency, and risk of harm; the program creates a state-level AI standards body with authority to issue evaluations but not to block deployment, structured as advisory rather than regulatory at launch, with the governor indicating that mandatory enforcement authority may follow in a 2027 legislative package; New York’s program is the most significant state-level AI governance initiative since California’s 2025 framework, and the first to focus explicitly on pre-deployment technical evaluation rather than post-hoc liability.

New York doing what California did — but focused on pre-deployment technical evaluation rather than the broader liability framework California tried — is the right sequencing for state-level AI governance. Advisory-first establishes the institutional capacity and builds the technical knowledge base; mandatory-enforcement-later is what becomes sustainable once the regulator actually knows what it’s evaluating. The 2027 legislative package will reveal whether that path is real or a deferral.

The United States and China agreed in diplomatic meetings on Wednesday to establish a direct AI incident-alert channel — a bilateral mechanism for rapid government-to-government communication when either side identifies dangerous AI system malfunctions, misuse by third parties, or behavior that could affect the other nation’s critical infrastructure — in a move that extends the AI safety conversation between the two countries beyond the annual diplomatic summits that have provided the only structured forum to date; the channel is modeled on the “hotline” frameworks established during the Cold War for nuclear incidents and updated for internet-era cyberattacks, adapted for the AI context where attribution is harder and timelines for damage may be faster; a nine-country audience survey published the same day found that 51% of respondents now use standalone AI tools — ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini — weekly, up from 38% in 2025, and that generative AI for news and information jumped to 38% weekly from 24% a year ago.

The US-China AI incident channel is the most significant bilateral AI safety agreement since the two countries committed to pause development of autonomous weapons systems. What makes it notable is the implicit admission in both governments: that AI systems can now produce effects severe enough to require the same crisis-communication infrastructure as nuclear weapons and cyberattacks. The 51% weekly AI use figure is the demand-side context: the systems that need this oversight are already deployed at population scale.
