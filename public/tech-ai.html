The Anchor · The AI Chip Export Rule — What Commerce’s 132-Page Interim Final Rule Closes and Why Xi Has to Respond Before Shenzhen

The Commerce Department’s new AI chip export rule does three things simultaneously: it closes the remote-access loophole that allowed Chinese companies to treat foreign data centers as an end-run around existing chip restrictions, it creates licensing requirements for five countries deemed to have elevated diversion risk, and it makes the chip restriction effectively global for Chinese firms rather than bilateral. The loophole the rule closes was structural, not incidental. When the Biden administration implemented the original chip export controls targeting China, it restricted the physical export of Nvidia’s most advanced chips to Chinese entities. What it did not restrict was Chinese companies paying for time on those same chips through data centers in Singapore, Malaysia, or Brazil — countries that had no such restrictions. The practical effect was that Chinese AI labs could train on Nvidia H100s and B200s without ever importing them. Commerce’s interim final rule ends this by treating remote computing access as equivalent to export for restriction purposes. The five-country licensing requirement adds a second layer: even legitimate export to those countries now requires government approval, reducing the probability that chips sold to data centers in Singapore or Indonesia will be rented to Chinese firms while ostensibly serving other customers. The Bloomberg framing — that the chip curbs force Xi to weigh a response before the Shenzhen summit Thursday — identifies the most significant diplomatic consequence of Tuesday’s timing. Xi is already committed to meeting Trump and Putin on Thursday. The chip rule forces him to arrive at a summit with the world’s other two most powerful leaders while simultaneously deciding whether to retaliate against a unilateral US action announced two days before the meeting. That is not a coincidence of timing. It is a negotiating posture.

The Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security published a 132-page interim final rule Tuesday closing the loophole that allowed Chinese companies to access Nvidia’s most restricted AI chips by renting compute through data centers in Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, and Brazil — the rule makes remote computing access to restricted chips equivalent to physical export for restriction purposes, and separately bans Chinese firms from accessing restricted compute through any third-country arrangement regardless of geography; the five-country licensing requirement creates a new approval layer for chip exports to nations deemed at elevated diversion risk, reducing the ability of data center operators in those countries to rent compute to Chinese customers under existing trade terms; Bloomberg reported that the timing forces Xi Jinping to decide his response to a unilateral US action before sitting down with Trump and Putin in Shenzhen on Thursday, characterizing the chip rule as a negotiating variable introduced 48 hours before the most consequential trilateral summit of the year.

The rule is not a chip export ban — it is an administrative closure of an engineering workaround. Chinese AI labs that have been computing on Nvidia hardware through Singapore-based data centers must now either obtain licenses, restructure their compute arrangements, or accept significantly constrained access. The 90-day implementation window for the five-country licensing requirement means the operational impact on Chinese AI labs is six to eight weeks away, not immediate. The market impact on Nvidia is immediate: the addressable market just shrank by the revenue associated with Chinese remote-access compute.
