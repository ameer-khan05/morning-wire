The Anchor · OpenAI's $40B ARR at $852B Private Valuation Is the AI Era's IPO Benchmark — The S-1 Will Reveal Whether the Market Prices AI Infrastructure Economics or AI Platform Multiples

OpenAI's public S-1 filing is the most anticipated technology prospectus since at least the Meta IPO of 2012 — and structurally more significant, because its financials will set the public-market reference point for how AI companies are valued at scale. The Bloomberg revenue confirmation ($40 billion annualized run rate, roughly double the end-2025 pace) means the company is growing very fast. The loss structure — approximately $1.22 in losses per $1 earned — means it is also burning capital at a rate that requires the public market to believe the AI infrastructure cost curve will compress as the model improves, the way cloud computing cost curves compressed for SaaS companies in the 2010s. That is a plausible bet, but it is a bet, not a guarantee. The $852 billion private-round valuation already prices a significant portion of that bet — a public listing above $1 trillion means retail and institutional investors are being asked to price it further. Whether the S-1 justifies those multiples depends on three numbers the prospectus will reveal: gross margin (how much of each $1 of revenue survives after compute costs), the revenue growth trajectory quarter-by-quarter, and the path to cash-flow breakeven. If gross margins are expanding as the model becomes more efficient, the IPO case is strong. If gross margins are flat or shrinking — because frontier model compute costs are rising as fast as revenue — the $1T+ target requires pure growth-multiple faith. Big Tech's $1.5 trillion in aggregate AI infrastructure commitments provides the denominator context for OpenAI's supply economics: the companies competing with OpenAI on AI infrastructure are spending at a scale that makes model compute cost efficiency the critical competitive variable, not research capability alone. Apple's China AI model with Alibaba deserves more attention than it has received. Training an AI model specifically for the Chinese market — where US-origin AI products are either restricted or commercially disadvantaged — and doing so with Alibaba as a partner, means Apple is making a deliberate choice to provide AI capabilities in China through a domestic partner rather than exporting American AI under export-control constraints. This is a template other US tech companies with significant China revenue will evaluate: develop separate China-market AI products with local partners, avoiding the export restriction exposure that limits Nvidia chip sales and OpenAI API access in China. If Apple's Alibaba-partnered China model succeeds commercially, it will be replicated.

Gemini's 1 billion monthly active users milestone — reached by August 11, nearly two years after general availability — is Google's clearest evidence that it has not ceded the AI consumer market to OpenAI, and that its distribution advantage (embedding Gemini in Android, Gmail, Google Workspace, and Search) is doing what Google's distribution always does: compounding reach faster than an independent startup can match organically. ChatGPT crossed 1 billion users in roughly 18 months from launch; Gemini's path reflects both Google's distribution power and the slower initial reception for a product that took time to match GPT-4-level quality.

The IPO would be the largest US technology listing in years.

The financial picture Bloomberg confirmed — $40 billion run rate, $1.22 in losses per $1 earned — means the IPO pricing argument centers on the belief that AI compute costs will compress as models become more efficient and inference economics improve, the way cloud infrastructure economics changed from 2014 to 2020. That argument is plausible: OpenAI's newer models are more capable and cheaper per token than their predecessors, and inference optimization has been a consistent improvement curve. Whether the public market will price that belief at $1 trillion-plus in the current interest rate environment — with 10-year yields recently touching near-decade highs — is the IPO's central tension.

The six largest US technology companies — Alphabet, Microsoft, Amazon, Nvidia, Oracle, and Meta — have accumulated close to $1.5 trillion in combined purchase commitments tied to AI infrastructure: data center capacity, chip procurement contracts, energy agreements, and computing buildout that represents the physical substrate of every AI product sold in 2026 and beyond. The figure dwarfs any prior technology infrastructure investment cycle and sets the AI compute cost environment within which smaller AI companies, including OpenAI, operate their model training and inference economics. Nvidia occupies a unique dual position: it receives a portion of that $1.5 trillion in chip sales while also committing its own infrastructure capital.

Apple's China AI model, trained with Alibaba's assistance, represents a commercially pragmatic response to the US-China technology decoupling dynamic. US export restrictions limit Nvidia chip access in China and constrain the deployment of American AI API products for Chinese consumers. Apple's path around this — partnering with Alibaba to build a China-specific model that runs on domestically available hardware and satisfies Chinese data-sovereignty requirements — preserves Apple's AI competitiveness in a market it cannot access through its US-developed AI stack. If the model succeeds commercially, it will become the template for other US technology companies that generate significant China revenue and cannot afford to cede their AI product differentiation to local competitors.

Google's Gemini crossed 1 billion monthly active users as of August 11 — a milestone that reflects both the power of Google's distribution infrastructure and the AI assistant market's rapid maturation from early-adopter technology to mainstream consumer product. The 1 billion figure covers users who interact with Gemini across Google's embedded integration points: Android devices, Gmail, Google Workspace, Google Search's AI Overviews, and the standalone Gemini app. Google's path to 1 billion monthly active users is faster in absolute terms than any prior Google product at comparable market maturity, because Gemini began with embedded distribution across Google's existing user base rather than acquiring users from scratch.

The competitive significance for OpenAI is direct: Gemini's 1 billion users, combined with Google's contribution to the $1.5 trillion Big Tech AI infrastructure build and the Gemini Ultra and Gemini Flash model tiers competing against OpenAI's GPT-4o and o3 series, means OpenAI is pricing its September IPO into a market where its primary competitor is already at consumer-AI scale through distribution advantages OpenAI cannot replicate organically. The IPO's $1 trillion-plus valuation target implicitly assumes OpenAI's brand, API ecosystem, and developer relationships are worth a premium over Gemini's distribution-driven reach — a premium the S-1 will need to justify with enterprise revenue concentration and developer retention data.

Quick hits

  • The OpenAI S-1's gross margin line will be the most important number in the prospectus. If OpenAI is generating $40 billion in annualized revenue and losing $1.22 per $1 earned, gross margins below 30% would indicate that compute costs — model training and inference — are consuming more than 70 cents of every dollar, leaving almost nothing for sales, R&D, and G&A before the company reaches operating loss. SaaS companies that successfully went public at comparable growth rates typically had gross margins above 65%. OpenAI's best case is that its newest models (o3, GPT-4o) run cheaply enough on inference that gross margins are higher than the loss ratio implies — meaning the losses are in R&D and compute for next-generation training, not in serving existing customers. The S-1 will settle the debate.
  • Apple's Alibaba partnership for the China AI model has a regulatory dimension that its commercial framing obscures. US export control law restricts the transfer of AI model weights, training data, and certain algorithmic techniques to entities in restricted countries. Training a model "with Alibaba's help" can range from fully compliant (Alibaba provides compute, Apple controls model architecture and weights) to potentially complex (joint architecture design, shared training data, model weight transfer). The exact nature of the collaboration — who controls what, which party retains the weights, and whether Alibaba's involvement constitutes a technology transfer — is the legal question that will follow Apple's China AI strategy into its next earnings call and any Congressional scrutiny of US-China AI technology flows.
