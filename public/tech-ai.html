Tech & AI · Deep Dive · OpenAI + Broadcom Jalapeño Inference Chip: 13.4 PFLOPs at 700W, 1.5–1.9× Nvidia Efficiency — Deployed by Year-End · Warsh Hawkish Speech Triggers Tech Selloff · Nasdaq ▼0.50% · Rate-Hike Odds to 55.7% · Friday, August 28, 2026

OpenAI and Broadcom published detailed benchmarks for the Jalapeño inference chip Friday — 13.4 PFLOPs of MXFP4 compute at 700W on TSMC N3P, paired with HBM4 at 15.4 TB/s — posting 1.5–1.9× better work per watt than Nvidia hardware across GPT-OSS, DeepSeek R1, and Kimi K2.5 1T. The chip was taped out in 16 months from design to silicon, reaching 700+ tokens per second per user on DeepSeek R1 and approximately 1,400 tok/s on GPT-OSS, with OpenAI confirming deployment within its own infrastructure by year-end and work on second and third generations already underway. Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh simultaneously delivered a hawkish Jackson Hole keynote — "inflation is too high, the Fed has more work to do" — that sent rate-hike odds for September to 55.7% and triggered a tech selloff that unwound much of Thursday's Nvidia-led gain, with the Nasdaq falling 0.50% to 26,411.07.

Jalapeño is the first serious empirical challenge to Nvidia's inference dominance from a hyperscale customer that has built its own silicon. OpenAI's 1.5–1.9× efficiency claim — if it holds across diverse production workloads — means every inference dollar deployed on Jalapeño buys materially more computation than the same dollar on Nvidia, for the specific workloads OpenAI cares most about. That is the needle every AI hardware competitor has chased since 2022, and OpenAI appears to have threaded it for inference, even as Nvidia retains its dominance for training.

The Anchor · Jalapeño Proves Custom Silicon Can Beat Nvidia at Inference — But Warsh's Rate-Hike Warning Is the Day's Bigger Market Story

OpenAI's Jalapeño chip is the most significant empirical evidence yet that the hyperscaler custom-silicon strategy can produce inference hardware that outperforms Nvidia's best on real-world LLM workloads — not on a benchmark designed to showcase the chip's architecture, but on the three workloads that define OpenAI's actual production inference: its own GPT-OSS models, DeepSeek R1, and Kimi K2.5. A 1.5–1.9× efficiency advantage at 700W versus Rubin's 900-1,150W means OpenAI can serve the same number of tokens at lower power and lower cost per token, closing the economic gap that has made Nvidia hardware necessary even when operators have wanted alternatives. The chip does not threaten Nvidia's training dominance — Jalapeño is optimized for inference, not for the large-scale matrix multiplications that define training workloads, where Blackwell and Rubin will remain dominant. But it establishes that inference, the fastest-growing segment of AI compute spending as deployed models scale, is now a competitive market where custom silicon from a major hyperscaler-equivalent customer has crossed the performance threshold. Warsh's Jackson Hole keynote changes the tech investment context in ways that are more immediately market-relevant than Jalapeño. The Fed Chair said Friday that inflation is "still too high" and that the central bank "has more work to do" — the clearest signal yet of a hawkish pivot away from the September cut that markets had been pricing. Rate-hike odds for September jumped to 55.7% from roughly 35% the day before. For AI infrastructure spending, the rate environment matters not because Nvidia's Blackwell sales require cheap credit — they don't, the hyperscalers fund GPU purchases from operating cash flows — but because the discount rate applied to long-duration AI earnings trajectories rises with every basis point of additional rate pressure. Stocks that are priced on 2028-2030 AI earnings are more sensitive to the discount rate than stocks priced on 2026 earnings, which is why the Nasdaq fell 0.50% despite Jalapeño's positive news. The $96.22 billion Nvidia beat was Thursday's story; the hawkish Fed is Friday's.

OpenAI and Broadcom published detailed specifications and benchmarks for the Jalapeño inference chip Friday — 13.4 PFLOPs of MXFP4 compute at 700W on TSMC N3P, with HBM4 memory bandwidth at 15.4 TB/s — confirming it delivers 1.5 to 1.9 times more work per watt than Nvidia hardware on the three LLM workloads OpenAI runs at scale: GPT-OSS, DeepSeek R1, and Kimi K2.5 1T. The chip hits 700+ tokens per second per user on DeepSeek R1 and approximately 1,400 tokens per second on GPT-OSS, while drawing 200 to 450 watts less than Nvidia's Rubin architecture at similar throughput levels. OpenAI says Jalapeño will be deployed in its production inference infrastructure by year-end, and the company is already working on second and third silicon generations.

The 16-month tape-out timeline — from design start to production-capable silicon — is itself significant: it demonstrates that a non-semiconductor company with a focused partner can build competitive AI inference silicon faster than the traditional chip development cycle that gave Nvidia its multi-year head start. Jalapeño does not challenge Nvidia for training workloads, where Blackwell and Rubin remain dominant and where the matrix-multiplication scale requirements play to Nvidia's architecture strengths. But inference is the segment growing fastest as AI model deployment scales beyond training — and Jalapeño's efficiency advantage in that segment means the addressable market where Nvidia competes without a performance guarantee is growing.

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh delivered his first Jackson Hole keynote Friday, telling the annual economic policy symposium that inflation is "still too high" and that the central bank "has more work to do" to bring price stability — language widely read as closing the door on a September rate cut and opening it on potential rate hikes by October or December. Markets initially edged higher on Warsh's comment that "the economy appears to have strengthened," but turned lower as the cumulative hawkish signal dominated: CME FedWatch showed September rate-hike probability jumping to 55.7% from roughly 35% the day before. The Nasdaq fell 0.50% to 26,411.07 and the S&P 500 fell 0.30% to 7,715.47, partially unwinding Thursday's Nvidia-led gains, with short-term Treasury yields rising several basis points as the FOMC blackout begins Saturday.

For the tech sector specifically, the rate-hike repricing is a double pressure: it raises the discount rate applied to long-duration AI earnings, and it forces a reassessment of whether the hyperscaler AI capital expenditure cycle — which Nvidia's $96.22 billion Q2 result validated Thursday — is priced for a declining rate environment that may not materialize. The combination of Jalapeño's efficiency signal (Nvidia has inference competition) and Warsh's hawkish signal (the macro tailwind may become a headwind) creates a more complex tech investment backdrop entering the weekend than the simple Nvidia-beat narrative of Thursday suggested.

Nvidia AI Server Prices Rising 15%+ in Early 2027 — Contract Builders Warn Microsoft, Google, Oracle · Federal Judge Blocks Pentagon from Blacklisting Anthropic for Drawing AI Safety Lines · AWS Mechanical Turk Shutting Down Sept 30 After 21 Years +15% Nvidia server prices (Fri Aug 28): contract manufacturers (Foxconn, Quanta, Wistron) told hyperscalers that AI server prices rising 15%+ starting early 2027 → reasons: Blackwell Ultra + Rubin advanced packaging costs, CoWoS-S/L substrate constraints, cooling system complexity → affected: Microsoft, Google, Oracle → implication: AI compute costs rising even as GPU performance improves → partially offsets Nvidia's Blackwell efficiency gains for buyers → Pentagon-Anthropic: federal judge ruled DoD cannot blacklist Anthropic for enforcing AI safety policies on content generation → free speech grounds → Anthropic had declined certain military-use requests citing safety guidelines → AWS Mechanical Turk: shutdown announced for Sept 30, 2026 → 2005-era platform → AI displacement cited: most tasks previously done by human annotators now handled by AI models → marks end of crowdsourced-AI-training-data era

Nvidia's contract server manufacturers — Foxconn, Quanta, and Wistron — have told Microsoft, Google, and Oracle that AI server prices will rise more than 15% starting in early 2027, driven by the advanced packaging costs, CoWoS substrate constraints, and cooling complexity associated with Blackwell Ultra and Rubin hardware generations. The price increase partially offsets the performance improvements of next-generation Nvidia hardware and adds cost pressure to hyperscaler AI infrastructure budgets already under scrutiny following Warsh's hawkish Jackson Hole remarks. Separately, a federal judge ruled that the Pentagon cannot blacklist Anthropic from government contracting for enforcing AI safety lines on content generation — a decision that affirms AI companies' ability to decline certain use cases on safety grounds without facing government retaliation. AWS announced the shutdown of Mechanical Turk effective September 30, closing a 21-year-old crowdsourced task platform whose core use case — human annotation for AI training — has been substantially displaced by AI models themselves.

The Mechanical Turk shutdown is a quiet but significant signal about the AI automation cycle: the platform that trained generations of AI systems by paying humans to label data, transcribe audio, and classify images is being closed because the AI systems it helped train can now do those tasks more efficiently. The AWS decision also ends a chapter in the AI annotation economy that employed hundreds of thousands of workers globally. The Pentagon-Anthropic ruling carries broader implications for the government AI procurement market: it establishes that safety-motivated refusals by AI companies are protected, which affects how defense agencies approach AI safety requirements in future contracts.

Friday's two tech stories — Jalapeño and Warsh — point in opposite directions, and understanding both is required to understand where the AI sector goes from here. Jalapeño says that OpenAI has built inference silicon that beats Nvidia on efficiency for the workloads that matter most to OpenAI, in 16 months, with a commercial partner rather than a semiconductor team built from scratch. That is unambiguously positive for AI adoption: if inference gets cheaper, more AI gets deployed. But Warsh says the macro environment that made AI infrastructure investment easy — declining rates, accommodative monetary policy, a Fed that was signaling relief — may be ending. Rate hikes are now the market's base case for the next six months, and AI companies that raised capital, set spending plans, and priced long-duration earnings on the assumption of declining rates face a discount-rate environment that has just gotten materially worse. The two stories coexist: AI capability is advancing (Jalapeño), and the macro tailwind that made AI infrastructure investment easy is reversing (Warsh). The net effect on the AI sector will depend on which force dominates — and Friday's 0.50% Nasdaq decline suggests the market is not yet sure.
