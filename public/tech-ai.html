The Anchor · The Orbital AI Layer — What It Means That the FCC Has Cleared 1 Million AI Satellite Data Centers While Astronomers Warn the Sky Is Being Closed

The FCC’s Starmind authorization is not a satellite story. It is a computing infrastructure story with a satellite delivery mechanism. SpaceX has proposed putting AI computing hardware — processors, memory, storage — into orbit on solar-powered satellites at altitudes between 500 and 1,002 kilometers. The pitch is that orbital AI processing has lower latency to globally distributed users than terrestrial data centers in a few metro areas, and that solar power eliminates the energy-cost problem that is constraining the expansion of AI infrastructure on the ground. The FCC approved the authorization in principle on November 25, denying petitions to block it from Amazon’s Kuiper Systems, Viasat, and the American Astronomical Society. The Astronomical Society’s concern is specific: 1 million satellites in low-Earth orbit would create a continuous curtain of moving lights visible from every ground-based optical telescope on the planet, permanently degrading ground-based astronomy. SpaceX has not been required to resolve the light-pollution question before launching — the FCC deferred it. The commercial and scientific stakes are running in opposite directions: orbital AI infrastructure would be a transformative capability for edge computing and global AI access; a permanently brightened sky would foreclose scientific observation that cannot be replicated from space. The FCC chose the commercial path and deferred the scientific one. That deferral will not last: the first Starmind satellite launches will force a resolution.

The FCC authorized SpaceX’s Starmind constellation of up to 1 million solar-powered AI data center satellites on Wednesday — the first full approval of an AI satellite fleet in US regulatory history — granting SpaceX authority to operate at altitudes between 500 and 1,002 km, with TechCrunch and Spaceflight Now reporting that SpaceX must launch its first satellite by November 2033, 10% of the constellation by 2035, half by 2038, and the full fleet by 2040 or the authorization lapses; the FCC denied petitions to block the approval filed by Amazon’s Kuiper Systems, Viasat, and the American Astronomical Society, deferring the AAS’s light-pollution concerns to a later proceeding; and NPR reported that scientists are skeptical — 1 million additional low-Earth-orbit satellites would create a nearly continuous luminous curtain visible to ground-based optical telescopes worldwide, potentially ending certain categories of ground-based astronomical observation that cannot be replicated from space.

The FCC deferred rather than resolved the astronomy dispute. SpaceX’s Starlink constellation has already created measurable interference with optical astronomy; Starmind at 1 million satellites is an order of magnitude larger. The question that the FCC did not answer — whether SpaceX can mitigate light pollution at that scale — will be answered by the first launches, not by the authorization.

Governor Kathy Hochul signed bill S.9408A/A.11144B on Tuesday, November 24 — at a Harlem toy store, in a deliberate pre-holiday signal — establishing a five-year prohibition on the manufacture, distribution, and sale of AI chatbot-embedded toys in New York, with NBC News reporting the law imposes fines of $15,000 per day per violation enforced by the state Attorney General; the ban covers toys with embedded AI chatbots — talking stuffed animals, dolls, robots, and similar products that engage children in conversational interactions — and takes effect in 90 days, meaning toys already on shelves for the 2026 holiday season remain legal to purchase through February; and New York becomes the second state after California to ban AI chatbot toys, with Hochul warning parents that the prohibition does not cover holiday purchases made before the 90-day effective date, citing documented cases of AI toys making disturbing remarks to children as the primary safety basis for the ban.

The 90-day lag is both a grace period and a loophole: retailers can sell existing AI toy inventory through the holiday season without violating the law, and manufacturers can ship existing product into New York until February. The law’s enforcement mechanism — $15,000 per day per violation — is designed for deterrence rather than revenue, targeting the economic risk calculation of manufacturers rather than individual sellers.

A coalition of 42 state attorneys general filed formal objections to Character.AI’s plan to auction private user chat data, arguing that the auction violates state consumer privacy statutes and that users never provided informed consent to have their conversational data sold; the objection letter — signed by AGs from 42 states — contends that Character.AI’s terms of service, while containing broad data-use provisions, did not specifically disclose that conversations could be auctioned to third parties, making the auction non-compliant with state-level consent requirements; and the multi-state action is the largest coordinated state AG intervention in an AI platform data-use dispute since the emergence of large consumer AI applications, with the coalition’s size suggesting that Character.AI faces a credible legal obstacle to completing the auction without revising its user consent framework or abandoning the plan.

Forty-two AGs is a rare alignment in consumer protection enforcement — it signals that the legal theory is broadly accepted across both Democratic- and Republican-led state attorneys general offices and that the enforcement risk is not concentrated in a single jurisdiction. Character.AI’s next move is either to revise its consent framework and reoffer the auction, or to abandon it — proceeding without resolving the objection would put the company in simultaneous litigation in dozens of states.

OpenAI began a limited rollout of a unified Chat and Work experience within ChatGPT for a small group of Pro subscribers, consolidating the platform’s conversational interface and productivity-tool surfaces into a single integrated experience — the first significant interface restructuring of ChatGPT since the launch of the Projects and Tasks features earlier this year; the rollout, confirmed by multiple AI tracking publications on Thursday, positions ChatGPT to compete more directly with Microsoft Copilot and Google Gemini’s workspace integrations by reducing the context-switching cost for users who alternate between freeform conversation and structured work tasks; and the limited Pro-subscriber rollout follows OpenAI’s pattern of deploying interface changes to paying subscribers first to gather feedback before broader release, with no announced timeline for expansion to free or Plus tier accounts.

Unified interface rollouts in AI platforms tend to have one of two effects: they either reduce friction and increase daily active use, or they confuse users who had learned the previous navigation model. Pro subscribers — who pay $200 per month — are the highest-tolerance segment for interface experiments, which is why OpenAI consistently routes new UX deployments to this group before testing broader reception.
