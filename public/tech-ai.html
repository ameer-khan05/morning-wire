The Anchor · The SEC Probe — What Nvidia’s Accounting Investigation Means for the AI Trade’s Revenue Story and the Valuation Multiples Built on It

The SEC opened an accounting probe into Nvidia on Friday. Nvidia fell 14%. The stock has now fallen 46% across seven consecutive weekly losses. The probe is about how Nvidia reports where its data-center revenue comes from. The significance of the SEC’s accounting probe is not that it proves Nvidia has misreported revenue. It is that it introduces the possibility — and the regulatory mechanism to investigate it — at precisely the moment when the entire AI trade has been built on the assumption that the revenue these companies report from AI hardware sales is accurately characterized and attributable to the sources they describe. The GPU supercycle thesis depends on data-center AI chip demand being as large and as persistent as the revenue figures suggest. That thesis has been priced into Nvidia at record multiples for two years. The SEC’s probe doesn’t break the thesis Friday. What it does is introduce the category of regulatory risk that the AI hardware trade had not yet priced: the risk that the revenue reporting methodology, rather than the demand itself, comes under scrutiny. That is a different and structurally more serious risk than the export-control tightening and valuation questions that have driven the prior six weeks of Nvidia losses. The first six weeks were the market’s reassessment of forward demand. The seventh week is the opening of an investigation into whether the reported demand is what it says it is. These are different questions. The probe resolves relatively quickly if it is a disclosure methodology question. It resolves slowly and painfully if it is a revenue-characterization question. The SEC has not said which kind it is. That ambiguity is now part of Nvidia’s price.

Nvidia fell 14% Friday after Reuters reported that the SEC had opened an accounting investigation into how the company reports the geographic and business-unit sources of its data-center revenue — a probe that Motley Fool described as arriving “after its worst week in 18 years,” with the weekly loss reaching 22.9% and the stock now down 46% from its October peak across seven consecutive weekly declines; Crypto Briefing reported that the seventh weekly loss marks the longest losing streak since the 2008 financial crisis, with the SEC probe adding a new category of risk to Nvidia’s existing pressure from export-control tightening, demand uncertainty, and the broader AI-trade valuation reset triggered by Anthropic’s IPO pricing question; the SEC’s focus on how Nvidia categorizes whether its revenue comes from domestic or international data-center customers is significant because Nvidia’s export-control compliance has been a recurring question — a probe that ties revenue reporting to geographic categorization directly connects compliance and accounting in a way that extends the regulatory surface area beyond chip sales approval into the financial reporting of those sales.

The SEC accounting probe is structurally different from every other pressure Nvidia has faced this year. Export controls restrict what can be sold. Demand uncertainty questions how much will be bought. The probe questions how the sales that have already occurred were reported. That retroactive dimension — looking back at how existing revenue was characterized — introduces the possibility of restatements and enforcement actions that have no analogue in forward-looking demand or regulatory questions. At 46% below its October peak, the market has priced significant bad news. The probe opens the possibility of a category of bad news that wasn’t yet in the price.

Google’s Gemini Tasks launched the ability to call businesses on a user’s behalf — booking appointments, checking availability, negotiating hours, and handling other phone-based interactions — available free in the US, Japan, Korea, Canada, Australia, and India, extending Google’s Duplex-era concept of AI-made phone calls into the Gemini agent framework at no additional cost to users; the feature arrives the same week that Apple confirmed its own Mac AI agent and Cognition AI raised at $75 billion, completing the roster of major consumer AI platforms with live agentic task-execution capabilities beyond chat; and Sam on AI’s brief noted the same week also saw Google’s Genie 4 land in the Gemini API with playable 720p worlds with audio, as Google’s multimodal AI product push continues on multiple fronts simultaneously.

Gemini Tasks’ business-call capability is the most consumer-visible agentic action any major AI platform has deployed to date. Prior AI agents (Apple’s Mac agent, Microsoft’s Copilot Autopilot) operate on a user’s device. Gemini Tasks operates in the external world — on behalf of the user, talking to businesses that have no AI interface. That is a genuinely new category of AI-human-business interaction, and Google is offering it free at launch across six markets. The adoption rate in those markets will be the clearest early evidence of how consumers engage with AI acting as an external proxy.

Signal announced it will restrict screenshots on iOS and Android to prevent Siri and Gemini from reading users’ private chats — a defensive measure against the new generation of on-device AI assistants that can analyze screen contents in real time, with Signal’s move reflecting the growing tension between AI assistants’ screen-reading capabilities and end-to-end encrypted communication applications designed to prevent exactly that kind of content access; separately, a group of authors filed a collective lawsuit against six frontier AI labs over training data usage, adding a major content-creator legal front to the ongoing copyright litigation the AI industry has faced from news publishers, visual artists, and musicians; the two stories connect through the same underlying tension: the expansion of AI capabilities into personal and professional contexts is outpacing the legal and product frameworks designed to govern what AI systems may access and use.

Signal’s screenshot restriction is a product-layer response to a capability-layer problem. Siri and Gemini’s ability to read screen contents is a feature for most apps — it allows AI assistants to help with what’s on-screen. For Signal, it is a threat model, because the value proposition of end-to-end encryption is that only the sender and recipient can read the message. An AI assistant that reads the screen breaks that model at the application layer without touching the cryptography. Signal’s fix is technically sound. The broader question it raises is how many other sensitive applications are currently exposing their contents to on-device AI screen readers that users have granted broad permissions to.
