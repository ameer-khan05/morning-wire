The Anchor · Anthropic’s IPO — Wednesday Pricing Into an AI Selloff and the Week’s Hardest Question

The IPO book is six times oversubscribed. The valuation is up to $1.4 trillion. The pricing date is Wednesday. The question the market has not yet answered is whether the AI selloff that removed $10 billion in Nvidia market cap and pushed Bitcoin to $62,199 has been fully absorbed — or whether Wednesday’s debut lands into ongoing weakness. Anthropic’s S-1 amendment listed $26 billion in anchor investments — Google at $10 billion, Nvidia at $10 billion, AMD at $2.5 billion, Broadcom, Micron, and Microsoft at approximately $1 billion each, TSMC at $500 million. The 6x oversubscription reflects institutional confidence in AI’s commercial trajectory as of the roadshow period. What has changed since: Nvidia fell 12.75% on the week. Bitcoin fell 16.3%. The broader AI selloff reduced the valuations of AI-adjacent public companies across the board. And the AI selloff’s catalyst — Jensen Huang’s resignation and the national security investigation into Nvidia chip smuggling — has not resolved. Two of Nvidia’s largest outside shareholders are now petitioning the board to allow Jensen’s return, which is an institutional investor signal that the selloff has not been absorbed but repriced as a talent and governance risk. What this means for Wednesday’s pricing: Anthropic’s underwriters must decide whether to price at the high end of the $70–$78 range — maximizing valuation but risking a first-day decline — or price at the mid-to-low end, leaving performance for the IPO’s public narrative. The selloff has not broken the IPO’s structure — the $26 billion anchor package is committed regardless — but it has changed the conversation about where ANT opens on Thursday morning.

Two of Nvidia’s largest outside shareholders asked its board Sunday to leave the door open for Jensen Huang’s return as CEO — less than one week after his Thursday resignation — arguing that Huang’s relationships with anchor investors, government officials, and the AI ecosystem are central to Nvidia’s ability to maintain its infrastructure dominance and cannot be replicated by an interim appointment; the petition reflects the institutional investor view that the AI ecosystem’s dependence on Jensen-era Nvidia — the neocluster anchor checks, the inference lab partnerships, the government chip access relationships — is a relationship network, not an institutional framework, and that its value depreciation began when Jensen’s resignation was announced; interim CEO Colette Kress, who simultaneously serves as CFO, confirmed Saturday that the board has opened a formal search process, but the shareholders’ petition signals that the market’s preferred resolution is not a new CEO but Huang’s reinstatement — a resolution that the ongoing DOJ investigation into the Shanghai chip smuggling operation complicates.

The shareholders’ petition is not a binding instruction — it is a price signal. It says: the market is discounting Nvidia’s future based on Jensen’s absence, not the interim management’s quality. If the board interprets the petition correctly, the CEO search timeline becomes secondary to the question of whether the investigation’s resolution creates a pathway for Huang’s return.

OpenAI Disbands Standalone Robotics Team Bets General Purpose Models Run Robots Directly Without Dedicated Research Unit Strategic Pivot Sunday November 15 2026 OpenAI Ends Standalone Robotics · General Models Run Robots · Strategic Pivot OpenAI (Sun Nov 15 2026): robotics → disbanded → standalone → team → general → purpose → models → run → robots → directly → strategic → pivot

OpenAI disbanded its standalone robotics team on Sunday, betting that its general-purpose foundation models are capable enough to run robots directly without a dedicated robotics research unit — a strategic signal that the company views model capability as having crossed the threshold where application-specific research teams are redundant; the decision mirrors the broader AI industry debate about whether general-purpose models will subsume specialized AI applications or whether specialized teams remain necessary for physical world deployment, with OpenAI taking the former position; the disbanding comes in the same week that OpenAI posted its first $10 billion quarterly revenue, raised $15 billion at $1.25 trillion, and confirmed it is building toward a general artificial intelligence platform rather than a portfolio of application products; competitors including Figure AI and Physical Intelligence, which raised $400 million last year on the thesis that physical AI requires dedicated research infrastructure, will now compete against OpenAI’s general model in the robotics application space.

The strategic logic is clear: if GPT-5 can run a robot better than a team of robotics researchers building on GPT-4, the team’s marginal contribution is negative. OpenAI is betting that general model capability has crossed that threshold. Physical AI companies are betting it has not. One of them will be proved right in the next robot deployment cycle.

LangChain raised $300 million in a Series C led by CapitalG — Google’s independent growth fund — for its AI agent development and management platform, confirming institutional capital’s continued conviction in AI infrastructure despite the week’s selloff; Fractile raised $720 million in a Series C on the same day for its hardware approach to AI inference, combining stacked memory and logic to address the data movement bottleneck that limits large model efficiency; together, $1.02 billion in AI infrastructure capital was deployed on a single Sunday — the day before a week that includes the Anthropic IPO pricing — a data point that separates the AI market’s public market volatility from its private market conviction; both rounds were led by established growth funds, not speculative early-stage investors, suggesting that the AI infrastructure layer remains insulated from the same selloff that reduced public AI valuations.

The LangChain and Fractile rounds are capital deployed at different layers of the AI stack — software orchestration and silicon inference — on the same day. Neither is coincidental. Both rounds were structured before the week’s selloff and closed in its aftermath, which is the most direct possible signal that private market conviction in AI infrastructure has not followed public market volatility lower.
