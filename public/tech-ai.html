Anthropic’s ANT fell 9.0% to $87.90 on its second day of trading Friday as a broad AI selloff dragged the Nasdaq down 2.25%, dashing hopes that Thursday’s record $81.3 billion IPO would stabilize an AI trade already shaken by export-control tightening and chip-sector pressure — leaving ANT above its $83 IPO price but far below the $111.50 opening pop that defined Thursday’s session; Apple revealed it is developing an autonomous AI agent capable of operating a Mac computer on a user’s behalf — browsing the web, running and debugging code, controlling apps, navigating devices, and calling external services — with internal plans pointing to a possible launch as early as late 2027 and development still at an early stage; AMD reported that agentic AI workloads are fundamentally shifting data center CPU-to-GPU ratios from the traditional 1:4 configuration toward 1:1, as the persistent monitoring, state management, and tool-calling demands of agentic AI require CPU cycles alongside GPU inference in a way that pure LLM deployment did not; and the European Commission confirmed it was targeted by an AI-assisted cyberattack using the publicly available Artex AI penetration-testing framework, temporarily disrupting its HR platform Sysper, its medical reimbursement system MyPMO, and its translation services, while investigators found no evidence of a broader data breach.

Friday’s tech beat delivers its defining question in ANT’s $87.90 price: what is the AI application layer worth on day two of public trading, when the euphoria of a record debut gives way to the sustained interrogation of a $1.5 trillion valuation? The Apple agent announcement and AMD’s infrastructure data suggest the structural case for the application layer is intact. The price discovery is not.

The Anchor · The Day-Two Question — What ANT’s 9% Reversal Tells the AI Sector About the Gap Between IPO Pricing and Daily Price Discovery

Anthropic’s ANT fell 9.0% on its second trading day. It is still above its $83 IPO price. The fall is not about the IPO being wrong. It is about what daily price discovery requires. Thursday’s 34% opening pop was an expression of institutional conviction: investors who had already committed to the IPO at $83 were confirmed right, and the market opened at $111.50 as the size of the institutional bid became visible. Friday’s 9% decline is the daily market’s response to a $1.5 trillion valuation on a company whose revenue, while growing rapidly, does not yet support that multiple at conventional metrics. The gap between $1.5 trillion and provable revenue is where price discovery lives. It lives there every trading day until ANT closes the gap. That is not a bearish statement about Anthropic’s long-run trajectory. It is a description of how equity markets work: they price expectations, and they revise those expectations daily. What Thursday confirmed is that the institutional expectation for Anthropic’s long-run revenue is very high. What Friday confirmed is that the daily market will continue to test whether that expectation is justified. ANT’s performance from here depends on one thing: how quickly Anthropic’s actual revenue trajectory closes the gap between $87.90 and the valuation that $87.90 implies. The Apple agent announcement and AMD’s 1:1 CPU:GPU finding are the structural data points that say the application layer’s revenue case is real. They do not close the gap. They say the gap is worth betting on.

ANT’s 9% second-day decline confirms the pattern that large AI IPOs have established: the opening pop reflects the size of the institutional bid that built up over months; the second day reflects what every other market participant thinks it is worth. Those two prices are rarely the same. That gap is not a verdict on Anthropic’s long-run value. Apple is building an autonomous AI agent capable of operating a Mac computer on a user’s behalf — browsing the web, writing and debugging code, controlling applications, navigating device interfaces, and calling external services — according to Bloomberg and TechCrunch reports citing a careers site job posting that described the system as an “AI agent that can use a computer” with the ability to work across apps and run commands without per-action prompting; internal plans cited in the reports point to a possible launch target as early as late 2027, with development still at an early stage that the job posting’s infrastructure requirements suggest is focused on the core agent execution loop rather than a consumer-facing product; the upcoming macOS 27.3 will also warn users against granting full system access to third-party agents, Apple Insider reported, a defensive measure that positions Apple’s own agent as the governed alternative to external competitors while simultaneously raising the governance bar for the category.

Apple’s Mac AI agent announcement is structurally significant because it completes the roster of major platform companies building persistent autonomous agents: Microsoft unveiled Copilot Autopilot at Ignite Thursday, Google has Gemini-based agent capabilities in development, and Apple is now publicly in the race for the native device agent that operates the user’s computer on their behalf. The macOS 27.3 third-party warning is Apple playing both sides: opening the agent category on its platform while governing who gets to operate there. That is the same playbook Apple has used for the App Store for 18 years.

AMD executives reported that agentic AI workloads are shifting data center CPU-to-GPU ratios from the traditional 1 CPU per 4 GPUs toward 1:1, repositioning the server CPU at the center of AI infrastructure after years in which GPU clusters dominated AI deployment architecture; the shift reflects the distinctive computational demands of agentic AI — persistent monitoring of system state, multi-step tool calling, memory retrieval, and long-horizon planning — which require sustained CPU cycles alongside the burst GPU inference that dominated pure LLM deployment; AxeCompute’s analysis noted that since October 2026, Nvidia, AMD, and Intel have all placed agentic CPU demand at the center of their data center roadmaps, with the networking and memory bottleneck between CPUs and GPUs emerging as the new primary infrastructure constraint for agentic workloads at scale.

AMD’s 1:1 CPU:GPU ratio finding reframes the AI hardware investment thesis at exactly the moment ANT’s IPO has focused attention on the application layer. The GPU supercycle thesis — buy GPUs because AI demand is insatiable — assumed LLM inference as the dominant workload. Agentic AI adds a CPU component that changes the data center economics: not just more GPUs, but a balanced compute architecture where CPU investment grows proportionally. The implication is that the AI infrastructure buildout has a second wave that the pure-GPU thesis underpriced.

The European Commission confirmed it was targeted by an AI-assisted cyberattack using the Artex AI penetration-testing framework — a tool that is publicly available — which temporarily disrupted several internal systems including the HR platform Sysper, the medical reimbursement system MyPMO, and the Commission’s translation services, affecting staff payroll processing and medical claim reimbursements; the attack is part of a broader pattern that Google’s Threat Intelligence Group documented on November 16, when it reported the first observed case of an AI framework autonomously completing a full attack cycle — vulnerability discovery, exploit development, and execution — without human intervention, targeting French media, entertainment, and education websites; investigators found no evidence of a broader data breach affecting Commission staff, Help Net Security reported, with the Commission describing the incident as “a misuse of an AI tool” that exploited the same penetration-testing capabilities designed for defensive security research.

The European Commission attack and Google’s autonomous exploit agent finding arrive in the same week that Arizona’s attorney general opened a criminal investigation into rogue AI agents in banking and that the first-ever government watchdog test of chatbots found all four leading models produced viable terrorist attack plans in under 12 hours. The pattern is not a single incident — it is a category of AI risk that is maturing faster than the governance infrastructure designed to contain it, at the same moment the AI sector has just priced its application layer at $1.5 trillion.
